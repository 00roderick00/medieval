## Objection 1 — Ruggie: "there are none of it"

**Steelman.** §I claims a repeatable mechanism. Ruggie, the discipline's
lexicographer of the medieval-to-modern transformation, considered the
functional theory that "a shift in the advantage to the offense in
weapons technology allegedly drove the desire for larger and fiscally
more capable political formations" and rejected it on two counts: its
retrodictive value is limited (the Italian city-states and the Hansa
were "viable political alternatives to the territorial state, fully
able to levy taxes and raise armies, for the better part of two
centuries. In social life, two centuries is no mere time lag"), and
there is "a substantial logical and empirical gap between the existence
of some functional pressure for political units to grow, and their
blossoming specifically into a system of territorially defined,
territorially fixed, and mutually exclusive state formations" (p. 156 /
PDF 19). His verdict on method reaches the coda directly: "while there
may be law-like generalizations in the medieval-to-modern
transformation, there are none of it" (p. 169 / PDF 32). A coda that
calls its claim a mechanism is claiming what the canon says cannot be
claimed.

**The book's answer.** Three limbs. First, Ruggie attacks a claim the
book does not make: that weapons determined the *form*. The chain ends
at the subordination of intermediaries under a permanent fisc, and spine
§8(g) has ruled the constitutional form open; the second shortcoming is
the book's own position and is conceded under his name. Second, his two
centuries are the two centuries *before* the ratchet (1300–1500); the
mechanism runs 1440–1500 and the book's evidence is the end of exactly
those alternatives (the Peterhof, Venice's limit, Charles VIII's train)
— and Ruggie's own p. 161 concedes the decades. Third, "none of it" is
answered by claiming one link and not the transformation: relationships
among variables, in Tilly's 1975 sentence, not sequences or forms.
**Graded good** on the first two limbs; the residue is honest — the book
has no account of why the *idea* of exclusive sovereignty formed and
should not pretend to one. (Assessment: Ruggie §5A; ch01 and ch12
critiques carry the chapter-level forms.)

## Objection 2 — Krasner: sovereignty was always hypocritical, so there was no lock

**Steelman.** §VI says the first medieval order "became irreversible
within two generations" and rests the book's disagreement with Bull on
that lock. Krasner: "Violations of Westphalian sovereignty have been
almost routine in international politics" (p. 28 / PDF 38); "There has
never been some ideal time during which all, or even most, political
entities conformed with all of the characteristics that have been
associated with sovereignty" (p. 238 / PDF 248); and the hypocrisy runs
back through the book's own period — rulers in "the European feudal
system" likewise expounded principles they violated (pp. 227–28 / PDF
237–38). If the sovereign order never existed as practice, the
neomedievalists diagnosed a change in what analysts notice, not in the
world; there was no lock to spring and nothing for a pendulum to leave.
Worse for §VI's debt: the critic holds that Bull's constitutive rules do
not exist — "The international system is not like the game of chess. It
does not have constitutive rules" (p. 229 / PDF 239).

**The book's answer.** Krasner's grid dissolves the objection. His
violated sovereignty is the exclusion of *external* actors from a
polity's authority structures (Westphalian); the book's lock is the
subordination of *internal* intermediate authorities and the crown's
monopoly of decisive force — domestic sovereignty in the control sense,
which he says "is not necessarily related to international legal or
Westphalian sovereignty" (p. 12 / PDF 22). He brackets the internal
consolidation ("I offer no explanation for the displacement of other
institutional forms", p. 5 / PDF 15) and then concedes it as fact with a
functional cause (p. 68 / PDF 78). **Graded good** provided the lock is
stated as internal subordination and fiscal monopoly, never as
exclusivity — the coda's §VI wording is already in the safe cell; weak
wherever the manuscript says "exclusive" (ch01 §VI, the Introduction —
not the coda). The residue to carry: the consolidated state was, from
1648, routinely penetrated by invitation, so Part III's absorption of
the stack may itself run by invitation — spine §8(g)'s ornamental route
reached from the other side; that is an ally, not a cut.

## Objection 3 — Crouch: the parabola has no return stroke

**Steelman.** §VI describes the neomedieval condition as "a waypoint
with a well-documented exit." Crouch's account of the democratic
polities runs the other way from the book's and has held for
twenty-five years: the firm wins because it can leave and because the
state, envying "the phantom firm its flexibility and apparent
efficiency", dismantles its own competence until "Government becomes a
kind of institutional idiot" (PDF 17); "These changes are so powerful
and widespread that it is impossible to see any major reversal of them"
(PDF 31); the party becomes a firm (PDF 28). On his account the firm
absorbs the state, not the state the stack, and the drift is terminal.
The ornamental route the book now scores is a concession that Crouch
may be right about the form even where the book is right about the
object.

**The book's answer.** The disanalogy of object: Crouch's state
outsources schools and rail; it never has to outsource the cannon, and
his firm's leverage — exit — does not exist for a foundry, a
constellation or a frontier lab whose licence, export controls and
revenue depend on one sovereign. Decisive force cannot be discounted by
"smart market actors" because it is not a market. His own p. 32
sentence concedes the principle — democracy "has depended on the
capacity of politicians to reduce the political power of the military
while at the same time sustaining its effectiveness as a fighting
force". **Graded good as far as it goes; weaker on the twenty-five
years**, which the coda's multi-path concession should carry as the
named alternative (the no-return parabola beside Wu's Cycle, Objection
7). The honest form is the ruling's: the barons' subordination is
defended, the form of the state that subordinates them is open, and
Crouch's condition is the outcome the sub-wager scores against.

## Objection 5 — Chastellain: the forecaster who cites him invites his charge

**Steelman.** §IV invites the reader to hold the book "to the published
version and to none softer." The one fifteenth-century forecaster the
corpus holds mocks a fixed-term prediction that failed (the astrologers'
March 1458 for Charles VII's death — pp. 444–450 / PDF 454–460) and
keeps his own comet-forecast alive by hedging its necessity (p. 117 /
PDF 127) and cashing it on whatever deaths followed (p. 387 / PDF 397).
If the book cites him as the cautionary specimen, it invites the same
charge against Appendix C's indicators the moment one is re-dated.

**The book's answer.** Procedural, not rhetorical: Appendix C's
indicators are dated and directional and are not re-dated after the fact
(CC-15's rule; Tilly 1975 p. 635 as the precedent); the sentence that
cites him says so. **Graded adequate.** Placement of the specimen is
Roderick's (ch12 (uu); coda-i).

## Objection 6 — Scott: the settlement predicted is his four elements assembled

**Steelman.** §V says the book "predicts, and declines to bless." Scott's
whole book argues that the synoptic scheme the book prices — a fully
legible society under a consolidated state — is constitutively brittle
("The more schematic, thin, and simplified the formal order, the less
resilient", p. 351 / PDF 368), that war is the paradigm mētis domain
(p. 315 / PDF 332), and that the polity which most completely executes
the settlement is the polity most exposed to the catastrophic
combination he documents, whose worked example is the Great Leap
Forward. A coda that disclaims desirability while predicting the
recipe's first and third elements is disclaiming too little.

**The book's answer.** Scott concedes the exception the mechanism needs
— "centralized high-modernist solutions can be the most efficient …
airplane manufacturing" (p. 221 / PDF 238): decisive-force production is
a wheat crop, not a raspberry crop; the falsifier is already built
(condition 1 is the Scott scenario); and the Ukraine record shows the
mētis layer aiming *through* the formal stack, mutual parasitism rather
than one-way dependence. **Graded good on the mechanism, exposed on the
settlement's durability** — a Tudor settlement run as personal rule over
a fully legible society is Scott's four elements assembled, and the
book's own China-succession question is where the exposure surfaces.
The register fix is in his words: state the recipe by name; prediction
is not commendation.

## Objection 7 — Wu: the settlement is itself a phase

**Steelman.** "Every consolidated entity may well have only until the
next turn of the Cycle before being scattered, and everything scattered
may await only its eventual imperial visionary" (c18). The book predicts
a durable consolidation inside blocs; Wu predicts it is a phase, and his
AT&T sequel — severed 1984, reconstituted with federal approval by 2005,
rewarded with retroactive immunity in 2008 — is a verified case of the
off-switch migrating back to private hands.

**The book's answer.** The mechanisms differ — Wu's cycle is driven by
disruptive innovation in information industries, the book's
consolidation by the price of decisive force, and its historical run
lasted a century and a half; Appendix C's fifteen-year horizon sits
inside even a Wu-length closed phase, so the wager is scoreable either
way; and Wu allows the Cycle can be arrested by "the combined forces of
a dominant industry and the federal government" (c10). **Graded
adequate** — the coda's honesty gains from conceding the oscillation as
the named alternative to the pendulum's forward swing, and C11-1 must be
able to score a reconstitution as failure (ch12 (oo)).

## Objection 8 — Goldsmith & Wu; Farrell & Newman: half the stack was taken back for free

**Steelman.** §I's core empirical assertion is that "the price of
decisive capability has moved beyond subordinate reach." But the
identity–payments–legibility stack of claim 3 was re-territorialised
between 2000 and 2006 by cheap legal coercion of local intermediaries
("Government regulation works by cost and bother, not by hermetic seal",
G&W p. 68 / PDF 81), and the most consequential coercive capability of
the era accrued to the American state "inadvertently", built by market
actors and inherited by jurisdictional accident (F&N article p. 54 / PDF
13) — no taille, no permanent finance, no fiscal threshold. The
mechanism is superfluous to that half of the wager.

**The book's answer.** Category: their chokepoints are the
canon-and-register layer, which the manuscript already holds cheap
(ch11's fifth move); what cheap coercion cannot do is build the router,
the fab, the model or the constellation — the compulsion to move from
mandating the canon to holding the plant is where the fiscal cost
begins; and the institutions condition (F&N p. 58 / PDF 17) is the
book's administrative-capacity gate in modern IR. **Graded good but
partial**: the concession must be made in text — a layered tense,
accomplished for canon and register, compelled and unaccomplished for
the decisive stack (ch12 (ll); coda-d) — and the fiscal threshold
defended on the military stack, not the coercion channels.

## Objection 9 — McNeill: the stack held the state to ransom for two centuries

**Steelman.** §I's "one completed run" elides the run's internal
timetable: "Their very weakness thus allowed the Liégeois to set their
own prices. Even the mightiest rulers had to pay what was asked, or do
without" (p. 113 / PDF 122); "the sovereignty of the market over even
the greatest ruler of the age remained an ultimate reality" (p. 114 /
PDF 123). The artillery state subordinated its barons and was itself
subordinated by its bankers and gunmakers; if the analogy is exact, the
compute-foundry layer keeps its price long after the private-force
layer has been commissioned.

**The book's answer.** The dependence ran on jurisdictional refuge
("Dozens of other refuges for entrepreneurs were scattered across the
face of Europe", p. 114) and the bloc settlement is the closure of
refuge inside the bloc; McNeill concedes the hybrid strengthened the
states in the long run (p. 114); his long arc ends where the book's
does (pp. 116, 386). **Graded good on direction, exposed on timetable**
— the lag is a confessed limit or a separately scored risk (coda-e;
CC-8; ch11 (jj)).
