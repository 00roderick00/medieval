# Introduction — The Return Swing

## I.

On the morning of 17 July 1453, in the valley of the Dordogne east of
Bordeaux, the most famous soldier in England heard mass — and broke it
off, on the strength of a report that turned out to be false.

John Talbot, earl of Shrewsbury, was about sixty-six years old — not
the eighty of the legend — and he commanded the last English army in
the last English province in France. The report said the French
besieging Castillon were abandoning their fortified camp; a man of his
own nation, who had seen much war, told him to his face that it was
untrue; Talbot struck him and rode. What waited behind the ditch and
palisade along the little river Lidoire was not an army in the sense
Talbot's sixty years had taught him — it was an artillery park,
perhaps two to three hundred guns of all sizes, entrenched, provisioned
and paid, sited by a man who had begun his career as a financial clerk.
The Anglo-Gascon charge went in frontally, banners forward, and was
destroyed in an afternoon; Talbot's horse was brought down by a round
and the old man was killed on the ground; his son died with him. He
had passed through the new order's machinery once already — held
hostage for a surrendered city's compositions, his liberty a clause in
a treaty — and if one reading of the day is right, he charged not in
despair but for the guns themselves, the war's decisive instrument
briefly, falsely, reported loose (a possibility Chapter 6 weighs and
declines to decide). Either way, the field settled the larger
question. Bordeaux capitulated in October; three centuries of English
France ended; and the instrument that ended it was not a general or a
dynasty but a budget line — a fiscal apparatus, two permanent
artillery accounts, a salaried technical service of about a hundred
and fifty men, a tax that no longer paused between wars. The French
had not out-fought the English so much as out-built them. They had
made the one thing the age's whole political order was unequipped to
answer: a state that could pay for artillery.

This book is about that state — call it the *artillery state* — and
about the strong claim that its like is being built again now, around
us, in three or four places at once. The claim needs a book because
it runs through two arguments the reader may hold apart: an argument
about the Middle Ages, and an argument about the 2020s. The first is
that the plural, overlapping, contractual world the medieval sources
show us — the world before exclusive territorial sovereignty — was
ended by an identifiable mechanism, at an identifiable price, in an
identifiable half-century: the capital-intensification of decisive
force, which set the cost of military decision above what any subject
could pay, and thereby compelled the permanent taxation, the standing
institutions and the administrative reach that we retrospectively
call the state. The second is that the same mechanism is operating
again — through foundries, constellations, munitions lines and
hyperscale compute rather than bombards — and that it predicts the
same class of outcome: not a restored global order and not a stable
dispersal, but a set of parallel consolidations, executed inside
civilisational blocs, on the pattern this book calls the *Tudor
turn*. Between the two arguments stands the present condition, which
a serious scholarly literature spent fifty years naming: the new
medievalism — the observed return of overlapping authority and
multiple loyalty, of private force and plural law and universal
institutions that answer to no territory. This book accepts that
literature's diagnosis almost entirely. Its quarrel is with the
literature's tense. The dispersal is real; it is also, on the
evidence assembled here, the middle of an arc whose ending has
happened once before, on the record, with account books.

## II.

Somewhere in the American Southwest this year, a fabrication plant
is rising behind security fencing, part-financed by a statute and —
since the summer of 2025 — part-owned by the state that passed it.
In a laboratory in California, the fastest computer on earth runs
its stated mission: the stewardship of a nuclear deterrent. In low
orbit, a reconnaissance constellation that is explicitly "owned and
controlled by the US government" proliferates by the monthly launch.
The reader may stand at any of these sites — or at their
counterparts outside Shanghai, or at the empty ground where a
European flagship fab was cancelled — and ask the question this book
exists to answer: what are these? The available official answers —
industrial policy, supply-chain resilience, innovation strategy —
are true and shallow. The deeper answer, the book will argue, is
that the price of decisive capability has moved again (the
demonstration is Chapter 10's, and it is made from budget documents,
not metaphors), and polities are responding as polities respond when
that happens: by building the fiscal, administrative and legibility
apparatus that the new price demands, and by beginning — half
consciously, in the American case; deliberately, in the Chinese —
to subordinate every intermediate authority that stands between the
centre and the capability. The scene is left deliberately
underdetermined, because in 1445 it was underdetermined too. Nobody
at Louppy-le-Châtel knew they were founding the modern state. The
ordonnance that did it is lost.

The book's three claims, in the order its parts argue them. First,
the diagnostic claim: the neomedieval literature was right about the
dispersal and wrong about two things — it mistook the platforms for
market actors when they are institutional universalisms, churches in
structure if not in creed; and it mistook the dispersal for a stable
condition when it is a transitional one (Part I). Second, the
mechanistic claim: between 1440 and 1500 the price of decisive
violence changed for identifiable, technology-specific reasons; the
fiscal-military state was the response; and this is a mechanism with
documented components — a procurement memorandum of 1442, a tax made
permanent by practice, two artillery accounts audited by name — not
an analogy (Part II). Third, the predictive claim: the same
mechanism, running now through the compute-foundry-satellite-
munitions stack and the identity-payments-legibility stack, predicts
parallel Tudor settlements inside three or four blocs, with durable
neomedieval disorder persisting in the interstitial spaces (Part
III). The book never argues that the present resembles the Middle
Ages. Resemblance is cheap, and the genre it produces deserves its
reputation. The book argues a causal chain — weapon capital-intensity
to permanent finance to administrative capacity to the suppression
of intermediate authorities — and stakes itself on the chain's being
checkable at every link.

## III.

Checkable means falsifiable, and the wager is stated here, at the
front of the book, in the form its reviewers should quote. The
passage is numbered; each condition names the instrument that scores
it (the indicators, with their baselines and sources, are Appendix
C's).

> The wager is falsifiable, and the reader should hold the book to
> it. If, over the next fifteen years:
>
> **1.** the decisive military layer proliferates — the number of
> polities able independently to field leading-node fabrication,
> proliferated military low-orbit reconnaissance, hyperscale
> sovereign compute, and a sustained peer-war munitions base rises
> materially above four; or a full-stack power is defeated in
> decisive engagement by cheap distributed systems fielded *without*
> a patron's intelligence, connectivity, and interceptor layers
> behind them [C6-1/C10-2; C12-1];
>
> **2.** the legibility infrastructure fragments in fact and not
> only in principle — bloc-internal fiscal and reporting reach
> falls, the untaxed share of offshore wealth rises again, and
> jurisdictionally plural, privacy-default identity and payment
> systems displace the bloc-scale stacks as the operative default
> [C11-2; C8-3];
>
> **3.** the platform universalisms consolidate independence instead
> of being absorbed — an actor combining decisive-relevant
> capability with a majority-commercial fiscal base escapes the
> state's instruments, and, decisively, resolves its disputes with
> states in fora states do not control [C2-3/C3-2; C11-3];
>
> **4.** the Tudor moves stall across all four candidate polities —
> no fiscal absorption of platform surplus, no canonical assertion
> that is enforced and upheld, no absorption of private force, no
> re-territorialisation of critical infrastructure [C11-1; C10-1;
> C4-1] — and, in the one candidate asserting canon without a fisc,
> the canon alone proves sufficient to consolidate a bloc [the
> Depreter test, chs. 10 and 12];
>
> — then the thesis fails, and the neomedievalists were right that
> the dispersal is stable. The book stakes itself on the opposite
> reading.

Two notes on the passage's construction, so the reader knows it was
built and not decorated. Each condition is the *narrowed* form of a
broader condition the research forced tighter: the drone condition
acquired its without-patron-scaffolding clause from the Ukraine
record (Chapter 12); the platform condition acquired its
forum-migration limb from the legal history (Chapters 8 and 11);
the fourth condition's final limb cuts against the book in both
directions by design — a European bloc consolidated by canon alone,
without a fisc, would falsify the mechanism even while producing a
settlement look-alike. And the fifteen-year horizon is kept from
the book's first outline: long enough for the moves to run,
short enough that the author expects to be marked.

## IV.

Three objections deserve answers before the first chapter rather
than after the last, because each, left standing, would spare the
reader the trouble of the book.

The first says: declinism is a genre with a rap sheet. Every
generation finds medieval ruin in its headlines, and the finest
instance is worth quoting at its best. Robert Kaplan, in 1994, saw
in West Africa "the symbol of worldwide demographic, environmental,
and societal stress, in which criminal anarchy emerges as the real
'strategic' danger," drew his "Last Map" — "an ever-mutating
representation of chaos" — and carried an image that has outlived
the essay: "Think of a stretch limo in the potholed streets of New
York City…" — the gated rich inside, the rest of humanity outside
— an image that is, the attribution matters, Thomas Homer-Dixon's,
quoted and adopted by Kaplan [^1-1].
The Coming Anarchy did not come, or came only where it was already
resident; the genre's record is the objection, and this book's
answer is method, not indignation. The book does not argue from
atmosphere; it argues from a measured price shift, in the tradition
of the military-revolution historians — Roberts's "great divide
separating mediaeval society from the modern world," disciplined by
fifty years of criticism into the calibrated versions Chapters 6
and 10 actually use. And it does the one thing the genre never
does: it publishes its own failure conditions, above, with dates.

The second objection is the medievalists', and it has teeth: that
political theory's Middle Ages is a fairyland — in Holsinger's
words, neomedievalism as "the global idiom of the non-state actor,"
and the international-relations variant "a frightening lens on to
the ultimate co-optability of academic theorising into a regressive
and destructive political culture" [^1-2]; Rengger's version asks whether the
new Middle Ages could be anything but "its calamitous predecessor"
[GAP: Rengger's chapter is gated; the fragment is carried via a
named intermediary and his argument is not paraphrased further
until retrieved]. The answer is architectural, and it is this
book's evidentiary signature. The medieval half of the mechanism is
sourced to the specialist historiography *at its most sceptical* —
assembled, at every contested joint, from the sceptics' own
concessions: Rogers, ranking the infantry revolution above the
artillery revolution, supplies the artillery-tax-centralisation
spiral himself; DeVries, the field's anti-determinist, concedes
that France and Burgundy "conform" to the consolidation pattern he
resists; Curry, the gradualist, concedes that siege artillery
"could not be provided by any other organisation than the state…
at the level required"; and Cerny — the neomedieval literature's
own theorist — locates the ending of the first dispersal in "the
creative destruction of fifteenth to seventeenth century warfare."
The disagreements that remain among these scholars are over
ranking, tempo and sequence inside an agreed direction of travel.
A mechanism assembled from its critics' concessions may still be
wrong, but it cannot be fantasy — and where its medieval
components are contested, the book says so in the text, by name.

The third objection says: this is English history wearing a
universal hat. The answer is auditable. Every English-leaning
chapter carries at least two continental comparators — France and
the Ottomans beside Henry VI; France, Italy and the Empire beside
Cade; the Empire and Italy beside the Pastons; France, Burgundy
and the Swiss beside the artillery state, with England demoted to
the control case; France, Spain and the Ottomans beside Henry VII
— and the comparative register is tabulated in Appendix B. Where
the English evidence is merely the best-documented instance of a
pan-European pattern, the book says that too.

## V.

The book runs in three parts, which are three views of one
object. Part I is an engagement with a literature: the neomedieval
diagnosis accepted, tested against the present on its own
checklist, and extended where it stopped — the churches, the
retinues, the contract polities, the hollow crowns. Part II is
late-medieval history: the crisis of the personal constitution,
the year the walls fell, the commons in arms, the plural law, the
broken fiction of the two bodies, and the machine — companies,
taille, artillery service — that answered all of them. Part III
is contemporary strategy: the five moves of the new monarchies
translated into the instruments of the 2020s, the wager, and the
field where the book began. The reader in a hurry may read
Chapters 6, 10 and 11 and hold the spine; the reader with a red
pen should go straight to Appendix C, where the wager is scored.

It remains only to say where the argument starts, and it starts —
deliberately — with its founder's doubt. Hedley Bull, who gave
the new medievalism its name in 1977, weighed the evidence of his
decade and declined his own hypothesis: "it would be going beyond
the evidence to conclude that 'groups other than the state' have
made such inroads on the sovereignty of states that the states
system is now giving way to this alternative" [^1-3]. Bull's caution was correct, and this book's
first chapter begins by saying so: in 1977 the evidence was not
there. The argument of everything that follows is that it is
there now — and that it points, not where Bull's successors
looked, but backward, through the one precedent the record
supplies entire. History does not repeat here; it reprices. The
first chapter opens the books.

# Chapter 1 — The Return of the Middle Ages

## I.

In the autumn of 2024 the Republic of Honduras was being sued for
roughly eleven billion dollars — a sum equal to roughly a third of the
country's annual gross domestic product — by a city on one of its own
islands.

The claimant, Próspera, is a privately governed jurisdiction on
Roatán: a charter enclave with its own regulatory code, its own
dispute-resolution arrangements, its own residency contracts, founded
under a special-economic-zone statute that Honduras had enacted,
entrenched, and then — after a change of government and a
constitutional-court ruling — repealed. The company's answer to the
repeal was not lobbying but litigation, and not in a Honduran court:
the claim proceeds before an international investor–state tribunal,
under treaty protections the republic cannot unilaterally withdraw,
for an amount that would bend the fiscal trajectory of the state
[^2-1]. Set aside the merits, which are contested and not
this book's business. Attend to the structure. A territorial sovereign
and a chartered corporate polity, nested one inside the other, each
claiming lawful authority over the same ground; the dispute removed to
a third forum that neither fully controls; the outcome turning on
which of two overlapping legal orders — the constitution or the treaty
— takes precedence, a question to which there is no agreed answer. A
lawyer of the thirteenth century would need the technology explained
to him. The jurisdictional geometry he would recognise at once.

That recognition is this chapter's subject, and the book's point of
departure. The intuition that our world has come to resemble the
Middle Ages — plural, overlapping, contractual, its loyalties divided
among bodies none of which commands the whole person — is not new,
and this book did not invent it. It has a founding text, a careful
literature, and a name: the new medievalism. The discipline of this
chapter is to take that literature on its own terms — to state its
diagnosis at full strength, run its tests against the present, and
accept what the tests show — before the rest of the book does what
the literature never did. For the neomedieval writers described a
condition. They did not ask, with any persistence, how the *first*
medieval condition ended — by what specific mechanism Europe's
overlapping authorities were, over one long century, hammered into
the exclusive territorial states we mistake for nature. That question
is Part II's, and its answer is the book's. This chapter only earns
the right to ask it: by establishing that the condition is real, that
the diagnosis is sound, and that the thing being diagnosed has
happened before — the substrate first, then the theorists, then
their checklist run against our own decade.

## II.

Begin, as the comparison requires, with the substrate: Europe around
the year 1200, before the settlement this book will call an episode.

The governing fact of that world is the one modern political
instinct finds hardest to hold: authority was real, dense,
law-governed — and nowhere exclusive. Perry Anderson's description
remains the standard one: a "patchwork of overlapping and incomplete
rights of government… inextricably superimposed and tangled," in
which "plural allegiances, asymmetrical suzerainties and anomalous
enclaves abounded" [^2-2]. No one in 1200
was confused by this. A burgher of a free city might owe civic duty
to his commune, spiritual obedience to his bishop, commercial law to
his merchant guild's usages, and ultimate — largely notional —
allegiance to an emperor he would never see; the map of his
obligations was not a jigsaw of coloured territories but a page
written over many times with nothing fully erased. Above the whole
lattice stood two genuine universalisms, each claiming the orbis in
its own register. The papal monarchy, at its apex under Innocent III
(r. 1198–1216), claimed ultimate arbitration in spiritual affairs
and, at the apex's apex, in temporal ones [GAP: the scholarly
statement of the Innocent-III claim awaits Morris, *The Papal
Monarchy*, on the acquisition list]; the Holy Roman Empire claimed a
universal political authority that its own constituent princes —
holders of *Landeshoheit*, a territorial authority approaching but
never equalling sovereignty — contested from inside. Beneath and
between the universalisms, the modal political units of the age
were exactly the entities a Westphalian taxonomy cannot classify:
the free city subject to no one but the emperor; the military
order — Temple, Hospital, the Teutonic Knights with their own
territorial state in the making — supranational, papally
chartered, armed [GAP: scholarly statement awaits Nicholson/Forey,
on the borrow list]; the merchant league negotiating with kings.
(Precision about dating, because this book has a rule about it:
the Hanseatic League crystallised as a named confederation across
the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries — around 1200 it is
nascent, and the northern-Italian communal leagues are the better
strictly-contemporary instance; and the vivid counts of imperial
fragmentation that circulate — the dozens of principalities, the
ninety ecclesiastical territories — describe the Empire of about
1450, not 1200, and appear in this book only where they belong.)

Two features of that order matter for everything that follows,
and they are stated here so Part II can be understood as their
undoing. First, the plurality was not chaos. It was law-dense —
probably more law-dense than our own order, in the sense that a
larger share of daily life was conducted under explicit,
enforceable, written rule: canon law, feudal custom, borough
charter, guild statute, the learned laws reviving in the schools.
What the order lacked was not law but *hierarchy among laws* — a
final answer to the question of which rule prevailed when rules
collided, which is why its politics was a politics of boundary
dispute, privilege, exemption and appeal. Second, the plurality
was durable. It reproduced itself for centuries; contemporaries
did not experience it as transitional; and no internal tendency
was visibly carrying it toward the exclusive territorial state —
a point on which the neomedieval theorists and this book agree,
and on which everything turns, because if the first plural order
was stable in itself, then its ending requires a cause, and the
cause must be found rather than assumed. That is the trailhead of
Part II, marked now, walked later.

One narrowing, conceded before any medievalist demands it. "Europe
c. 1200" flattens a continent of enormous variation — Capetian
France was already consolidating; England after Henry II ran the
most centralised administration in Latin Christendom — and the
comparison this book runs is therefore pinned, deliberately, to
the times and places where plurality was densest, because those
are what the modern condition most resembles. The exemplary case
is northern Italy in the mid-thirteenth century: the expansionist
communes; the Guelph–Ghibelline contest structuring the politics
of every city; and, after Frederick II's death in 1250, the Great
Interregnum — a quarter-century in which the universal political
authority of Latin Christendom was, in effect, vacant, and
nothing collapsed, because the working order had never depended
on its occupancy [GAP: the scholarly anchor awaits Jones, *The
Italian City-State*, on the acquisition list]. A second regional
baseline — the mid-century Rhineland — is the outline's intended
companion case, and it is deliberately *not* asserted here: the
open literature documents the region's later fragmentation, not
its thirteenth-century condition, and the claim waits on the
regional monographs now on the acquisition list. Until they are
read, northern Italy carries the baseline alone, and carries it
adequately.

## III.

The theorists next — in order of their arrival, because the order
is itself instructive: the founder first, doubting his own
hypothesis; the systematiser a generation later, rebuilding it.

Hedley Bull entertained the idea in 1977, in the tenth chapter of
*The Anarchical Society*, as one of several alternatives to the
states system: that "sovereign states might disappear and be
replaced not by a world government but by a modern and secular
equivalent of the kind of universal political organisation that
existed in Western Christendom in the Middle Ages" — an order
whose "central characteristic" would be "a system of overlapping
authority and multiple loyalty" [^2-3]. He named five observable trends as prima facie
evidence: the regional integration of states; the disintegration
of states; the restoration of private international violence;
transnational organisations; and the technological unification of
the world [^2-4]. And then — the honesty
for which this book keeps returning to him — he weighed his own
five trends and declined the conclusion: the evidence for an
emergent new medievalism was, in the mid-1970s, insufficient
[GAP: Bull's verbatim caution sentences await the retrieval of
*Anarchical Society* ch. 10; the paraphrase is Baaz's]. The book's
relation to that caution was fixed in the Introduction and bears
restating in one line: Bull's doubt was correct for his decade.
The five trends were, in 1977, weak signals. Every one of them is
now a headline condition — the regional integrations institutional
(and juridically active, as the gatekeeper designations below will
show), the state disintegrations on the nightly news, the private
violence a documented industry this book's Chapter 3 will price,
the transnational organisations no longer merely lobbying states
but adjudicating against them, the technological unification
carried in four billion pockets. The founder's caution has aged
into the book's licence.

It is worth being precise about what kind of claim that is, because
the book's positioning depends on it. The argument is not that Bull
would now agree — counterfactual endorsements are cheap and
unfalsifiable — but that the evidential threshold he himself set has
been crossed on his own five measures. He asked for regional
integration that genuinely qualified sovereignty: the European Union
now legislates directly into the domestic law of twenty-seven states
and designates the world's largest firms for structural regulation.
He asked for state disintegration beyond the decolonisation wave:
the fragile-state archipelago of Chapter 5 is its standing exhibit.
He asked whether private international violence would return from
the margins: Chapter 3 documents an industry. He asked whether
transnational organisations would acquire authority rather than
merely voice: the arbitral tribunal hearing an eleven-billion-dollar
claim against Honduras is not a pressure group. And the
technological unification he could only extrapolate from telegraphy
and television now runs to a computational layer through which most
of humanity's commerce, memory and speech is transacted. On every
axis, the 1977 reading of insufficiency was right, and a 1977
reading of the 2020s evidence would not be. That is all the chapter
claims, and it is enough.

Jörg Friedrichs supplied, a generation later, the analytical
engine the intuition had lacked, and his article is now in this
book's corpus and cited first-hand. His definition: the medieval —
and neomedieval — order is "a system of overlapping authority and
multiple loyalty, held together by a duality of competing
universalistic claims" [^2-5]. The load-bearing word is
*duality*. What held the medieval lattice together, on this
account, was not an equilibrium of fragments but the standing
competition of two universalisms — Empire and Church, each
claiming the whole world in a different register, neither able to
absorb the other — beneath whose contest the plural lesser
authorities found their room to exist. And the modern translation:
our own order is "held together by the competing universal claims
of the nation-state system and the transnational market economy"
[^2-6] — the territorial universalism
of the states, the non-territorial universalism of the market,
Empire and Church in modern dress. Friedrichs is careful about
the limits of the mapping — the medieval system was "based on
legal and spatial concepts," the neomedieval one on functional
linkages [^2-7] — and the book preserves his
care. But one amendment is entered now, in passing, because Part
I exists to argue it: this book will contend, in the next
chapter, that Friedrichs's second universalism is misdescribed as
a market. The entities that actually wield the non-territorial
claim — the platforms — are not markets but institutions:
rule-making, adjudicating, excommunicating bodies, nearer in
structure to the medieval Church than to any bazaar. The
diagnosis is accepted; the taxonomy will be corrected.

## IV.

Philip Cerny turned the diagnosis into an instrument, and the
instrument can be run. His 1998 article — the founding statement
of "durable disorder," now in this book's corpus at page level —
organises the neomedieval condition into six characteristics, by
the article's own section headings: multiple competing
institutions; the lack of exogenous territorialising pressures;
the uneven consolidation of new spaces, cleavages, conflicts and
inequalities; fragmented identities; contested property rights;
and the spread of *zones grises* — grey zones, the coinage is
Alain Minc's, as Cerny's own note records [^2-8]. Run the checklist against the 2020s, characteristic by
characteristic, with the evidence graded as this book grades
everything.

*Multiple competing institutions.* On 6 September 2023 the
European Commission designated six firms — five of them American,
one Chinese — as "gatekeepers" under the Digital Markets Act,
subjecting the core architecture of their products to a regulatory
code written by a polity in which none of them is domiciled [^2-9]. The same data, meanwhile, may sit
simultaneously under the European GDPR, the American CLOUD Act,
and the Chinese PIPL — three territorial legal orders issuing
partially contradictory commands to one non-territorial entity,
which must navigate among them as a medieval abbey navigated
between bishop and king (the statutes primary; the trilemma
framing T3). Jurisdictional overlap is not a residue at the
system's edges; it is the operating condition of its largest
actors.

*The lack of exogenous territorialising pressure.* Here the
honest report is that the characteristic fits the 2020s *worst* —
and the book wants the reader to stare directly at why. Cerny's
own section names what the absent pressure once was: "the
inter-dynastic struggles of the Hundred Years' War" — the
external, existential, consolidating force that squeezed the
first plural order into states [^2-10]. Since 2022 that
species of pressure has visibly begun returning: a land war on
the continent, rearmament, alliance consolidation, the
re-militarisation of frontiers. Read one way, this falsifies the
checklist's second characteristic. Read correctly, it confirms
the book: Cerny's claim was that *without* exogenous pressure
there is no consolidation — so the pressure's return is not a
defect in the diagnosis but the leading edge of the
re-consolidation this book predicts. The weakest neomedieval
characteristic is weak precisely where the pendulum has started
back. Part II supplies the mechanism; Part III the wager. The
chapter only marks the hinge.

*Uneven consolidation of spaces, cleavages, inequalities.* The
World Inequality Report's baseline figures: the richest tenth of
humanity holds 76 per cent of wealth and takes 52 per cent of
income; the poorest half owns 2 per cent (WIR 2022, primary).
And the unevenness is now jurisdictional as well as material —
the charter enclave that opened this chapter is one of a family
of spaces deliberately consolidated *out* of their host
sovereignties, whose disputes then migrate to fora above both
parties.

*Fragmented identities.* The Pew Research Center's 36-country
studies document sharp declines since 2016 in the share of
publics who regard birthplace, customs or religion as central to
national belonging (Pew 2023–24, primary). What no survey yet
measures cleanly — and the book flags rather than asserts — is
the platform-mediated layer: the loyalties, statuses and
communal memberships administered by the institutions of Chapter
2, for which no quantified 2020s instrument exists. The
qualitative observation stands; the number does not, and is not
invented.

*Contested and entrenched property rights.* By the end of 2023
the cumulative count of treaty-based investor–state arbitrations
reached 1,332 [^2-11] — thirteen hundred instances of
property claims prosecuted against sovereigns in fora the
sovereigns cannot dissolve; Katharina Pistor's account of capital
as legal code supplies the frame. The much-quoted estimate that
intangibles now constitute some ninety per cent of the value of
the leading American equity index is a proprietary methodology
and is cited as an illustrative estimate, no more.

*Zones grises.* The EU Tax Observatory's accounting puts roughly
10 per cent of world GDP in offshore holdings — with the honest
counter-motion stated: automatic information exchange has
measurably *reduced* the untaxed share since 2013 (EU Tax
Observatory 2024, primary). The shadow-economy and
illicit-crypto figures that circulate (a third of global GDP;
tens of billions in flows) are model estimates and lower bounds
respectively, and are flagged as such. The grey zone is real;
its measurements are themselves grey, which is perhaps the most
medieval fact about it.

Six characteristics; five fit the present cleanly on primary
evidence; the sixth fits it badly in exactly the direction the
book's thesis requires. The checklist is the diagnostic
instrument, not the argument — the dispersal it documents is the
condition Part II will explain the first ending of.

## V.

One serious alternative reading of the same phenomena must be
faced before the frame can close, because it was offered by a
scholar of standing and it is the optimist's version of
everything above. Anne-Marie Slaughter's *A New World Order*
(2004, chs. 5–6, in this book's corpus) looked at the same
disaggregation — regulators dealing with foreign regulators,
courts citing foreign courts, the state coming apart into its
functional components — and read it as architecture: not
dispersal but *networked governance*, the emerging constitution
of a manageable world. The book's judgement, stated with the
respect owed a thesis this influential: important, and wrong.
Important, because the networks are real — Slaughter documented
the actual connective tissue of the era this book calls the
dispersal phase. Wrong, because tissue is not skeleton. The
networks she identified coordinate; they do not command,
tax, or compel; and twenty years of subsequent evidence has
shown them doing what this book's frame predicts connective
tissue does when the underlying loads shift — transmitting the
pressures of the states and the platforms, not restraining
them. The disaggregated state was not the destination. It was
the condition on which the next consolidation will operate. The
difference between the two readings is not temperamental but
testable, and the test is the one this book applies everywhere:
follow the capacity. If the networked order were architecture, one
would expect its nodes — the regulatory colleges, the judicial
dialogues, the standard-setting bodies — to have accumulated, over
two decades, independent means: budgets, enforcement instruments,
loyalties that survive the preferences of their member states. They
have not. The consequential decisions of the period — the platform
designations, the export controls, the subsidy programmes Part III
will tabulate — were taken by states and blocs, transmitted
*through* the networks, and resisted by none of them. Slaughter
described the wiring of the dispersal phase with great accuracy;
the book's quarrel is only with the load the wiring was asked to
bear. Chapter 12 returns to her to say precisely what the networks
become inside a bloc-consolidated order — and the answer will not
be nothing.

## VI.

The chapter can now say what it has been building toward, which
is not that the present resembles the Middle Ages — resemblance
is cheap, and this book has forsworn it — but something narrower
and structural: the sovereign territorial state, against which
all this plurality registers as anomaly, is itself the anomaly.
It is a settlement, not a default — a specific institutional
answer, produced in a specific period by specific pressures,
to the plural condition that is Europe's actual long-run norm.
The scholarly ground for this is firmer than the textbook
narrative suggests: Osiander's demolition of the "Westphalian
myth" shows the 1648 treaties concerned overwhelmingly with the
Holy Roman Empire's internal constitution — the sovereign-state
reading is a nineteenth- and twentieth-century retrojection
[^2-12]; Teschke's *Myth of 1648* pushes the emergence
of genuinely modern territorial sovereignty later still
[^2-13]. The contrary position exists and is named:
Latham reads late-medieval and early-modern sovereignty as
variation on a theme rather than difference in kind — on which
account the "anomaly" framing overstates. The book does not
need the strong version. It needs only what both sides of that
dispute concede: that the exclusive territorial state was
*made*, under pressure, out of something else — and what is
made under pressure can be remade when the pressures change.
The state is not the default of European politics; it is an
episode.

Which returns the argument to the question this chapter has
deliberately left standing. The neomedieval literature, taken
whole, is a diagnosis without a history: it describes the
plural condition, dates its return, debates its stability — and
treats the five-hundred-year interruption as background. But
the interruption is the interesting part. Something ended the
first medieval order: some force, operating in identifiable
decades, made the overlapping authorities of 1200 into the
exclusive sovereignties of 1700 — dissolved the universalisms,
disarmed the lords, subordinated the cities, and para-
doxically produced, out of the densest plurality in European
history, the most exclusive form of political authority ever
devised. The literature's founder pointed at the period and
moved on; its systematiser named the pressure in a footnoted
aside. This book proposes to walk in. What follows in Part I
completes the diagnosis — the institutions that now hold the
Church's old position (Chapter 2), the private violence that
now holds the retinue's (Chapter 3), the contract polities
that now hold the condottiere's (Chapter 4), and the hollow
crowns above them all (Chapter 5). Then Part II goes to the
fifteenth century to watch the first dispersal die — not of
old age, and not of ideas, but of something with a price, a
payroll and a date. The reader is asked to hold the question,
because the book's entire wager rests on its answer having
been, once already, a matter of record.

# Chapter 2 — The New Church

## I.

On 8 January 2021, a private company permanently expelled the sitting
President of the United States from the communications medium through
which he governed.

The word for what Twitter did that day exists, but not in the
vocabulary of commercial law. A firm terminated a user account, as its
terms of service permitted; that is the transaction described in the
company's own announcement, and it is true and inadequate. What
happened, functionally, was that a universal authority — self-
constituted, non-territorial, claiming rightful power over a domain of
human conduct — cast out a member, and the member's temporal office,
the most powerful on earth, gave him no appeal. The adequate
vocabulary is older. When Meta's parallel suspension of the same man
came before the company's own review body — the Oversight Board, a
quasi-judicial college constituted by charter, whose decisions on
individual cases bind the company that created it — the Board upheld
the expulsion but struck down its *form*, rejecting an "indeterminate
and standardless" indefinite penalty and requiring a defined one; Meta
returned with a two-year term [^3-1]. An authority that legislates rules of
conduct for billions; that judges breaches through a constituted
appellate body; that punishes with expulsion from communion; and that
publishes reasoned decisions the secular power can read but not
reverse. A canonist would not need the technology explained. He would
ask only one question — *by what right?* — and that question, which no
one in the modern order can answer cleanly, is this chapter's
subject.

Chapter 1 accepted the neomedieval diagnosis and entered one
amendment against its best theorist: Friedrichs's second universalism
— the non-territorial claim competing with the states system — is
misdescribed as a "market." This chapter executes the amendment. The
entities that actually wield the non-territorial claim are not
markets. They are *institutions*: rule-making, adjudicating,
sanctioning, missionary bodies, with doctrines, internal reform
movements, and jurisdictional quarrels — nearer in structure to the
medieval Church than to anything in an economics textbook. The
argument is not that the platforms resemble the Church; resemblance
is banned in this book. The argument is that they occupy the same
*structural position* — a universal authority claiming jurisdiction
over subjects who are simultaneously subjects of territorial powers —
and that the structural position, not the technology, generates the
politics. Two rival universalisms claiming the same subjects is a
problem Latin Christendom worked at for four centuries, through
identifiable crises to identifiable settlements. The crises are
running again. The settlements are therefore worth knowing.

## II.

The prophecy came before the institutions did, and its blind spot is
the chapter's licence.

Stephen Kobrin saw the essential thing in 1998 (his Wharton
manuscript is in this book's corpus): jurisdiction assumes geography
— the whole apparatus of modern law presumes that a transaction
occurs *somewhere*, and that the somewhere selects the sovereign — 
and a digital, networked economy dissolves the assumption. Where
does a cloud transaction occur? Whose law applies to it; who may tax
it? When territory can no longer individuate authority, singular
jurisdiction gives way to overlapping, heteronomous authority: the
medieval condition, returning through fibre. What Kobrin could not
see from 1998 — and what the literature after him never developed —
is *which institutions would fill the void*. He reasoned, plausibly,
toward re-centralisation in some supranational authority with powers
of taxation and enforcement. What actually filled the void was
nothing supranational and nothing neutral: a handful of specific
institutional actors, each with a universalist claim of its own —
and their emergence converts Kobrin's jurisdictional vacuum into a
contest the medieval sources know intimately: not law against
lawlessness, but *authority against authority*.

Three templates from the first contest govern this chapter, each a
settlement to the same structural problem — bishops who were
simultaneously spiritual officers of a universal Church and
temporal vassals of territorial lords; subjects, in other words,
claimed whole by two masters.

The first is the Investiture Contest, and it is the deep template.
The question it litigated — *who invests an officeholder with
legitimacy* — sounds procedural and is constitutional: whoever
confers the office defines the institution. Gregory VII's *Dictatus
Papae* (1075) asserted the monarchic answer — a papacy that may
depose emperors and is judged by no one; Henry IV's winter penance
at Canossa (1077) measured the claim's reach; and the Concordat of
Worms (1122) ended the open war by *partitioning the symbols*: the
emperor renounced investiture with ring and crozier, the spiritual
instruments, and retained investiture with the sceptre, the
temporal ones [GAP: the interpretive framing — Worms as structural
stalemate, the Dictatus as monarchic sovereignty — awaits Ullmann,
the chapter's scholarly spine, on the acquisition list]. Note what
Worms did not do: it did not decide which universalism was
ultimately superior. It stabilised the question by splitting the
regalia — and the question, unresolved, resurfaced for four
centuries. Worms is what a *truce* between universalisms looks
like. This book will need the distinction between a truce and a
settlement before the chapter ends.

The second template is Avignon (1309–1377): the universal authority
perceived as captured by a particular polity. The perception is the
datum — Petrarch's "Babylonian Captivity" was polemic, and modern
scholarship complicates the puppet reading considerably; this book
presents the capture as contemporary perception, not established
fact. But perception was constitutionally sufficient: a universal
authority seen to sit in one king's pocket loses the impartiality
that is its jurisdiction's premise, and the other territorial
powers respond accordingly. The third template is what their
response, compounded, produced: the Great Schism (1378–1417), two
and then — after the Council of Pisa's intervention in 1409 —
*three* simultaneous claimants to the universal office, each with
named territorial backers: the Roman obedience holding Italy, the
Empire, England and Flanders; the Avignonese holding France,
Naples, Castile, Aragon and Scotland; the Pisan drawing support
from both. Christendom did not divide over doctrine — the three
popes taught the same faith. It divided over *which instance of
the universal authority was legitimate*, and it divided along the
lines of the territorial powers' interests. The resolution came
through the era's great constitutional experiment — conciliarism,
the attempt to set a representative council above the universal
monarch — which deposed and outlasted the claimants at Constance
and elected Martin V in November 1417 [GAP: Haec Sancta and
conciliar theory await Tierney or Ullmann]. Hold the Schism's
structure: three universalisms, mutually incompatible, each backed
by a bloc. It is the load-bearing analogue of everything below.

## III.

Now the modern churches, functionally described — canon law,
sanction regime, jurisdictional claim — from their own primary
documents. There are three, and the enumeration is the chapter's
central move.

The first is the hyperscaler church: the American platform
complex — Google, Meta, Apple, Amazon, Microsoft, and now the
frontier AI laboratories — holding universal epistemic authority
over discovery, memory, identity and, with foundation models,
something adjacent to cognition. Its canon law is written and
public: terms of service, community standards, developer
guidelines — legislative instruments governing the conduct of
billions, amended by procedures the legislature alone controls.
Its judiciary exists in embryo and heard its Trump appeal above.
Its sanction regime's documented instrument is the one this
chapter opened with: expulsion from communion — deplatforming as
excommunication is a structural identity, not a flourish, because
in both cases a universal authority withdraws the membership
through which a whole domain of social existence is transacted.
(The extensions the outline gestures at — demonetisation as
interdict, algorithmic demotion as penance — are [ANALOGY-ONLY]:
suggestive, and carried in this book as illustration, because no
primary instrument documents them as constituted sanctions. The
discipline matters; the case is strong enough without them.)

The second is the Chinese state-platform church, and its defining
property is fusion: the universal claim is not rival to the state
but *absorbed into it* — which makes China, in this chapter's
terms, not a church-state contest but its pre-emptive resolution.
The demonstration was the crackdown of 2020–21, best read — the
structural licence holds — as intra-church discipline: a reform
movement imposed on over-mighty religious orders by the authority
that claims them. In sequence: Ant's record-setting initial
public offering suspended two days before listing (3 November
2020; the "largest IPO in history" figure was a pre-suspension
projection, and is cited as such); Alibaba fined RMB 18.228
billion — four per cent of its domestic sales (10 April 2021);
DiDi ordered out of the app stores days after its New York
listing, delisted within the year, and fined RMB 8.026 billion
(21 July 2022); the gaming and private-tutoring sectors
restructured by administrative fiat in a single summer; the whole
programme doctrinally framed by Xi's "Common Prosperity" essay
[^3-2]. Call it the Gregorian moment
[ANALOGY-ONLY in its label; structural in its content]: as
Gregory VII asserted that the universal institution's officers
held their offices *of* the centre and not of themselves, the
party-state asserted — with fines, delistings and disappeared
founders — that the platforms' authority was delegated, not
owned. And the fused church claims its diaspora: the Personal
Information Protection Law's Article 3 (effective 1 November
2021) reaches data processing conducted *outside* China wherever
it targets persons within it — the extraterritorial reflex of
every universalism in this chapter. (Citation discipline: the
underlying Cybersecurity Law was amended in October 2025,
effective January 2026; this book's claims about the original
regime cite the 2016 text and say so.)

The third church holds no servers worth the name and is the most
jurisdictionally aggressive of the three. The European Union is
not a platform; it is a *canonical* authority — a body whose
power consists almost entirely in doctrine and its enforcement,
asserting jurisdiction over the other two churches' conduct
toward subjects on its territory and, through market-access
conditionality, far beyond it. Its apparatus is the most
systematic legal construction of the digital age, all of it
primary on EUR-Lex: the GDPR (2016/679, applicable May 2018,
extraterritorial by its Article 3); the Digital Markets Act
(2022/1925), under which six gatekeepers — five American, one
Chinese — were designated on 6 September 2023; the Digital
Services Act (2022/2065); the AI Act (2024/1689, in force August
2024, reaching any system whose output is used in the Union).
And the doctrine now has documented sanctions: the first DMA
non-compliance decisions — Apple €500 million, Meta €200 million
(23 April 2025) — and the first DSA fine, €120 million against X
(5 December 2025). The Brussels effect, in Bradford's phrase, is
the mechanism: a church with no divisions whose writ runs
through every actor's need for access to its four hundred and
fifty million communicants. Whether canonical authority without
material capability can survive the consolidation era is one of
Part III's sharpest questions, and this chapter only files it.

## IV.

Set the three beside each other and the shape of the present
becomes legible, and the chapter can make its central move in one
paragraph. The condition of the 2020s is not the High-Medieval
papal monarchy — no single universal authority stands over the
territorial powers. It is the *Schism*: three universalisms,
mutually incompatible — an American church of private
institutions under increasingly assertive state discipline; a
Chinese church fused with its state; a European church that is
all canon and no infrastructure — each backed by a civilisational
bloc, each claiming the same class of subjects, none able to
absorb the others. Call the condition *three-body sovereignty*:
like its celestial namesake, a configuration with no closed-form
solution and no stable equilibrium, only trajectories. The map of
the world this chapter has drawn is not drawn in borders but in
obediences — whose app store, whose data regime, whose model
weights, whose adequacy decision — and most of the world's
polities, like the middle kingdoms of the 1380s, must choose an
obedience without the luxury of neutrality. The framing earns its
place in the book because of what it implies for Part III:
schisms do not resolve globally. Each obedience consolidated
*internally* while the schism ran; the resolution, when it came,
was imposed within and between blocs, not above them. The book's
prediction of parallel Tudor settlements inside three or four
blocs — rather than one world settlement — is the Schism's
geometry, projected.

## V.

The investiture contests are running now, and the chapter's
demonstration is that the live disputes of the 2020s, read
structurally, are fights over exactly the Gregorian question:
who invests an actor with legitimacy or permission.

*Who invests app publishers.* The Epic–Apple litigation is a
decade-long investiture fight over whether a platform sovereign
alone confers the right to distribute software to its
communicants and on what tribute terms. The record, all in this
book's corpus from the court documents: the 2021 Rule 52 order —
"the Court cannot ultimately conclude that Apple is a monopolist…
Success is not illegal," but the anti-steering provisions "hide
critical information from consumers and illegally stifle consumer
choice" [^3-3]; the
Ninth Circuit's 2023 affirmance [^3-4], rejecting Apple's
stay arguments as falling "far short of establishing legal
error"; Apple's 27-per-cent workaround; the 30 April 2025
contempt order with its criminal referral; the Ninth Circuit's
filed opinion of 11 December 2025 affirming the contempt findings
by "clear and convincing evidence"; certiorari granted, 2026,
pending. Meanwhile the European church simply legislated the
question: under the DMA, iOS 17.4 (March 2024) opened alternative
marketplaces on the church's territory. One universalism
litigates its investiture powers at home and has them regulated
abroad — Worms in real time, the symbols partitioned
jurisdiction by jurisdiction.

*Who invests AI development with permission.* The chip-control
regime is investiture at the level of physics: the American
state asserting that the capability to train frontier systems is
conferred — or withheld — by licence. The instrument is anchored
in this book to the rule text, not the press: the Bureau of
Industry and Security's 7 October 2022 rule, whose ECCN 3A090
captures integrated circuits meeting a *conjunction* — aggregate
bidirectional transfer rate of 600 gigabytes per second or more
*and* bit-length-times-performance of 4,800 TOPS-bits or more —
with NVIDIA's own Form 8-K of 26 August 2022 naming the A100,
the forthcoming H100 and their systems as captured [^3-5]; tightened October 2023 (the performance-density
threshold closing the A800/H800 workaround); expanded December
2024 (high-bandwidth memory; 140 entities). The regime has been
in flux through 2025–26 and every claim about it in this book is
dated. What is not in flux is the structure: a territorial power
claiming to invest, deny and excommunicate at the level of the
transistor — against the fused church, which answers with
self-sufficiency drives and its own export doctrine.

*Who has jurisdiction over the subjects' data.* Schrems II [^3-6] — the European church annulling the
principal instrument of transatlantic data transfer; the €1.2
billion GDPR penalty against Meta [^3-7]; the 2023 adequacy decision resting on an American
executive order — a concordat, negotiated, provisional, and
challengeable before the same court that destroyed its
predecessor. *And who may release the sacred texts at all.* The
frontier laboratories asserted, unprompted, an independent
authority over their own instruments: OpenAI's staged release of
GPT-2 [^3-8] was the origin case — a private body
claiming, and exercising, discretion over whether a capability
enters the world; the White House voluntary commitments of July
and September 2023 then folded fifteen labs' release-gating
into a quasi-concordat with the territorial power. Each dispute
in this section is a fifteenth-century argument in
twenty-first-century clothes — not because the actors imitate
the past, but because the structural problem admits only so
many arguments, and they have all been had before.

## VI.

Which returns the chapter to the difference between a truce and
a settlement, and to the foreshadow Part III will redeem.

Worms partitioned symbols and left the sovereignty question
standing; the Schism was resolved by council and promptly
re-litigated for a century. The genuinely decisive settlement of
the church-state problem in this book's period was neither: it
was Henry VIII's, and its method was not adjudication but
*absorption*. The Act in Restraint of Appeals (1533) declared
"this realm of England is an empire" — no appeal ran outward, to
any universal authority; the Act of Supremacy (1534) made the
crown "the only Supreme Head… of the Church of England" (both
primary). The universalism was not defeated, disestablished or
out-argued. Its functions — its courts, its revenues, its
legitimating apparatus — were taken into the state, which
thereafter performed them. The decisive settlements did not
defeat the universal authority; they annexed it. The reader is
asked to hold that sentence against Part III's argument about
what the consolidating blocs will do with their churches —
because the pattern of the 2020s already leans Henrician: the
American state converting subsidy into equity in its
foundational infrastructure, the Chinese state ruling its
platforms as departments, the European state legislating its
church's canon itself. The wager's precise form comes later.
What Part I still owes the reader is the rest of the diagnosis
— because beneath the churches' quarrels, the dispersal has
armed actors and contract polities of its own. The retinues
are the next chapter's business.

# Chapter 3 — Bastard Feudalism, Inc.

## I.

Every year, in the risk-factor section of its annual report to
shareholders, Booz Allen Hamilton — a firm of some thirty thousand
employees, most of them holding government security clearances —
discloses a fact it is legally obliged to characterise as a danger:
98 per cent of its revenue comes from a single customer [^4-1]. The customer is the United States
government. CACI makes the equivalent disclosure at 95.7 per cent;
Leidos at roughly 87 [^4-2].
These are not obscure firms; they are among the principal working
parts of American intelligence, defence analysis and military
logistics. And the disclosure describes, in the compulsory candour of
securities law, a relationship that the fifteenth century would have
had no difficulty classifying: a body of armed and cleared men,
maintained in being by a great paymaster, wearing his livery in the
form of badges that open his buildings, bound to him not by tenure or
inheritance but by renewable written contract. The fifteenth century
had a name for this arrangement, and the name was an accusation
before it was a category. The Victorians, who coined it, called it
bastard feudalism.

This chapter extends the neomedieval diagnosis into the domain where
it has been argued longest and understood most narrowly: the private
provision of state violence and its supporting functions. The
narrowness is McFate's inheritance — the modern literature, following
his anatomy of the mercenary industry, fixed on the gunmen; and the
medieval half of the comparison has been told, when told at all, as
an English story about liveried retainers. Both framings are too
small. The medieval original was pan-European — English indenture,
French free company, Burgundian ceremonial clientage, German
declared feud: four legal technologies for the same arrangement —
and the modern phenomenon runs far beyond the gunmen, through
intelligence analysis, border custody, logistics, offensive cyber
and, most consequentially, the emerging market in AI-mediated
decision support. The chapter's thesis is a composite, and this book
marks it as its own synthesis rather than anyone's consensus: where
the fiscal-military capacity of central authority weakens, the great
subjects raise private forces to perform what were once state
functions, financed by some mixture of their own rents, central
remittance and self-provisioning through violence. The components
are separately owned — Tilly's protection-market frame, Kaminsky's
feud-as-legal-institution, McFarlane's affinity [GAP: McFarlane's
1945 article and Hicks await retrieval] — the assembly is the
book's, and the chapter will earn it case by case before running it
forward.

## II.

England first, because England documented itself best — not because
the pattern was English.

The fifteenth-century retaining bond was, in the standard
formulation, "not tenurial but financial, not hereditary but
personal": a gentleman contracted by written indenture to serve his
lord armed and mounted, in peace and war, for an annual fee and the
lord's livery. Charles Plummer coined "bastard feudalism" for the
arrangement in 1885, and coined it as a slur — feudalism's
degenerate cash-nexus successor; McFarlane's redefinition, which
governs the modern field, inverted the judgement: payment-for-
service was the system's essence, not its corruption; the operative
political unit it produced was the *affinity* — the lord's retained
connection of gentry, lawyers, and administrators, the private
capability Chapter 5 will watch encounter an undermighty king; and the
disorder associated with it traced not to the institution but to
inadequate kingship [GAP: McFarlane's exact formulations await the
BIHR article and Hicks]. The statutory record measures the crown's
long unease: livery restricted to household men in 1390, tightened
in 1429, extended to indentured retaining in 1468, and — the
sequence's destination, and this book's foreshadow — Henry VII's
act of 1504 requiring a *royal licence* to retain: private force
not abolished but licensed, the monopoly asserted at the level of
paperwork [GAP: enforcement record awaits McKelvie]. Hold the 1504
move; the modern half of this chapter ends inside its exact
structure.

France ran the arrangement without the politeness of indentures.
The écorcheurs — "flayers," the name a professional description —
were the mercenary companies left unemployed when the Congress of
Arras (1435) closed the Anglo-Burgundian front: bands under
captains of experience and standing, extracting their maintenance
from the countryside they nominally protected, for roughly a
decade. The crown's response sequence is the book's mechanism in
miniature and is owned by later chapters — export (the Dauphin led
the bands to the Upper Rhine in 1444–45, the truce-era problem
made someone else's), then absorption (the best companies taken
into the standing army of 1445), then destruction of the rest.
One detail from the absorption belongs here, because it completes
the class portrait this chapter is drawing: Contamine's analysis
of the 1445 selection found "aucun bâtard, un seul roturier" —
not one bastard, a single commoner — among the retained captains
[TRANS. CLAUDE]; the crown hired the legitimate-born of the
middling nobility and expelled the rest [^4-3].
The domestication of the market for force was also a class
settlement: the state, when it finally bought the industry,
bought only the respectable end of it.

Burgundy dressed the same clientage in ceremony. The Valois dukes
— constructing, across four generations, the composite state
whose fall Chapter 10 will narrate — bound the high
nobility of their scattered lands to the ducal person through the
most prestigious honour system in Europe: the Order of the Golden
Fleece, founded by Philip the Good at Bruges in 1430, a chivalric
confraternity whose membership was, functionally, a retainer list
at the highest social altitude. The mechanism is asserted here;
the numbers — court size, pension flows — are gated and not
invented [GAP: Vaughan, *Philip the Good*]. And Germany supplies
the juridically strangest variant, through the chapter's best
open scholarly anchor: the noble feud — the *Fehde* — was not
lawlessness but a *legal institution*, "in principle limited to
the holders of lordship, limited to certain kinds of hostile
action… and limited in duration," grounded in "the basic right of
every noble to use force to pursue his rights" [^4-4].
Private war as a procedure, with declaratory formalities and
customary bounds; the Empire's repeated *Landfrieden* ordinances
(Mainz, 1235) attempted restriction, and the Perpetual Peace of
7 August 1495 — the *Ewiger Landfriede* — banned the feud
outright and erected a supreme court, the Reichskammergericht,
to absorb the disputes the feud had processed. It did not work
quickly: feuding persisted deep into the sixteenth century — a
Grumbach was executed as a peace-breaker as late as 1567 [GAP:
Zmora for the post-1495 statistics]. The lesson the German case
adds is about tempo — a legal ban on private force, issued by a
weak centre, took three generations to become fact — and it is
a lesson Part III will need.

Italy — the arrangement's most developed form, the *condotta*,
and its one operator who crossed from contractor to sovereign —
is held for the next chapter, by design. Across the four cases
here, the constant is the instrument: the great subject retains
private force through a *legal form* the centre tolerates,
licenses or cannot suppress — indenture, company contract,
chivalric order, declared feud. Four constitutions, one
arrangement. The pattern is pan-European, and its recurrence
wherever the centre's capacity lapsed is the first half of what
this chapter came to show.

## III.

The second half is the present, and the present is bigger than
its reputation.

McFate's thesis frames it: the centuries-old taboo against
mercenarism has eroded; private international violence — Bull's
third trend — has returned as an industry; and the United States,
as the industry's monopsony consumer-in-chief, built the market
it now inhabits. The scale, from the government's own ledgers: US
federal contract obligations ran to some $755 billion in fiscal
2024, $445 billion of it — 59 per cent — Department of Defense
[^4-5]; the Congressional Research Service counts roughly
369,000 contractor full-time-equivalents in the department's
reportable service portfolios, an undercount that excludes
classified components; and across the two decades of Iraq and
Afghanistan, contractors constituted "50% or more of the total
DOD presence in country" [^4-6] — a one-to-one ratio of
contractor to soldier, sustained for a generation, with some
21,000 still in Central Command's area in mid-fiscal 2024 [^4-7]. The intelligence limb is quantified more
carefully than its legend: the famous claim that 70 per cent of
the intelligence budget flows to contractors is a leaked 2007
internal estimate, disputed by ODNI at the time as anecdotal,
and it appears in this book only as that — a disputed leak. The
official figures carry the load and are striking enough:
contractors were 27 per cent of the intelligence-community
workforce in fiscal 2007 (ODNI), and of the population holding
Top Secret eligibility *in access*, contractors approach
45 per cent (ODNI clearance report, FY2019). Beyond the gunmen
and the analysts: the United Kingdom's asylum accommodation —
custody of persons, the state function at its most elemental —
runs through three private contracts whose valuation the
National Audit Office revised from £4.5 billion to £15.3
billion [^4-8]; the statutory Brook House Inquiry
(2023) made credible findings of mistreatment in a G4S-run
removal centre; army logistics run through LOGCAP V's four
indefinite-delivery contracts under an $82 billion ceiling
(US Army, 2019); and the offensive-cyber market's documented
case — NSO Group — displays the licensing structure exactly:
Pegasus is classified as a defence export, saleable only under
Israeli Ministry of Defence licence, the leash worn by the
vendor (the further reading — the licence as an instrument of
foreign-policy leverage — rests on investigative reporting and
is flagged as contested). The newest tier is the one this book
watches hardest: AI-mediated decision support to states —
Palantir's Maven Smart System award of $480 million (May 2024),
its ceiling raised toward $1.3 billion within the year; and on
14 July 2025 the Pentagon's digital-AI office issued agreements
of up to $200 million each to Anthropic, Google, OpenAI and xAI
for "agentic AI workflows" — roughly $800 million of frontier
cognition, contracted deliberately outside the standard federal
acquisition regulation [GAP: pull the defense.gov primaries for
Maven and the CDAO awards before print].

Phil Williams's pessimist counter-read is integrated rather
than resisted: where the state has weakened furthest, he
argued in 2008, what emerges is not durable disorder but
something darker — and his zones are the ones Chapter 5 mapped,
where the contractor economy is not a symptom of governance but
its operative form. (Chronology stated: Williams wrote before
McFate; the contrast is the book's staging, not a debate they
conducted.) The two readings sum to this chapter's: the
contractor economy is the dispersal phase's characteristic
security institution — its affinity, its company, its feud —
scaled to a federal budget.

## IV.

Now the two mechanisms, because the comparison earns its keep
only if it is specified — and its one great disanalogy stated.

The mechanism of retention. The indenture was a legal fiction
of a particular kind: technically suspect, universally
practised, gesturing at the crown's authority while operating
independently of it. The modern instruments are neither
suspect nor fictional, and their solidity is the point: the
FAR contract as the uniform legal form; the security clearance
as the personal bond — 4,243,937 persons eligible as of
October 2019, contractors over a million of them (ODNI) — a
qualification that attaches to the individual, travels with
him between employers, and functions, precisely, as livery
worn invisibly; and ITAR, which controls not only goods but
"defense services" performed by *persons*, so that the licence
travels with the knowledge-holder himself. The structure of
Henry VII's 1504 statute — private force lawful only under
royal licence — is not merely echoed here; it is the actual
regulatory architecture, elaborated. (The "modern indenture"
is this book's framing of documented instruments, and is
marked as such.)

The mechanism of finance — and the disanalogy this book has
promised since its Introduction and now states in full. Livery
and maintenance ran on a variable mix: the lord's own rents,
central remittance when the crown hired the affinity for war,
and plunder when it self-provisioned. The modern contractor
economy runs almost wholly on the middle term. That is what
the 10-K disclosures with which this chapter opened actually
establish: 98 per cent, 95.7 per cent, 87 per cent — the
firms themselves certify, as a formal risk, that they have no
fiscal existence independent of the state. The modern
condition is therefore *less* neomedieval than the fifteenth
century in the decisive respect. The fifteenth-century
magnate could sustain his retinue against the crown, because
his revenues were his own; the modern contractor cannot
sustain itself against its paymaster for a fiscal quarter.
The retinue was never abolished; it was incorporated — and
an incorporated retinue can, in principle, be re-domesticated
by the paymaster whenever the paymaster resolves to pay only
on its own terms. That re-domestication is Chapter 11's
settlement material. The reason it is possible at all is this
disanalogy, and the book keeps the concession bright because
its honesty is load-bearing: whoever wants to argue that the
modern dispersal is *more* dangerous than the medieval one
must first explain away the fact that the armed subjects of
our era file their dependence with the SEC.

## V.

Except — and the chapter closes on the exception, because the
exception is where the next chapter and the book's wager
live — one class of actor has begun to invert the pattern.

The frontier AI laboratories are contractors in form: they
hold defence agreements, their systems enter the
decision-support tier documented above, their names sit beside
Palantir's in the Pentagon's award digests. But their finance
runs the other way. The state is a marginal customer, not the
paymaster: the flagship defence ceiling of $200 million stands
against commercial revenue reported — and this book flags
every such figure as a reported, unaudited estimate, by outlet
and date — in the tens of billions annualised [^4-9]; against which the largest conventional private security
contractor is a rounding error. The caveat is stated with the
same discipline: the labs burn capital at historic rates, and
a fiscal base that rests on continuous fundraising is not yet
an independent fisc — rents, not yet lands. And the live test
arrived in the spring of 2026, and is drafted here as dated,
reported events under this chapter's re-check flag
[RE-CHECK AT PRESS — the episode rested on single-outlet
reporting at research date, with litigation ongoing;
re-verification was not possible in-session]: per CNBC's
reporting of March–May 2026, a frontier lab — Anthropic —
refused the Pentagon's contract terms; the department
responded with a "supply chain risk" designation, the state's
coercive procurement instrument; the dispute entered
litigation; and the firm raised $65 billion *while
blacklisted* — a contractor-adjacent actor surviving the
paymaster's displeasure on its commercial base, which no firm
in the 98-per-cent class could contemplate. Whatever the
episode's legal outcome, its structural meaning is the
chapter's closing question sharpened to a point. The medieval
market for force produced, once, a contractor who converted
independent finance into sovereignty — a captain who ended as
a duke, in the one case the entire condottiere system's
paymasters had organised themselves to prevent. What the
modern condition looks like when a contractor develops
independent finance is therefore not a hypothetical; it is a
precedent with a name. His name was Francesco Sforza, and he
is the next chapter's business.

# Chapter 4 — The Condottieri

## I.

High on the north wall of Florence's cathedral, where the city put its
saints, rides a mercenary.

The fresco is Paolo Uccello's, commissioned by the city in 1436: an
equestrian portrait, painted to imitate a bronze that was never cast,
of Sir John Hawkwood — *Giovanni Acuto*, as Tuscany domesticated the
name — an Essex tanner's son who had sold organised violence in Italy
for thirty years and finished as Florence's commander-in-chief. The
commune had voted him a marble monument while he was still alive to
enjoy the prospect of it, in August 1393; gave him a state funeral the
following spring; and forty years later fixed his image in the Duomo,
between the frescoed saints and the relics, where it remains. Nothing
in the composition apologises. The city that hired him is the city
that honours him, in the building where it keeps its conscience — the
contractor absorbed, celebrated, and framed. This chapter is about
the system that made such a picture natural: the most developed
market for force the West has produced, the legal instrument that
organised it, the one operator who converted a company into a
sovereignty, and the modern actors now running each of those arcs
again. It closes Part I, because with it the diagnosis is complete —
and the fresco states the chapter's finding in advance: the
dispersal's most dangerous actors did not, in the main, devour their
employers. They were domesticated by them — where the employers
could pay.

## II.

Hawkwood's career is the system's origin story, and it begins, like
so much in this book, with a peace.

The Treaty of Brétigny (1360) suspended the Anglo-French war and
demobilised, at a stroke, the professional violence of two kingdoms
— Chapter 3's écorcheur problem, a generation early. The unemployed
organised. The White Company crossed into Italy in 1361 — roughly
three and a half thousand cavalry and two thousand infantry,
organised in the three-man lances of the French war — and Hawkwood,
a veteran of Crécy's generation, was its captain within two years.
What the company sold was the Hundred Years War's tactical
revolution, retailed: dismounted men-at-arms in disciplined bodies,
longbowmen on the flanks — an import Italian warfare had not met.
His employers, in sequence, map the market: Pisa against Florence;
the Visconti of Milan, who sealed the connection with a marriage
(Donnina, Bernabò's natural daughter); Pope Gregory XI, in whose
service the company's hands were bloodied at the massacre of
Cesena in 1377 — a stain this book records rather than launders;
and finally Florence, whose de facto commander-in-chief he was
from 1377 until his death in March 1394 [GAP: the 1380 lifetime
condotta is Caferro's reconstruction, gated]. His masterpiece,
the fighting retreat and counterstroke at Castagnaro (11 March
1387), belongs to the tactical histories. What belongs to this
book is the trajectory's shape: four decades in the market, and
the terminus not a usurpation but a pension, a funeral, and a
fresco. The system that could produce that ending is the
chapter's real subject.

The instrument at the system's heart gave the profession its name:
the *condotta*, the written contract — duration, pay, force
composition in lances — under which a captain conducted his
company into a state's service. (Its internal anatomy — the firm-
service and retainer structures, the pay mechanics — is Mallett's
territory and awaits retrieval; this book carries the outline and
not the detail [GAP: Mallett; Mallett & Hale].) The buyers were
the peninsula's fiscally capable powers — Milan, Venice, Florence,
the Papacy, Naples — and the market's structure is the chapter's
first analytical point: Italy domesticated its mercenaries not by
royal monopoly, as France would (Chapter 10's story), but by
*market competition among solvent buyers*. Across the fifteenth
century the contracts lengthened; commands became permanent;
captains were integrated by marriage, land and honours; and the
states began hiring men-at-arms as individuals — the *lanze
spezzate*, "broken lances," under state-appointed commanders — a
quiet step from renting companies toward owning soldiers. Venice,
the most institutionally careful buyer, supplies the pair of
exemplary cases that bracket the employment relation. Carmagnola,
the celebrated captain suspected of a double game, was summoned
to the city on a pretext, tried in secret, and beheaded between
the Piazzetta's columns on 5 May 1432. Colleoni, the loyal one,
was made captain-general for life (1455), endowed with estates
and a castle, and stands now in Verrocchio's bronze — the statue
Venice gave him being precisely the monument Florence had only
painted. Execution for the suspect; tenure and bronze for the
loyal: a market disciplining its contractors with instruments a
modern procurement office would recognise, scaled to the
fifteenth century's sanctions.

## III.

Once — exactly once — the system produced the outcome its buyers
organised everything to prevent, and the case rewards precision,
because every condition that enabled it can be named.

Francesco Sforza was born into the industry: his father, the
condottiere Muzio Attendolo, drowned crossing a river in 1424,
and Francesco inherited the company as another man inherits a
firm. He served the Papacy, Milan, Venice and Naples — the full
market — and made the market's ultimate marriage: in October
1441, Bianca Maria Visconti, illegitimate daughter of Duke
Filippo Maria of Milan, with Cremonese territories as her dowry.
When Filippo Maria died without male heir on 13 August 1447, the
succession dissolved into the experiment of the Golden Ambrosian
Republic, which did the one thing a fiscally shaken polity facing
war could do: it hired the best contractor available — Sforza —
as captain-general. He fought Venice for the republic, then came
to terms with Venice against it (October 1448), blockaded Milan
into famine, and when the starving city rose on 25 February 1450
he entered it the next day; on 25 March 1450 the assembly
acclaimed him duke. Note the year — the same year Normandy fell
to France, a coincidence this book does not convert into a
connection — and note, harder, what never followed. The empire
never invested him. Frederick III regarded the house as
usurpers; the first Sforza to receive formal imperial
investiture of Milan was Ludovico il Moro, from Maximilian, in
the mid-1490s — nearly half a century after the acclamation.
Francesco ruled regardless: coined, taxed, legislated, made war
and peace, founded a dynasty. Legitimacy followed force, and
never quite caught up.

That gap — operative authority exercised for fifty years while
granted legitimacy was withheld — is promoted, on review, to a
thread the book carries to its end: functional sovereignty and
formal sovereignty are separable, and the functional kind can
run a great polity for generations without the formal kind ever
arriving. Part III will need the distinction when it assesses
actors whose capabilities are sovereign-grade and whose charters
are corporate. Here, the case yields its checklist, and the
outline's five conditions can now be dated to the events: a
fiscally weak central authority (the republic, broke and
besieged); a fiscally strong prize (Milan, worth taking and
capable of sustaining a duke); a dynastic vacuum (no male heir,
a contested succession); a personal military reputation (the
market's best, inherited and enlarged); and a marriage into the
ruling house (1441, nine years before the gate opened). Strike
any condition and the arc fails. That is not a rhetorical
flourish; it is the analytical instrument the rest of the
chapter applies.

## IV.

Apply it first to the man who ran the arc in our own decade and
died of it.

Yevgeny Prigozhin's rise was Russian-institutional rather than
market-Italian: prison in the 1980s, a hot-dog stand,
restaurants, the Concord catering group — "Putin's chef" — and
then Wagner, founded for the Donbas war and publicly
acknowledged by its owner only on 26 September 2022. The
escalation sequence, institutionally sourced: Bakhmut claimed
on 20 May 2023 after the war's bloodiest siege; the ammunition
videos of early May — the contractor publicly cursing the
defence minister and chief of the general staff by name; the
trigger — the Ministry of Defence's order of 10 June 2023 that
all volunteer formations sign ministry contracts by 1 July,
which Putin publicly endorsed and Prigozhin refused; and the
mutiny of 23–24 June 2023 — Rostov-on-Don and its military
headquarters seized without resistance, armoured columns
driving to within roughly two hundred kilometres of Moscow, at
least thirteen Russian airmen killed in the shooting-down of
helicopters and a command aircraft [^5-1], the whole enterprise
stood down in a single evening through a Belarusian-brokered
arrangement whose specifics, in the Institute for the Study of
War's durable caveat, remain "unclear in the open source."
Two months later, on 23 August 2023, his aircraft fell out of
the sky north of Moscow with all ten aboard, Wagner's founder
and its field commander among them; genetic confirmation
followed within days; the American assessment of an
intentional explosion and the Wall Street Journal's account of
the operation's authorship are, respectively, an attributed
intelligence assessment and single-outlet investigative
reporting, and this book carries them as exactly that. The
aftermath is the datum the book cares most about: Wagner's
theatres, contracts and support services passed, in RUSI's
summary, "without much trouble" to the ministry's new Africa
Corps — the company nationalised by its paymaster within a
season of its founder's death.

Run the checklist. A fiscally weak centre? The Russian state of
2023 was sanctioned, strained — and fiscally functional, its
army in the field and its wages paid. A prize capable of
sustaining independence? There was no Milan; Rostov was seized
and could not be held, still less financed. A dynastic vacuum?
The succession was occupied. An owned reputation? Wagner's
prestige was real but rented — built on state ammunition, state
airlift, state prisons for recruiting; and the fiscal base
beneath it was state catering and state contracts, Chapter 3's
paymaster pattern in Russian dress. A marriage into the ruling
house? The court connection was patronage, revocable at will —
and revoked. Prigozhin failed every condition that had carried
Sforza, and the conclusion is structural, not biographical: the
Sforza move from below is closed, not because ambition died but
because the modern strong state holds the market for force too
completely — it is the paymaster, the arsenal, the recruiter
and the court, and a contractor who moves against it moves
against his own supply chain. What remains open is the move the
buyers themselves now run.

## V.

Call it *Sforza-from-above*: the state does not wait for the
condottiere to take a duchy; it gives him a commission, a
clearance and a programme of record — and thereby keeps the
duchy. The pattern is documented from the state's own
announcements, and the American cases are its cleanest run.

Anduril — a venture-built defence firm younger than the war on
terror — was announced in February 2025 as the successor to
Microsoft on the Army's integrated visual augmentation
programme, a franchise of some $22 billion; the Army approved
the contract's novation that April [^5-2], and the
follow-on award came within months. Its collaborative combat
aircraft, selected for prototyping in April 2024 alongside
General Atomics, flew on 31 October 2025 — "clean-sheet to
wheels-up in 556 days," in the programme's own framing — and
its counter-drone franchise with special-operations command
runs under a ceiling reported around the billion mark
(single-outlet on the upper figure; flagged). Palantir became
prime contractor on an Army *hardware* programme — TITAN,
$178.4 million, March 2024 (the "first software prime"
superlative circulates in commentary and is not asserted here)
— and joined the Anduril-led team selected in July 2025 for
the Army's next-generation command-and-control prototype at
$99.6 million (the attribution matters and is corrected here:
an Anduril-led team including Palantir, not a Palantir award).
The consortium reporting and the trade press now speak of "the
new primes" — the venture-defence complex assuming the
position the Cold War giants held [^5-3]. And on 13 June 2025 the state performed the
elevation ceremonially, in a form the fifteenth century would
have understood perfectly: Detachment 201, announced by the
Army itself — four technology executives, among them
Palantir's chief technology officer and OpenAI's chief product
officer, directly commissioned as lieutenant colonels of the
Army Reserve; reportedly without recusal from Pentagon
business touching their companies [^5-4], and with
the Army itself, per the defence press, not entirely certain
what the unit was for [^5-5]. The state conferring
*rank* on the contractor class: Colleoni's life-captaincy,
re-run as a commissioning photograph.

The structure of the American absorption deserves one more
precision, because an absence defines it. There is no federal
equity stake, no golden share, in Palantir or Anduril — the
absence was checked, and it distinguishes these cases both
from the Intel conversion Chapter 10 documented and from the
Chinese fused model of Chapter 2. The American
Sforza-from-above runs through procurement and personnel —
programmes of record, clearances, commissions — not through
ownership. And the disanalogy with 1450 is stated as the
book's discipline requires: elevation from above is on the
state's terms and revocable — the blacklisting of a frontier
lab within a year of the commissioning ceremonies (Chapter
3's dated episode) showed the same hand that raises can
strike — and what it confers is office, not sovereignty. The
condottieri of the 2020s are being given Colleoni's bargain,
not Sforza's throne. Whether any of them can convert office
into something less revocable is precisely the question the
functional-sovereignty thread exists to track.

## VI.

Two further Italian-age institutions complete Part I's
inventory, because each is the developed medieval form of an
actor the modern literature treats as unprecedented.

The first is the Hanseatic League, and the chapter's claim is
calibrated: the Hansa is a far better structural parallel to
the hyperscaler platform than either of Chapter 2's
universalisms — and its differences are the teaching. What the
League was: a transnational commercial network that operated
law, courts, coercion and extraterritorial enclaves across the
sovereignty of others. Its diet — the Hansetag, formally
convened at Lübeck from the mid-fourteenth century (the date
tradition varies and is flagged [GAP: Dollinger]) — issued
recesses that bound, imperfectly, a fluid membership; its
legal order was layered rather than codified (Lübeck law's
appellate hierarchy, diet recesses, the statutes of the
trading posts — this book avoids "Hanserecht" as if it named
a single code); and its four great Kontors were sovereign
enclaves in everything but name: the palisaded Peterhof at
Novgorod, whose statute — the *Schra* — is the oldest of the
Kontor codes; Bryggen at Bergen; the Bruges Kontor at the
centre of Flemish exchange; and the London Steelyard, a
walled compound with its own weigh-house and guildhall,
privileged from the Carta Mercatoria of 1303 to a
confirmatory Act of 1503. When the League fought, it fought
as a contract coalition: the Confederation of Cologne
(November 1367) pooled the towns against Denmark, and the
Treaty of Stralsund (24 May 1370) — the League's Castillon —
delivered fifteen years of Scanian fortresses, toll revenue,
and a right of prior consent over the Danish succession
(consent, not the encyclopedias' "veto" [GAP: Dollinger for
the clause wording]). A merchant network holding fortresses
and approving kings: Bull's private international violence
and Kobrin's non-territorial authority, operating together,
four centuries before either was theorised.

The disanalogies are why the parallel earns its place. The
Hansa had no centre, no treasury, no standing force and no
legal personality — it was, in the language of Chapter 2, all
network and no institution; the platforms have headquarters,
balance sheets and personhood, which makes them *more*
absorbable by consolidating states, not less. And the
League's end is the cautionary line Part III will quote back:
it was never defeated; it "silently disintegrated" as the
territorial states consolidated around it — Ivan III closed
the Peterhof in 1494; Elizabeth expelled the merchants in
1597 and the Steelyard closed the next year. The
consolidating state ends the enclave. No battle is recorded
because none was needed: the state simply, at last, could.

The second institution is Venice — and Venice belongs at
Part I's close because it unsettles the diagnosis from
inside. The republic developed, out of commercial necessity
and three centuries before Louis XIV, every apparatus the
modern literature treats as the signature of the
twentieth-century state. Surveillance: the Council of Ten,
created in 1310 after the Tiepolo conspiracy and permanent
within a generation, with secret funds, informer networks
and police powers, its lion-mouthed denunciation boxes — the
*bocche di leone* — set into the city's walls; and the
detail that redeems the institution from caricature:
anonymous denunciations were, as a rule, *destroyed* unless
of special gravity — the state demanding attributable
information, a procedural scruple many modern tip-lines do
not match [GAP: the voting-threshold rule awaits Lane;
Chambers & Pullan]. Manufacturing: the Arsenal, the largest
industrial complex of pre-industrial Europe — some
forty-five hectares, a workforce around sixteen thousand at
peak, standardised assembly-line galley production (the
famous galley-a-day is a surge anecdote from a state visit,
and is cited as anecdote). Finance: the *prestiti* — forced
loans on the wealthy, consolidated in 1262 into a single
funded debt paying five per cent semi-annually, with an
active secondary market: the first funded sovereign debt in
European history, the state converting its richest citizens
into its creditors and thereby into stakeholders in its
survival [GAP: the consolidation's specifics await Lane &
Mueller; the in-repo Pezzolo chapter proved adjacent, not
on point]. To do any of this the state had first to *see* —
to assess wealth, register persons, track obligation — and
fiscal legibility of that kind is the precondition of
consolidated finance everywhere this book looks; the
mechanism recurs in Part III, and the modern parallels are
left to it. The point Venice fixes here is narrower and
sharper: surveillance, state manufacturing and funded debt
arose in a *republic*, out of commerce, inside the plural
medieval order — they are not modern impositions on the
neomedieval condition but state-form phenomena that emerge
wherever scale, commerce and insecurity meet. The dispersal
contains, everywhere, the seeds of its own consolidators.

## VII.

Part I can now be closed, because its inventory is complete
and its verdict writes itself. The neomedieval literature —
Bull's trends, Friedrichs's duality, Cerny's checklist,
Kobrin's dissolved jurisdiction, McFate's returned
mercenaries — taught us to see the dispersal, and the
dispersal is real: the churches of Chapter 2, the incorporated
retinues of Chapter 3, the contract polities and commercial
sovereignties of this chapter. But walk back through the four
chapters and a second pattern stands in every one of them,
unremarked by the literature that supplied the first. The
condottiere was domesticated — by market competition, by
integration, by the axe between the columns. The Hansa's
enclaves were closed — by consolidating monarchies that
finally could. Venice became the proto-state itself. The
platforms are designated, fined, licensed and commissioned by
the polities they were said to have surpassed. The dispersal's
most developed actors are everywhere being absorbed by
fiscally capable centres — and the literature has no account
of the force that does the absorbing, because it never asked
how the first dispersal ended. That is the question Part II
exists to answer, and it begins where consolidation was least
in evidence: with the centres themselves failing. In the first
days of August 1453, at a hunting lodge in Wiltshire, the
king of England stopped speaking — and the crisis his silence
disclosed is where the mechanism's story starts.

# Chapter 5 — The Undermighty Kings

## I.

In the first days of August 1453, at the royal hunting lodge of
Clarendon in Wiltshire, the king of England stopped.

There is no better verb. Henry VI did not sicken in any way his
physicians could name, nor rave, nor take to his bed in the ordinary
manner of royal illness. He simply ceased to function: in the clinical
reconstruction, he "did not talk, sat slumped, had to be fed and moved,
did not recognise or respond to others and was incapable of government
for a year and a half" [^6-1]. When his only son was
born that October and presented to him around the new year, he gave no
sign of knowing what he was being shown. The traditional precipitant,
recorded in the sources and repeated with due caution in the modern
clinical literature, was "an unexpected fright – news of defeat at
Castillon on 17 July 1453" [^6-2]: the battle with which this
book opened, the old captain dead before the gun park, the last English
province gone. The kingdom's response to its sovereign's absence was,
for eight months, to pretend there wasn't one. Only in March 1454 did
the lords appoint Richard, duke of York, Protector and Defender of the
Realm; the king recovered his senses around Christmas 1454; the
protectorate was wound up within weeks; and five months after that the
armed retinues of the rival affinities met at St Albans, in the
engagement conventionally counted the first battle of the Wars of the
Roses.

It is tempting — and this book must resist the temptation carefully,
because the resonance is real and the causation is not — to read 1453
as a single event. [BRIDGE — the triple-hinge framing from here to the
end of the paragraph is illustration, never evidence; the analogy joint
is Roderick's.] In one calendar year the old order of Latin Christendom
broke in three places for three unrelated reasons. In May,
Constantinople — the second Rome, besieged a dozen times across a
thousand years — fell to Mehmed II, its land walls opened in part by
Ottoman bombardment. In July, Castillon ended three centuries of
English France, by the mechanism Chapter 6 will anatomise. In August,
the king of England went silent in Wiltshire. A siege, a battle, a
breakdown: an artillery success, an artillery-adjacent defeat, and a
psychiatric event with no artillery in it at all. Nothing joins the
three causally, and the chapter that follows will not pretend
otherwise. What the three shared was the thing exposed: in each case a
sovereign order organised around a single person — an emperor on the
walls, a king's lieutenant in the field, a king in his chair — was
tested at the point where the person and the office meet, and in each
case what the test revealed was how little lay behind the office when
the person failed. [END BRIDGE]

The subject of this chapter is that revelation, generalised. The
half-century on either side of 1450 was Europe's great age of what the
political scientists studying our own time would call hollow
sovereignty: the crown as a legal fiction disconnected from executive
capacity — authority intact in law, in ceremony, in the addresses of
petitions and the dating clauses of charters, and unable to raise a
tax, relieve a fortress or discipline a magnate. The English case is
the most famous because its sequel — thirty years of dynastic
bloodletting — makes the best story, and because English historiography
long treated it as a distinctively English catastrophe: a saintly
simpleton on the throne, wicked uncles, the wheel of fortune. The
argument of this chapter is that the English case was one instance of a
continental pattern, and that the pattern has a structure. The
chapter's title term should be credited before it is used: the
undermighty king is K. B. McFarlane's formulation — his famous dictum
holds that only a king who was under-mighty had anything to fear from
over-mighty subjects [GAP: pin the verbatim wording at retrieval —
McFarlane's essays are on the acquisition list] — and the debt is
acknowledged here because what follows deploys his term against part
of his point: this chapter generalises the condition across the
Channel and forward five centuries, and argues (§VI) that the
causation his dictum compresses runs in a direction the English
evidence alone leaves open. Personal
monarchy was a constitution with a single point of failure. Under
ordinary load, its redundancies — council, household, the great
offices — could carry a minority or a short illness. Under the
fiscal-military load the fifteenth century was now applying, they
could not. Hollow sovereignty was not a run of unfortunate accidents.
It was the systemic failure mode of the personalised constitution
under a demand curve that was moving, and the polities of mid-century
Europe were about to divide into those that found a structural answer
to it and those that became its case studies.

The chapter runs the comparison in three monarchies and then forward
five centuries. It takes Henry VI straight, as the type-case; reads
Charles VI's France as the deeper and more instructive precedent; sets
the Ottoman succession crises of the 1440s beside both, as the same
problem heading toward a radically different solution; and then asks
what the condition looks like in our own century — because it is
observable now, in two distinct forms, and the book's diagnostic
chapters have already met both without naming them.

## II.

Take the English case first, and take it straight — which means taking
it institutionally.

The reign was structurally anomalous long before it was medically so.
Henry VI came to the throne at nine months old, inheriting not one
kingdom but two — the double monarchy manufactured, as we shall see, by
another king's incapacity — and England was governed for fifteen years
by a minority council that governed, by the standards of the age,
remarkably well. The anomaly deepened as the king aged: majority
arrived and the person expected to fill the office did not appear.
John Watts, whose account of the reign this chapter follows at the
interpretive level, puts the finding with academic restraint: "There
seems to me to be little evidence that Henry himself was the author of
his slow and halting progression to something resembling full
authority: indeed, the fact that it was so slow and, in certain ways,
incomplete tends to suggest the opposite" [^6-3]. The
council had spent the minority constructing what Watts calls an
artificial exercise of royal power; his description of the 1435–45
transition is "the peaceful transfer of authority from artificial
council to (in effect) artificial king" — the machinery of conciliar
signatures amounting, in his phrase, to "the creation of an artificial
royal will in periods when the king could exercise no will in person"
[^6-4]. Read those formulations slowly, because they
are load-bearing for everything this book will later say about
executive fictions. A generation before the catatonia, England was
already being governed by a constructed royal will — a corporate
simulation of the king, maintained by the men around him because the
constitution provided no other way to act. The stupor of 1453 did not
create the vacancy. It disclosed one of long standing, and made it
undeniable [GAP: the administrative texture of the personal rule —
patronage flows, household finance — awaits Griffiths, *The Reign of
King Henry VI*, still on the acquisition list; the framing here rests
on the verified Watts chapters].

It is worth dwelling on why the disclosure was so difficult to act
upon, because the difficulty is constitutional and it recurs in every
case this chapter examines. Medieval monarchy had one well-worn
mechanism for the absent executive: the minority. Kings died leaving
children often enough that every realm had precedents, personnel and a
vocabulary for councils of regency — England had just run one for
fifteen years, competently. But a minority is a *scheduled* vacancy:
it has a known end-point, a boy who ages at a known rate, and
therefore a settlement everyone can price. Incapacity in an adult king
had no schedule. No one in 1453 could say whether the king's absence
would last a month or a reign; any magnate who accepted large powers
risked being destroyed by a recovered king, and any who declined them
left the powers lying in the street. The queen's claim to a regency
foundered on the lords' refusal; York's protectorate was drawn as
narrowly as the drafting could manage and terminated at the first
sign of recovery. The constitution, in other words, could handle the
predictable failure of its single point of failure and not the
unpredictable one — a distinction any engineer would recognise, and
one that will matter again when this chapter reaches the polities of
our own century, whose provisions for executive incapacity likewise
assume that it announces itself cleanly and ends on schedule.

The chronology of the losses matters, and this book has already fixed
it. Normandy fell in 1449–50 — the cascade of compositions Chapter 6
counts — three years before the king's collapse; Gascony fell in the
summer of 1453, and only that second loss coincided with the
incapacity. The sequence forbids the comfortable reading in which
military catastrophe follows royal breakdown. The military catastrophe
came first, to a king fully conscious and fully enthroned; what it
demonstrated was that England's executive incapacity did not require a
catatonic king — the conscious one had presided over the loss of a
province without ever taking the field. The breakdown then removed
even the fiction. For eight months the realm had no functioning
executive *and no mechanism for admitting it*: the queen's claim to a
regency was unacceptable to the lords, York's pre-eminence was
unacceptable to the court party, and the compromise — the protectorate
of March 1454 — was improvised, grudging, and dissolved the moment the
king could sit upright. Somerset came out of the Tower; York's
appointments were reversed; and the political nation, having twice
failed to construct a durable substitute executive, reverted to the
only instrument it had left, which was force. St Albans followed in
May 1455.

That sequencing is why this chapter treats the Wars of the Roses as
symptom rather than cause — the interpretive line for which Watts is
the authority and which the evidence above supports. The dynastic
reading, in which ambitious York reaches for a crown weakly held,
inverts the mechanism: York reached because the crown could not
execute, and because every attempt to build a lawful substitute for
its execution had failed. The wars were the form the vacancy took once
the peaceful workarounds — council, protectorate, the artificial royal
will — had each been tried and each been dismantled. A constitution
that could only be operated by a functioning king, operated without
one, produced faction as its output. It would go on producing it for
thirty years.

## III.

England's crisis was the second performance. The first, longer and
constitutionally more instructive, had run in France for a generation,
and its lesson is sharper precisely because its medical facts were
different.

On 5 August 1392, riding with his escort in the forest near Le Mans,
Charles VI turned on his own men and killed about four of them before
he could be restrained. The attack was the first of many: another
followed in 1393, six more in 1399, and the episodes recurred for the
remaining three decades of the reign. (The popular tradition has
embroidered the case — the forty-four counted episodes, the delusion
of being made of glass — and this book, which has a rule about
inherited anecdotes, confines itself to the documented attacks.) The
clinical picture, whatever retrospective diagnosis one favours, was
the opposite of Henry VI's in the respect that matters: Henry's
incapacity was total and continuous for eighteen months; Charles's was
recurrent and intermittent for thirty years. He was periodically,
genuinely lucid — and the lucid intervals were the constitutional
poison. A permanently incapable king can, in principle, be replaced by
a formal regency; the mechanism exists in most monarchical
constitutions precisely because minorities force its invention. An
*intermittently* capable king can be replaced by nothing, because
every arrangement made during an absence is unmade, or contested, on
his return. Power flowed to whoever stood nearest the chair when the
king's mind left it — and flowed again, in a different direction, when
his mind returned. The French constitution spent thirty years being
rewritten twice a year.

The comparison with England should be drawn precisely, because the
clinical difference is the analytic instrument, not an obstacle to be
apologised for. Henry VI's total incapacity posed the constitution a
hard question once, and the constitution failed it slowly; Charles
VI's intermittent incapacity posed the question afresh at every
relapse and every recovery, and so the French constitution failed it
serially, in public, for a generation. If the failure mode were a
matter of the particular illness — a catatonic king producing one
politics, a psychotic king another — the institutional outcomes should
have differed as the conditions did. They converged: in both realms,
the substitute-executive question ate the polity. Regency mechanisms
failed to stabilise; the great affinities and the ducal houses armed
around the vacancy; faction hardened into civil war; and the crown's
fiscal capacity — the one resource a substitute executive most needs —
eroded fastest of all, because taxation in a personalised
constitution rested on consent to a person, and there was no person
to consent to. Two different diseases, one institutional syndrome:
that is the strongest evidence the fifteenth century offers that the
syndrome belonged to the constitution and not to the patient — and it
is why this chapter's comparisons run at the level of institutional
response and stop, deliberately, at the door of retrospective
diagnosis.

What that dynamic produced was not merely instability but armed
faction organised around the substitute-executive question itself. The
king's brother Louis of Orléans and his cousin John the Fearless of
Burgundy each claimed the governance the king could not exercise; on
23 November 1407 John resolved the argument by having Louis
assassinated in a Paris street; the Armagnac–Burgundian civil war
that followed consumed France for a generation, inviting the English
back into a kingdom too divided to resist them. The war's second
assassination — John the Fearless, cut down at the bridge of
Montereau on 10 September 1419 at a parley with the Dauphin's party —
drove his heir Philip the Good into the English alliance, and the
alliance produced, at Troyes on 21 May 1420, the constitutional
monstrosity in which the whole sequence terminated: the mad king's
treaty, sealed while Charles was in no condition to weigh it,
disinheriting his own son and settling the crown of France, on
Charles's death, on Henry V of England and his heirs [GAP: the court
detail and the Burgundian side await Famiglietti's *Royal Intrigue*
and Vaughan's *John the Fearless* and *Philip the Good*, all on the
acquisition list; the sequence and dates above are the settled
consensus record].

Pause on what Troyes is, constitutionally. It is hollow sovereignty's
purest artefact: the legal person of the king of France — intact,
sealed, formally competent — deployed by the factions controlling his
person to alienate the succession itself. The office executed; the man
could not; and the gap between the two was wide enough to sign a
kingdom through. And its first beneficiary was its next victim. Henry
V died in 1422, two months before the mad king whose crown he was to
inherit, and the double monarchy of Troyes descended on a nine-month-
old — the infant of Chapter 5's second section, whose minority council
would govern an impossible inheritance for fifteen years and whose
majority would disclose that the inheritance had swallowed the heir.
The English and French crises were not parallel cases; they were one
case, propagating. Charles VI's incapacity manufactured the treaty;
the treaty manufactured Henry VI's inheritance; the inheritance's
military liquidation — Normandy in 1450, by the mechanism of the next
chapter; Gascony in 1453 — broke what remained of the heir. The
undermighty kings begat one another.

## IV.

Lest the pattern seem a Western or a Valois-Lancastrian peculiarity,
set the third case beside it, from the power that was about to end the
Roman millennium.

The Ottoman state in the 1440s ran the same experiment with different
variables. Murad II, weary of the throne, abdicated in 1444 in favour
of his son Mehmed — a boy of about twelve — and retired; within
months the Christian powers, reading a child sultan as a vacancy,
launched the crusade that Murad returned from retirement to destroy
at Varna in November 1444. The boy remained nominally sovereign; the
experiment continued; and in 1446 it ended in the manner such
experiments end — a Janissary rising, the Buçuktepe revolt,
engineered by the grand vizier Çandarlı Halil Pasha, which returned
Murad to the throne and Mehmed to the provinces. The Ottoman
succession crisis of the 1440s is Henry VI's minority and Charles
VI's regency contest in another key: a personalised sovereignty
briefly detached from a person capable of executing it, and the
armed and administrative interests around the office immediately
contesting its exercise. Mehmed's second accession, on his father's
death in February 1451, was the recovery — and its first great act,
two years later, was the siege whose bombardment the reader has
already met in this chapter's opening frame.

The reason the Ottoman case earns its place here, rather than merely
completing a trio, is the shape of the solution it was incubating.
The Western monarchies faced their over-mighty subjects — the
affinities, the ducal houses — with the tools of a hereditary
aristocratic constitution: negotiation, patronage, marriage, and,
failing all else, civil war. The Ottoman answer, already mature by
mid-century, was the *devşirme*: the levy of Christian boys, converted,
trained, and formed into the Janissary corps and the administrative
class — a service elite manufactured from raw material, owing
everything to the sultan and nothing to any hereditary interest,
because it had none. İnalcık's classic formulation — the *kul* system
as the most potent factor favouring Ottoman unity and centralised
administration — is the standard scholarly framing [GAP: İnalcık and
Kafadar are on the acquisition list; the formulation is cited here at
second hand and must not harden until retrieved]. The chapter names
the institution and holds it; Chapter 11 will develop it as the most
radical of the century's consolidation variants — the polity that
answered the over-mighty subject not by defeating him but by
manufacturing his replacement.

Even in the Ottoman case, note, the crisis decade displays the
pattern's signature: it was not the sovereign's weakness that
created the contending interests — the Janissary corps and the
vizieral households were mature institutions, built by strong
sultans for strong sultans — but the sovereign's abdication that
revealed how independently those interests could act. Çandarlı
Halil's engineered revolt was the act of an over-mighty servant, not
an over-mighty vassal, and the distinction previews the argument of
this chapter's sixth section: the form of the concentrated
capability varies with the constitution — hereditary affinity in
England, ducal apanage in France, a slave-recruited praetorian
corps and administrative dynasty at the Porte — but the direction of
causation does not. Capability concentrates first, inside a
functioning order; the weak moment then discloses what the strong
years had grown.

## V.

Five centuries on, the condition has returned, and the book's
diagnostic chapters have already walked through its territory without
pausing to name the structure. Name it now, and distinguish its two
forms — because the distinction is what keeps the concept honest.

The first form is territorial: the state, legally recognised and
seated in the international system, that cannot control the ground it
is sovereign over. Somalia has stood at or near the top of the Fragile
States Index for years (first, in the 2024 ranking — a composite
index, cited as such); in the areas of south-central Somalia beyond
Mogadishu's writ, Al-Shabaab does not merely contest the state but
performs it, running courts whose judgments are enforced and levying
taxation businesses describe as more predictable than the
government's — the movement's revenue is estimated, and the estimates
are flagged as such, in the low hundreds of millions of dollars a
year (EU Agency for Asylum; UNODC). Lebanon's case is subtler and in
one respect darker: the World Bank's assessment of its financial
collapse — real GDP contracting 19.2 per cent in 2020 alone — is
titled "deliberate depression," the adjective chosen because the
ruin was elite-managed; and the state shares its territory, its
border policy and effectively its war powers with Hezbollah, described
in the standard analysis as a state within a state and, in the
sharper recent variant, a state within a non-state — the government
having lost the capacity to deliver the services against which the
militia's parallel provision could be measured. Libya has two
governments, two central banks by turns, and an internationally
recognised authority whose writ stops at militia checkpoints within
its own capital. The scholarly literature has stopped calling such
spaces "ungoverned" — the corrective, in Clunan and Trinkunas's
phrase, is *alternatively* governed: the question is never whether
rule exists but who exercises it. Every element of that sentence
could be transposed to the Norman march or the Armagnac south of
1420 without strain. [BRIDGE — one sentence; the transposition is
Roderick's to keep or cut.]

The second form is the one the reader is less accustomed to calling
by the same name, and the chapter insists on the distinction even as
it applies the common label: functional hollow sovereignty — the
capable, wealthy, legitimate state that cannot execute a specific
function it nominally owns. Three exhibits, each from the state's own
paperwork. The United States government publishes, through the
Government Accountability Office, an annual assessment of its own
weapons acquisition, and the assessment is a study in a state
auditing its own executive incapacity with perfect candour and no
effect. The recent editions record that a major system now takes on
average more than twelve years to field; that the average delivery
timeline for major defence acquisition programmes has lengthened
from roughly eight years to roughly eleven under successive waves of
acquisition reform; that the Navy's lead frigate programme is more
than three years late with construction effectively stalled, and
that its shipbuilding programmes generally run one to three years
behind schedule [^6-5]. None of this is secret, contested or unmeasured; it is
measured annually, by the sovereign, about itself. The hollow
crown of the fifteenth century at least required chroniclers to
infer its condition. The modern functional variant publishes its
own diagnosis and proceeds. The European Union commissioned its own diagnosis; the
Draghi report's Part B states it without cushioning: "Of a total of
EUR 75 billion spent by Member States between June 2022 and June
2023, 78% of procurement spending was diverted to purchases from
suppliers located outside the EU, out of which 63% based in the US"
[^6-6]. A continental economy of four
hundred and fifty million people, formally committed to strategic
autonomy, executes three-quarters of its defence procurement abroad.
And Japan — orderly, rich, administratively superb — publishes each
year, through its health ministry's vital statistics, the numbers of
an incapacity no ministry can execute against: 686,061 births in 2024,
a record low and the ninth consecutive annual decline; a total
fertility rate of 1.15, likewise a record; a national population
falling by the better part of a million a year, with roughly thirty
per cent of the country over sixty-five [^6-7]. No insurgency contests the Japanese state's
territory. A function on which the state's long-run existence
depends is nevertheless outside its executive reach.

The two forms must not borrow gravity from each other — a delayed
frigate is not Mogadishu, and the chapter that lets the vivid
territorial cases lend their menace to the functional ones has
cheated. What unites them is a precise structural predicate, not a
severity: in each, a legally recognised central authority cannot
execute a function it nominally holds. That predicate is Cerny's
durable disorder wearing institutional clothes, and it is the
condition this book's Part I called neomedieval. The fifteenth-century
reader would have recognised it at once. Henry VI's England was a
functional case that became a territorial one: the law ran in the
king's name to the end; what failed, by degrees, was execution —
first of war finance, then of provincial defence, then of civic peace
— until the affinities were performing the state's core function,
organised violence, on their own account. The modal path from
functional to territorial hollowness is the fifteenth century's
warning to the twenty-first, and the chapter states it as a warning,
not a prophecy.

## VI.

There remains the question of direction, and it is the chapter's
sharpest claim, because getting the causation backwards produces a
comfortable and useless history.

The comfortable version says: weak kings let subjects grow over-
mighty. It flatters the constitution — the system was sound, the
personnel failed — and it underwrites the perennial hope that better
personnel will restore the system. The evidence of this half-century
runs the other way. The over-mighty subject came first. The
affinities of Lancastrian England — Beaufort's, York's, the
Nevilles' — were built across decades of royal adequacy, out of the
bastard-feudal machinery Chapter 3 anatomised: retaining, livery,
the privatised county. The ducal houses that dismembered Charles VI's
France — Burgundy above all — were creations of royal grant in the
1360s, endowed before anyone knew the king's mind would fail. The
capability concentrated first, lawfully, inside a functioning order;
the crown's weakness did not create the magnates, it *revealed* them,
by removing the one force that had held their rivalry in
equilibrium. McFarlane's dictum, credited in §I, states the
correlation; the sequence above fixes its direction. Undermighty
kings do not produce over-mighty subjects. Over-mighty subjects,
encountering an undermighty king, produce the
crisis — and the crisis then runs on the subjects' resources, which
is why it lasts. The Wars of the Roses were fought by private
armies that existed before the king fell ill and would have existed
had he never fallen ill at all.

The direction of causation is not a pedantry; it determines what
counts as a remedy. If weak kings make over-mighty subjects, the cure
is a strong king — better personnel, the perennial and comfortable
prescription, requiring nothing of the constitution itself. If
over-mighty subjects make kings weak, then personnel is beside the
point: a vigorous adult Henry VI would still have confronted
affinities whose retinues, revenues and county administrations had
grown for two generations inside the legal order, and his vigour
would have changed the management of the problem, not its existence.
The fifteenth century ran both experiments. England got better
personnel — Edward IV was everything Henry VI was not — and the
structural problem outlasted him, because it was structural. France
got a structural answer — the standing army, the permanent tax, the
artillery establishment — and the problem ended, not because French
kings became uniformly strong but because the resource asymmetry
between crown and magnate was rebuilt until no magnate's capability
could rival the crown's. That asymmetry, and the instrument that
anchored it, is the subject the reader has already met in Chapter 6
and will meet as institution in Chapter 10. The lesson generalises,
and Part III will lean on it: where capability has concentrated
outside the centre, changes of leadership at the centre are
cosmetic. The remedy that works is the one that changes what the
centre can afford that its subjects cannot.

The reason this matters beyond the fifteenth century is that the
structural analogue is now in place, and the book has already
documented it in its diagnostic register. [BRIDGE — the passage to
the end of this paragraph is the analogy joint; disanalogy stated;
Roderick's to set.] The over-mighty subjects of the present are not
territorial magnates but the continental-scale platform and
infrastructure firms whose capabilities Part I inventoried —
entities with independent fiscal bases, clienteles measured in
billions, and functions that the commentary now describes in frankly
political vocabulary: Bremmer's "technopolar" moment; the Carnegie
analysis of firms as "digital leviathans" performing functions
"that increasingly mirror those of states," including "core military
functions—command and control, reconnaissance, analytics" (Bremmer;
Csernatoni 2025 — both cited as framing, per this memo's reviewed
rule, with the load-bearing evidence reserved to the primary
regulatory and procurement record of Chapters 1–2). The disanalogy
is stated at once: a retinue is not a data centre; a firm holds no
lances, levies no private war, and can in the last resort be broken
by a state that decides to break it — as Chapter 2's China sequence
demonstrated, and as the book's Part III expects the consolidating
blocs to attempt. The parallel licensed here is structural and
narrow: capability concentrated lawfully inside a functioning order,
across decades in which the centre could not or did not resist its
growth; a centre now visibly unable to execute against it; and a
political class discovering, like the lords of 1454, that the
constitution provides no agreed mechanism for the substitution of
executive capacity the moment demands. The over-mighty subject
precedes the undermighty king. That ordering held in 1450. The book
will argue it holds now. [END BRIDGE]

## VII.

One calibration, and then the turn.

The claim of this chapter is not that everything sagged at once —
"modal condition" must not be allowed to mean universal condition.
The same decades that broke England and had broken France contain
Portugal stable, Aragon expanding, and above all France itself
*recovering*: Charles VII's kingdom, the wreckage of Troyes, was in
precisely these years constructing the standing army, the permanent
taxation and the artillery establishment whose operations Chapter 6
has already displayed. The claim is bounded: hollow sovereignty was
the modal condition of the personalised monarchies under acute
fiscal-military strain in the second quarter of the fifteenth
century. France belongs in the frame not as a counter-example but as
the pivot — the polity that passed through the condition first, at
its worst, and came out the other side transformed, because its
crisis ran so deep that the workarounds available to England (the
council, the artificial royal will, the dignified pretence) were
never available to it. The kingdom that had been signed away at
Troyes could not simulate its king; it had to rebuild the office
from the treasury upward. What that rebuilding produced — the
instrument, the institution and the price list — the reader has
seen at work in Normandy.

A crown that cannot execute is not a government; it is a title deed
to one. The fifteenth century's undermighty kings held the deed and
lost the estate, and the men who watched it happen drew the
century's operative conclusion: that the deed could be made good
only by an executive capacity built outside the king's person —
salaried, standing, and paid for by taxation that did not pause for
his health. Two of the polities in this chapter's frame found their
way to versions of that answer, and one of them found it first,
under artillery this book has already heard. The question the
crisis chapters leave open is therefore not whether the hollow
crowns could be refilled — France was demonstrating that they
could — but what it cost, who was made to pay, and what happened,
inside the winning polity and the losing one alike, to the men and
institutions the new executive rendered obsolete. England, which
found no answer until the century's end, ran the control experiment:
its bill for Normandy came due not in France but in Kent, in the
summer of 1450, presented by armed petitioners whom the crown's own
veterans had trained. What an undermighty king's subjects do when
the state's failure arrives in their own parishes — and what their
rising reveals about where executive capacity had actually gone —
is the business of the sequence this chapter opens: first the
instrument (Chapter 6), then the reckoning at home (Chapter 7).

# Chapter 6 — 1450: The Year the Walls Fell

## I.

*Cy ensuit les noms des cites, villes et places, chasteaux et forteresses
qui ont este perdus durant la charge et gouvernance du duc de Somerset* —
"the cities, towns and places, castles, and fortresses which have been
lost during the charge and governance of the duke of Somerset" [^7-1].

The document that opens with these words is not a chronicle. It is a
ledger. Some contemporary English hand — hostile to Somerset, compiling
ammunition for the reckoning that was already gathering at home — sat down
after the disasters of 1449 and 1450 and listed, place by place, every
fortified point the English had lost in Normandy: the great cities and the
river castles, the walled towns and the isolated keeps, each with a line
recording how it fell. Clifford Rogers, who brought the list back into the
military-historical argument, counts one hundred strongpoints taken by the
French in 1450, "including Château Gaillard, St-Sauveur-le-Vicomte,
Cherbourg, Roche-Guyon, and Rouen, all of which had earlier required long
sieges to capture" [^7-2]. A hundred fortified places in roughly a year, in a century in which
a single first-rate siege was understood to be a season's work, and often
a year's.

But the list's real evidence is not the number. It is the grammar. Read
down the entries and the same phrases recur with bureaucratic
monotony: *par composition* — surrendered on terms; *desemparee pour
doubte des ennemys* — abandoned for fear of the enemy. A minority fell
*dassault* or *par traison*; the places that resisted at all held for
days, not months — Touques "tint trois jours," Harcourt "tint huit jours"
[^7-3]. And nowhere in the hundred entries —
nowhere — does the list record a storming through an artillery breach,
and nowhere a sack. The two places whose walls the guns demonstrably
opened,
Bayeux and Avranches, both appear with the same closing formula as the
places that never saw a gun: taken *by composition*. The most violent
military instrument Europe had yet produced ran through the strongest
fortified province in Europe, and the paperwork it left behind is a
stack of negotiated surrenders.

That paradox is this chapter's subject, and the book's hinge. The
argument so far has been diagnostic: Part I found in our own moment a
dispersal of authority — plural, overlapping, contractual — that the
political theorists called neomedieval, and found the label more
accurate than its coiners quite intended. What the argument needs now is
a mechanism: a demonstration that the first medieval dispersal was ended
by an identifiable, dateable, technology-specific change in the price of
decisive force, because it is that mechanism — not any resemblance
between platform and prince — which the rest of the book claims is
operating again. This chapter is therefore the point of maximum
exposure. If the artillery revolution of the mid-fifteenth century did
not in fact change the economics of sovereignty, the book fails, and
fails here. The evidence is offered in that spirit: specific enough to
be checked, and specific enough to be wrong.

The claim to be tested is not the one the reader may expect. It is not
that the new French siege train battered Normandy's walls flat; the
Somerset list just told us it did not, and the historians who know the
campaign best would rightly savage the assertion. The claim is narrower,
stranger, and better evidenced, and the chapter will spend its length
earning the right to state it plainly. Between 1449 and 1453, at walls
whose defensive value had been proven against two generations of English
and French besiegers, something changed on the attacking side of the
ditch — and the change announced itself less in falling masonry than in
the sudden, cascading willingness of garrisons to sign.

## II.

Begin with the wall, because the wall was a constitution before it was a
structure.

The developed castle of high-medieval Europe was optimised against the
two threats its builders knew: the man on the ladder and the stone-
throwing engine. Against both, height was the governing virtue — a tall
wall defeated escalade and out-ranged the trebuchet's flat trajectories —
and height imposed thinness, because a wall both tall and thick was
ruinously expensive in stone and labour. "Tall and thin" was therefore
not a defect but a design, and for three centuries it worked. The
besieger's realistic options against a competently held first-rate
fortress reduced, in practice, to one: starvation. As English
Heritage's survey of siege practice puts it, "medieval sieges were
settled more often by treachery, starvation, or disease than by
breached walls" [GAP: T3 attribution; upgrade the siege-survey
authority to scholarly level before print]; the type-case, Kenilworth
in 1266, took roughly six months of blockade.
Investment on that timescale was itself a fiscal and political feat —
the besieging army had to be fed, paid and held together longer than the
garrison's stores lasted, while disease worked on the camp and relief
worked on the calendar.

The political consequence of this military fact is the foundation on
which Part I's whole medieval order rested. A lord who held a first-rate
castle held a veto measured in time. To reduce him, his sovereign had to
be willing to spend the better part of a campaigning season — sometimes
more than one — on a single point of the map, at a cost in money,
patience and prestige that made the exercise rare. The castle was, in
effect, a clock wound against the future: its walls told the garrison
how many months of other people's patience it could buy, and every
month bought was a month in which relief might march, plague might
strike the siege lines, or the besieger's coalition might simply come
apart. Fortification, in other words, had a *time value* — the months of
resistance a garrison could reliably sell — and on that time value the
autonomy of every castle-holding magnate, chartered town and
ecclesiastical liberty in Latin Christendom was, in the last resort,
collateralised.

The gun, when it arrived, did not at first threaten any of this. The
bombards of the decades around 1400 threw great stone balls with
impressive striking power, but at short range and with reduced charges —
the alternative being the bursting of the barrel — and against developed
stone fortification they were a slow instrument among slower ones.
Precisely when the balance tipped — when a first-rate siege train became
reliably faster than starvation against first-rate walls — is a question
the technical literature has not settled to the precision one would
like, and this chapter will not pretend otherwise [GAP: the tipping-point
chronology awaits DeVries's monograph and Hall at book level; Rogers
(1993) dates the reversal to the 1420s–1440s, and that dating carries
the argument here]. What can be said with confidence is mechanical
rather than chronological. The high wall that defeated the ladder was a
liability against the gun: tall-thin construction, rubble-cored and
mortared, shattered under sustained horizontal battering as a squat
rammed-earth rampart would not. The virtue and the vulnerability were
the same property. Nothing in the castle's design had to fail for the
castle to fail; the design itself had been repriced.

By the 1490s the repricing was proverbial — Guicciardini would write of
Charles VIII's guns in Italy doing as much execution in hours as the old
engines in days, an observation forty years downstream of our decade and
cited here only to mark where the curve was heading. The business of
this chapter is the decade in which the curve turned, and the
institution that turned it.

## III.

The institution has two names attached to it, and the first discipline
the evidence imposes is to resist making them inventors.

Jean Bureau — Champenois, trained as a financial officer, by turns
receiver, Treasurer of France and, after 1453, mayor of Bordeaux — and
his brother Gaspard were the masters of Charles VII's artillery through
the campaigns this chapter narrates. The office they held was older than
they were: the accounts of the *maîtres de l'artillerie* run in
unbroken series from 1418, and their predecessor Pierre Bessonneau
appears in them until 1440. The succession was gradual and documentary
rather than dramatic: Contamine's judgement is that Bessonneau "seems to
have ceased all official function on 20 February 1440, but, from 1437,
he was practically supplanted by Jean and Gaspard Bureau" [TRANS.
CLAUDE], and the account series bears the sequence out — Jean's accounts
open in August 1439, Gaspard's in January 1441 [^7-4]. The titles under which they served were themselves settling into
institutional form in these years: *maistre de l'artillerie du roy*,
*garde et gouverneur de l'artillerie* — an office of the crown's
financial administration as much as of its army, held by men who audited
as naturally as they besieged.

What the Bureaux did not do is invent the technology they wielded.
Corned powder — the wet-milled, granulated propellant that burned
uniformly where the old dry-mixed "serpentine" powder did not — dates
from shortly after 1400 and belongs to Europe generally. The trunnion
and the mobile carriage are French–Burgundian developments of the
mid-century, fixed in their mature form by about 1465, attributed by
the historians of technology to no single hand. Iron shot existed but
did not displace stone in the Bureau era; their great guns threw stone.
The temptation — to which a long line of popular retellings has
succumbed — is to assemble these devices into a package and hand the
package to the brothers as their invention. The evidence will not bear
it, and the argument does not want it. What the record supports is
something more consequential: the Bureaux built the first *institution*
that took a set of loose, generally available technologies and fielded
them as a standardised, permanently financed, technically staffed royal
siege train. Kelly DeVries, surveying the reconquest's artillery,
locates the achievement exactly there: "perhaps the most important
feature of Charles VII's gunpowder artillery train was its intricate
organization and superior leadership. Under masters of artillery Jean
and Gaspard Bureau… Duties of cannoneers were established, officers
were appointed, competence was improved and pay was increased" [^7-5]. The protagonist of this chapter is not a gadget. It is a
payroll.

The payroll can be read. In 1442 — seven years before the campaign that
made the train famous — Jean Bureau, "commis par Charles VII 'ou fait
et gouvernement' de son artillerie," drew up a procurement programme
for the coming season: the making or refitting of at least six
bombards, sixteen *veuglaires*, twenty *serpentines* and forty
*couleuvrines*, plus an unnumbered quantity of *ribaudequins*, at a
cost of 4,198 livres tournois; the purchase of twenty thousand pounds
of gunpowder, at 2,200 livres; and, for the traditional matériel of an
army — thirty thousand arrows, a thousand lances, a thousand bows, a
thousand quivers — a sufficient 3,100 livres. Contamine, printing the
document, draws the conclusion in a sentence: "Ainsi, dès cette époque,
l'artillerie nouvelle coûte plus cher que l'artillerie ancienne" —
already, at this date, the new artillery cost more than the old
[TRANS. CLAUDE] [^7-6]. Run the arithmetic and the
gunpowder establishment — guns and powder together — stands at roughly
twice the cost of the entire traditional arsenal: 6,398 livres against
3,100. Rogers, working from the same page, made the point that should
detain us: "Artillery was expensive. As early as 1442, the French
government was spending more than twice as much on its artillery train
as it spent on more traditional war materiel… The central governments
of large states could afford to acquire and maintain large siege
trains: their subjects and smaller neighbors, in general, could not"
[^7-7]. Hold that sentence; the chapter will return to who wrote
it and why it matters that he did.

Behind the programme stood the fiscal-administrative substrate without
which it is unintelligible: the reforms of the 1440s that gave the
French crown a permanent paid army — the ordinance companies of 1445 —
and the standing taxation to sustain it. Those reforms are Chapter 10's
business, and the reader is asked to take them on account until then.
What matters here is the conjunction. By the late 1440s the French
crown possessed, uniquely in Latin Europe at this scale, all three
elements of a new kind of military object: a standing corps of paid
specialists, a standardised and centrally maintained park of heavy
guns, and a revenue stream that did not lapse when a campaign ended.
No baron had this. No city had this. No league of either could
plausibly assemble it. The significance of that asymmetry was about to
be demonstrated on the walls of Normandy — twice over, at two walls in
particular, and it is those two walls that carry this chapter's proof.

## IV.

The proof this chapter rests on is a pair of controlled comparisons that
the war itself was kind enough to run. Twice in the space of a
generation, armies besieged the same walls — the same circuits of stone,
neither substantially rebuilt in the interval — and the durations can be
set side by side. Everything else about the campaigns differed: the
besieger, the garrison, the politics, the weather of the war. The walls
are the constant. What changed is what the comparison isolates.

The campaign that supplies the comparisons opened, fittingly, in the
old style. The truce of Tours was already dying when, in May 1449, a
French force took Pont-de-l'Arche by a merchant's-cart ruse — an
ambush at the gate, the captain Fauconberg taken at breakfast, not a
gun in sight; in July the town of Verneuil was betrayed by a miller
with a grievance; in August, after the formal rupture at the end of
July, Pont-Audemer fell to assault and Lisieux was delivered by its
own bishop, whose negotiated composition — the Somerset list records
it in a single line — carried with it Orbec, Courtonne, Auvilliers,
Crèvecœur, Le Breul, Fauguernon and a string of places toward
Argentan that no French soldier had approached [^7-8]. Ruse, treachery, clerical politics: these were the
war's ancient modes of taking towns, and their prominence in the
opening weeks is a caution the chapter accepts before it argues
anything — much of what fell in 1449–50 fell as towns had always
fallen. Even the field operations of that autumn read as the old war.
When Talbot manoeuvred against the French near Harcourt, the Berry
Herald describes him fortifying his position "behind hedges and some
waggons… for the conveyance of his provisions and artillery," standing
off his enemies through the daylight and withdrawing into the castle
by night [^7-9] — though the reader
may note, in passing, what even Talbot's baggage now carried, and what
the herald now thought worth listing beside the food. The new
instrument enters the record not with a fanfare but as a line in the
inventory. Where it enters with effect is at the walls that did not
open themselves — and that is where the comparisons begin.

Take Rouen first, because the brief rightly makes it mandatory. In July
1418 Henry V — no improviser, commanding the most experienced siege army
England ever sent to France — sat down before the city and began the
investment. The city fell in January 1419: five months and three weeks
of blockade, and the fall, when it came, was starvation's work, attended
by the episode that has stained the siege's memory ever since — the
expelled *bouches inutiles*, perhaps twelve thousand of them, dying in
the ditch between the lines through the winter because neither side
would feed them [GAP: the 1418–19 detail is carried at tertiary level
citing Barker; upgrade to Barker's *Conquest* at scholarly level before
print]. That was what taking Rouen cost the greatest siege commander of
the age: half a year, and a moral wound that chroniclers on both sides
recorded.

In October 1449 a French army appeared before the same walls. On 16
October an assault was repelled — the garrison was competent and the
walls were sound. On 19 October, while the garrison heard mass, the
townsmen opened a gate. On 29 October the English capitulated on terms;
in the first days of November the castle and palace were handed over;
on 10 November Charles VII made his ceremonial entry by the Porte
Beauvoisine, the Berry Herald riding in the procession he would later
describe [^7-10]. Investment to
capitulation: some three weeks. And the terms tell us more than the
duration. Somerset's garrison was not massacred through a breach; it
negotiated its way out, at the price of 50,000 écus, a clutch of
hostages — the greatest of them John Talbot, earl of Shrewsbury — and
the contracted delivery of a whole cluster of subsidiary places: Arques,
Caudebec, Tancarville, Montivilliers, Lillebonne, the Harfleur–Honfleur
estuary forts [^7-11]. One siege, settled
by composition, was worth half a dozen fortresses that the French never
had to approach. The 1449 instrument was not a sack; it was a
settlement of accounts, and the account was Normandy's.

The sceptic will object, correctly, that Rouen 1449 was decided at the
gate, not the wall — the townsmen's rising, not the train, opened the
city. Hold the objection; it is half the argument, and the chapter will
give it its due. But first observe the second pair, where no rising
intervened and the comparison is cleaner.

Harfleur was where the English war in Normandy had begun. In August and
September 1415 Henry V besieged it for some five weeks — by the usual
account the first major English siege in which gunpowder artillery
played a serious part, and hard-won at that; the
town's resistance cost him a sick army and shaped the desperate march
that ended, by accident, at Agincourt. When the English themselves had
to retake Harfleur in 1440, the operation took somewhere between three
and six months, depending on which chronicler one credits [^7-12]. In December 1449 the Bureau train arrived before the same
town, emplaced sixteen large bombards, and battered it for seventeen
days, whereupon the garrison made terms; the Somerset list dates the
surrender "the first day of the year," 1 January 1450 [^7-13]. Five weeks
against the town's stores and disease in 1415; a season or more in
1440; seventeen days of bombardment in 1449. Same walls. The
seventeen-day figure, note, is not a collapse of the defence's will
alone: the garrison tried the sword first. When the French attempted an
escalade, Talbot's namesake garrison threw it back — the Berry Herald
describes the French knights driven bodily off the wall [^7-14]. Storming failed at Harfleur; the guns did not.

And here the chronicle hands the argument its own language, in a
sentence this book could not improve upon. Robert Blondel, the Norman
Latinist who wrote the reconquest's official-minded narrative within a
few years of the events, describes what the seventeen days of battery
actually did: "crebris fulminibus repetita tormentorum improbitas
fortalitii *moenia et clausorum animos* a pede in verticem labefactat" —
the relentless guns, thundering again and again, shook *the walls and
the minds of the besieged* from foot to summit [TRANS. CLAUDE]
[^7-15]. Walls and minds in a single clause,
governed by a single verb. The fifteen hundred defenders, "obsidionis
acerbitate victi" — beaten by the harshness of the siege, not through
any breach — opened the gates on terms that let them keep their goods
and their liberty. Blondel was not writing military theory. He was
describing what he understood to have happened, and what he understood
to have happened is the mechanism this chapter is arguing for.

The pattern repeats at the next fortress down the estuary with almost
diagrammatic clarity. Honfleur was reputed "velut inexpugnabile" — as
if impregnable — and Blondel says its proud spirit was tamed by "the
irresistible thunder of the guns" ("ineluctabile tormentorum fulmen
elatum ejus animum domat" [TRANS. CLAUDE]); its captain, Curson, then
"componit" — composed, made terms — and the terms themselves deserve
attention, because they show how routinised the transaction had already
become. Honfleur would be delivered on 18 February 1450 unless the
English relieved it by battle in the open field before that date;
hostages were given for performance [^7-16].
This is the conditional-surrender instrument of the old siege law, but
executed on a new clock: the garrison was no longer selling months, it
was buying weeks. No relief came; the town was handed over on the day.
The composition system, moreover, had teeth beyond the wall in
question: when Curson had earlier refused to deliver Honfleur under
the Rouen capitulation's terms, the legal machinery ran its course and
Talbot — the hostage for that treaty — was formally adjudged Charles's
prisoner, "processu legitime confecto" [^7-17]. These surrenders were not battlefield improvisations. They were
contracts, enforced.

Blondel's Rouen chapters add the mechanism's other face: surrender
propagating ahead of the guns entirely. When the garrison of
Mont-Sainte-Catherine — the abbey-fortress commanding Rouen, which had
anchored Henry V's siege lines a generation earlier — learned that the
city below them had come to terms, they gave up the place at once,
"formidine immoderata territi ne insultu obruantur," terrified beyond
measure of being overwhelmed by storm, bargaining for nothing but
their lives; Charles, "compatiens," moved by their destitution as they
marched out, gave them a hundred livres for the road [^7-18]. No gun had been laid against
them. The fear of what the guns made possible — the breach, and behind
the breach the law of war's licensed sack — did the train's work at
one remove. At Valognes, in the Cotentin, the chronicler compresses
the whole transaction into a phrase: the town endured the guns'
"intolerabili tormentorum fragore," their intolerable din, only
briefly before its lieutenant swore himself the French king's man and
the place was given up [^7-19]. The din, note — not the breach. By the campaign's middle
months the sound of the train was functioning as its effect.

One case from the same theatre keeps the argument honest, and the
chapter owes it prominence precisely because it cuts the other way. At
Fougères — the border fortress whose seizure by an English freebooter
had given Charles his casus belli — the duke of Brittany's own siege,
with his own guns, made no such progress. Blondel is unsparing: the
town's solid walls shrugged off the bombardment, "nec satis immanes
machinae ad conterendam tam solidam murorum constructuram" — not even
the huge machines sufficed to crush so solid a construction [TRANS.
CLAUDE] — and what finally forced the issue was not artillery at all
but pestilence in the besieging camp, which killed the count of
Rohan's heir and the duke's own cupbearer and drove the Bretons to
accept a composition partly on the defenders' terms [^7-20]. The lesson is the one this chapter most needs
the reader to carry: guns as such collapsed nothing. A ducal train —
and Brittany's was a serious principality's serious establishment —
remained what artillery had been for a century: one more slow
instrument among the slow. What collapsed Normandy was a specific
institution operating at a specific scale of finance, maintenance and
technical staffing, and the claims of this chapter attach to that
institution, not to the technology in general. Fougères is the
control case, and it failed.

Where resistance was offered and the guns given time to work, the
durations compress toward the absurd. Bayeux, in May 1450, held for
sixteen days, at the end of which — in the Somerset list's own words —
"the walls being pierced by great cannon," it was "taken by
composition"; Rogers's parallel source has "almost the entire wall…
'pierced and brought down'" [^7-21].
Avranches, in June, the same sequence in fifteen days: walls pierced by
cannon, surrender by composition. Caen, the greatest prize after Rouen,
was invested on 5 June by four converging corps — perhaps fifteen to
twenty thousand men — while the artillery of "Gaspard and Jean Bureau,"
for once both brothers named in the tradition, together with sapping,
brought down sections of wall including a great corner tower; the
treaty was sealed on 24 June and the keys handed over on 1 July.
Falaise made terms in about two weeks, its surrender conditioned — a
detail that measures how completely the political and military
registers had merged — on Talbot's release from his Rouen hostageship
[^7-22]. That transaction deserves a paragraph of its own,
because it displays the composition system operating as a complete
legal order. Talbot had stood hostage since October 1449 for the
performance of Rouen's capitulation; when Curson's refusal at Honfleur
breached the pact, the process ran and the earl was adjudged the
French king's prisoner; and in July 1450 his liberty became a
negotiable term in another town's surrender — released on conditions,
among them, the tradition records, an obligation to pilgrimage in
Rome's jubilee year [^7-23]. The most feared soldier in the English establishment spent
the campaign's decisive year not on a wall but inside the surrender
instruments' clause structure — pledged, forfeited, adjudicated,
redeemed. And the system's last irony was still to come: the man the
compositions had processed as a chattel would return in 1452 as the
armed answer to them, and die at Castillon proving their point. Domfront, one of the strongest keeps in Normandy, went
in about twenty days in August, "after great assaults… taken by siege"
— the nearest thing to a force-taken place in the whole census, and
even there no sack is recorded. And Cherbourg, the last English toehold,
fell on 12 August 1450 after roughly five and a half weeks — the
longest resistance of the entire campaign. Let the significance of
that superlative register: the *maximum* duration any fortress achieved
against the train in 1449–50 was shorter than the *typical* duration of
a serious pre-artillery siege. The next year, in Gascony, even the
records fell: Blaye's walls were "completely thrown down in many
places" in five days [^7-24].

A century of stone was repriced in a season. But the repricing, as the
Somerset list has already warned us, was mostly not executed at the
wall. It was executed in the minds of men reading news.

## V.

Set the full census out plainly, because the book's discipline requires
it and because the distribution is itself the finding. Of the hundred
strongpoints in the Somerset list, the great majority fell without a
siege: by composition, by abandonment "for fear of the enemies," by
treason, by the act of their own townsmen or clergy — Lisieux delivered
by its bishop, Coutances "by the churchmen," Verneuil's town betrayed by
a miller, Rouen opened by its citizens. The contested falls held days.
The two documented artillery breaches ended, like everything else, in
composition. "Most of the one hundred strongpoints referred to," Rogers
writes, "were actually never besieged; they surrendered rather than
make a hopeless attempt to resist" [^7-25]. Anne Curry, the
leading specialist of Lancastrian Normandy and no friend of
revolution-talk, states the same distribution from the other side:
"More commonly in the Reconquest, the French *preparations* for
bombardment and assault, rather than actual bombardment, were enough to
prompt surrender" [^7-26]. Her account of the campaign's
shape is the one this chapter adopts: there was "no siege as there had
been in 1418–19. Each place was picked off, one by one, but the fall
of the major places led to a domino effect" — and she is careful to
add the concession that keeps the two halves of the scholarship
joined: "That does not deny that the guns used in 1449–50 were more
powerful than those in 1417–19, as Rogers and others have shown"
[^7-27]. Stronger guns, fewer sieges: the two findings sit
together only if the guns were working on something other than
masonry.

A naïve reading takes this as deflation: if the fortresses were not
battered down, the guns did not matter. The Berry Herald, who rode
with the royal army and watched the transactions happen, already knew
better. At Verneuil the garrison of the Tour Grise — thirty men —
surrendered by treaty to the king's negotiators although, in the
Herald's own words as his English translator rendered them, the tower
"was impregnable so long as there was food within" ("la dite tour
estoit imprenable tant quil y eust en dedens que mengier") [^7-28]. Read that sentence twice, because the
entire argument of this chapter sits inside it. The place was
impregnable, by the standards under which every wall in Europe had been
financed — and it surrendered anyway. Impregnability had been a
statement about time: the tower could not be taken *before hunger*, and
hunger was months away, and months were what the defence was selling.
What the train destroyed was not the tower. It was the market for
months. Even where the sword was tried and succeeded, the record bends
back to the same instrument: at Saint-James-de-Beuvron the assault ran
from nine in the morning until night, and on the morrow the garrison
surrendered the fortress "their lives and goods being saved" [^7-29] — a day's storming, concluded as a
composition. The forms of violence varied; the form of the ending
almost never did.

This is the moment to give the counter-arguments their full weight,
because there are three, each with genuine evidence behind it, and the
chapter's claim survives only if it can absorb rather than dodge them.

The first says: France had recovered, and the reconquest was won by
men, not guns. True as far as it goes. The armies of 1449–50 were the
product of the 1440s reforms — paid, disciplined, numerous; four corps
converged on Caen; the relief operations around Formigny drew on
thousands of men-at-arms and francs-archers. The train never operated
alone, and the book's own mechanism (Chapter 10 will formalise it)
treats train, standing companies and standing taxation as a single
fiscal object — three line-items of one budget. But manpower cannot
explain the same-wall compressions. Henry V had numbers at Rouen in
1418 and needed six months; the French had numbers at Harfleur in 1449
and needed seventeen days *because the wall failed*. Where resistance
was actually offered, the differential is the guns.

The second says: Normandy fell politically — the gates were opened from
inside. Curry again: "many surrenders were forced on the English by the
local populations… the fall of the major places led to a domino effect"
[^7-30]. Substantially conceded — and absorbed, because the
reputational mechanism *requires* a transmission belt. A threat
collapses resistance only if the threatened believe it, and the belief
ran through Norman townsmen who could do the arithmetic as well as any
captain: why endure a bombardment, and risk the sack that the law of
war licensed after a breach, for a garrison that could not hold once
the guns arrived? The politics and the ballistics were not rival
causes; the politics was the medium through which the ballistics
propagated. And the campaign itself supplies the discriminating test.
If popular disaffection were the sufficient cause, the reconquest
should have stalled where the population was loyal. In 1451 the train
moved into Gascony — English for three centuries, and, in Rogers's
words, "despite the deeply ingrained pro-English sympathies of the
inhabitants," all of Guienne fell rapidly [^7-31]. Fronsac,
"la clef de la Guyenne," the strongest English fortress in the duchy,
surrendered by composition on 15 June 1451 without an assault; Blaye
went in five days; Bordeaux itself treated on 12 June and received
Dunois's army — Jean Bureau riding in the entry — on 30 June, seven
thousand three hundred and twenty French troops in the procession
[^7-32]. The texture of the Gascon
capitulations is worth a moment, because it shows the composition
system operating as a market whose prices everyone now knew. Blaye
and Bourg — "the keys to Bordeaux," in the contemporary judgement
Vale reports — "surrendered cravenly to Dunois," and the terms their
defenders extracted included pensions from Charles VII for, among
others, Gadifer Shorthose, the *English* mayor of Bordeaux, with his
sub-mayor and three jurats [^7-33]. Bayonne, the second city of
the English south-west, held two weeks in August. The first Bordeaux
settlement itself was studiedly generous — privileges confirmed, the
city spared the taxes of the French interior, judicial and minting
concessions in the tradition's account [GAP: the 1451 terms are
carried at tertiary level; upgrade to Vale's *English Gascony* when
retrieved] — because in 1451 the crown was buying a province's
acquiescence, and knew the difference between a price worth paying
and a precedent worth setting. Loyal populations, hostile
politics, same cascade — faster, if anything. And the counter-
experiment ran the following year: in October 1452 Talbot appeared
before Bordeaux with no siege train at all, the city rose for its old
allegiance, and a gate was opened to him on 20 October. Politics
alone, it turns out, could open gates. What politics alone could not
do — as 1453 would demonstrate — was hold walls against the train.
The two Bordeaux reversals, twenty months apart, are as close to a
controlled experiment as the fifteenth century offers: the political
variable operating without the train in 1452, the train operating
against the political variable in 1449–51 and 1453. Each fell to the
other's weapon exactly once.

The third counter-argument says: the defender was bankrupt before the
attacker was armed. The Lancastrian establishment in Normandy had been
cut to some 2,500 men in the truce years; Somerset himself pleaded in
1449 that "there is no place in the kings obeissaunce there purveied
nother in reparations, ordenaunce ne in eny maner artillerie," and
that remedy was possible only at "inestimable costs"; the one relief
force England scraped together sailed short of its establishment
[^7-34]. All true, and the book claims it as its own:
this is Chapter 5's hollowed sovereignty meeting Chapter 6's
artillery, the two blades of the same shears. The fiscal state that
could afford the train faced a fiscal state that could no longer
afford walls, powder or relief — the asymmetry is the mechanism seen
from the losing side. Curry's own summary sentence — "A military
revolution needs a strong, well resourced government to produce it"
[^7-35] — is not a rebuttal of this chapter. It is its
thesis, stated by its most careful sceptic.

So the evidence, taken whole — the same-wall compressions where
fighting happened, the composition census where it did not, the
loyalty test of Gascony, the trainless counter-experiment of 1452, the
exhaustion of the defender — resolves into a single sentence, and it
is the sentence this chapter has been travelling toward. **Artillery
collapsed the defender's time horizon and forced composition; it did
not demolish its way in.** The gun's work was done less at the wall
than in the negotiation the wall could no longer postpone. Rogers's
collapsed horizon and Curry's political cascade are not competing
accounts; they are the same event, read from the ditch and from the
gate. The fortress had been an instrument for buying time, and the
train made time unbuyable — after which every garrison in Normandy
was holding an asset whose value had gone, and the rational act,
place after place, was to sell what remained: possession, in exchange
for lives, goods and terms. The walls did not fall because they were
pushed. They fell because they were priced.

## VI.

Two field battles frame the reconquest, and the argument requires that
they be weighted honestly, because they are not equivalent and the
temptation to make them so has misled a century of popular retelling.

Formigny, 15 April 1450, is the minor key. Sir Thomas Kyriell's relief
force — some four thousand men, nearly three-quarters of them archers,
landed at Cherbourg in March — was intercepted on the Carentan–Bayeux
road by the Count of Clermont's roughly three thousand, with the
Constable Richemont's two thousand Bretons within marching distance.
The English did what English armies had done for a century: took a
prepared defensive position and invited attack. What happened next was
new in one particular only. Two French culverins — light wheeled
pieces, probably breech-loaders, though the sources describe both the
guns and their handling with less precision than one would wish — were
brought up and fired on the line from beyond bowshot.
Blondel names the hand that placed them: Giraud, "regiarum machinarum
unus magistrorum," one of the masters of the royal machines, siting his
"binas colubrinas" against the massed formation [TRANS. CLAUDE]
[^7-36]. The guns did not break the line.
They did something subtler: they made the position untenable to stand
in, and the English — an army whose whole tactical inheritance said
*never leave the position* — charged out and took them. The Berry
Herald records the sequel from the mêlée itself: the English "took from
them… two culverines"; the seneschal of Poitou dismounted his men,
counter-charged, and "recovered the two said culverines," at a cost the
Herald puts at some two hundred English dead in that passage of arms
alone [^7-37]. While the armies were
locked, Richemont's cavalry came in on the flank — drawn to the field,
the tradition has it, by the sound of the firing — and the last English
field army in Normandy was destroyed where it stood. French sources
claimed some 3,700 English dead and upwards of nine hundred prisoners,
Kyriell among them; the figures are victors' figures and are cited as
such.

Formigny, honestly told, is therefore not the battle field artillery
won; it is the battle at which field artillery first *mattered* — the
battle in which two guns broke not the English line but English
discipline, prising a hitherto-unbeaten defensive system out of the
posture that had won Crécy, Poitiers and Agincourt. The kill was
delivered by cavalry and the press of men, as it had been for
centuries. The novelty was the lever, not the hammer. Drafting the
claim any larger than that would hand the specialist reviewer the
easiest rebuttal in the book; drawn at this size, it holds.

Castillon, 17 July 1453, is the major key, and it will bear the weight.
The campaign context matters. Bordeaux's rising of October 1452 had
returned Gascony's capital to England — and the French response began,
characteristically, in a warehouse. Within the month, the record shows
Jean Aubry, lieutenant of Gaspard Bureau, master of the king's
artillery, buying pieces of artillery in Paris and forwarding them to
Rouen [^7-38]: the institution replenishing its Norman magazines
against one contingency while the court digested another, procurement
proceeding by delegated routine rather than royal fiat. It is a small
entry, and its smallness is the point — by 1452 the train was not an
expedition that had to be conjured but an establishment that restocked
itself. Charles VII's answer to the rising, the following summer, was
a convergent invasion — and the opening move, the chronicle is
specific, was the artillery administrator's. Mathieu
d'Escouchy, whose account of the Gascon campaign is the fullest we
have, records the royal council of war resolving its strategy "par
l'advis… de maistre Jehan Burreau, chevalier, seigneur de Monglat":
besiege Castillon first, Bureau counselled, because "qui porroit avoir
ladicte ville de Chasteillon… on averoit ladicte rivière pour luy" —
whoever held Castillon held the river [TRANS. CLAUDE] — and "a ce faire
se conclud tout le conseil" [^7-39]. Note what has happened
to the office. The master of the artillery is no longer a technician
summoned to serve the plan; he is the counsellor whose logistical
geography *is* the plan, and the whole council concludes on his advice.
The campaign's design now began from where the guns could be supplied
and what the river could carry.

The force sent against Castillon — Escouchy gives three thousand
men-at-arms with archers in proportion, under Boussac, Joachim Rouault
and Bureau himself — did not assault the town. It dug. The famous camp
went in beside the Lidoire: a park fortified with ditch and palisade on
three sides and the river on the fourth, the guns — perhaps two to
three hundred pieces of all sizes; the count is not settled and this
book will not settle it — sited to sweep the approaches; eight hundred
francs-archers pushed forward into an abbey nearer the town [^7-40]. It was, in effect, a fortress improvised in
days by men who had spent four years demonstrating that fortresses
could no longer be defended — with this difference, that its garrison
wanted to be attacked.

Talbot came because Bordeaux made him. He was around sixty-six years
old, the most feared English captain of the war's last generation — *le
roi Talbot*, the French called him — and he had given the Bordelais his
word, when they opened their gate to him, that he would meet the French
power in the field. Escouchy shows the burgesses pressing that promise
back on him almost word for word, and Talbot answering coldly that he
would keep it "quant je verray que temps et heure sera" — when he
judged the time and hour right [TRANS. CLAUDE] [^7-41].
The time was chosen for him. At dawn on the day of battle his advance
guard surprised the francs-archers in the abbey and cut its way
through them; the survivors fell back on the park. Then came the
report on which the day turned: a messenger told Talbot that the
French were abandoning their camp and streaming away — "les Franchois
habandonnent leur parc et s'enfuyent; il est heure ou jamais" [TRANS.
CLAUDE]. Escouchy, who tells the story as a mirror-for-princes, has
Talbot break off the mass he was hearing with the words that "never
shall I hear the mass, or this day I shall have overthrown the company
of the French who are in that park before me" [TRANS. CLAUDE]
[^7-42]. The report was false — what was moving was
camp-followers, not the army [GAP: the identity of the movers is
carried in the tradition, not pinned in the parsed passage; verify in
Escouchy ii. before print]. As the English column approached, an old
gentleman of the English nation, "qui avoit beaucoup veu des fais de
la guerre," told Talbot plainly that the report was untrue, that the
French stood in strength behind their works, and that he would gain
nothing that day; Talbot answered the last man to tell him the truth
with a sword-stroke across the face, and rode on [^7-43].

The defenders "afusterrent leur artillerie au lez devers dont véoient
venir ledit Talbot" — trained their guns on the side from which they
saw him coming [TRANS. CLAUDE] [^7-44]. The Anglo-Gascon
army attacked the park frontally, banners forward, trumpets sounding,
the men crying "Talbot! Talbot! Saint-George!" — a feudal host doing
the bravest thing it knew how to do, into massed artillery firing from
prepared positions at close range. It was destroyed in an afternoon.
Talbot's horse was brought down by a round; he was killed on the
ground — by whose hand, the sources disagree and this book declines to
choose; his son Lord Lisle died with him. The town of Castillon
surrendered two days later under the continuing fire. What died on the
field is easy to name and easier to over-name: not chivalry, which had
been dying of other causes for a century, but the assumption that
valour could still, on its own, carry a decision. The last great
captain of the old
war charged the first great instrument of the new one, on the advice
of a false report and against the advice of a true one, and the
instrument did not care.

[BOOK'S ARGUMENT — the reading that follows is the book's own.]
Before leaving Talbot on the field, the record permits — and its
gaps invite — one further reading, offered as hypothesis and tagged
as such. Pollard's considered judgement of the man should be stated
first, because it forbids the easy version: Talbot was probably
England's ablest commander of the war's last generation, his gifts
lying in operational tempo — raid, surprise, relief, siege and
counter-siege — rather than set-piece battle; the blanket charge of
impetuosity, Basin's and the older tradition's, founders on a
career of battles prudently refused; and Castillon itself Pollard
reads as honour-bound error rather than habitual recklessness,
while insisting that the exact battlefield sequence remains
uncertain [^7-45]. That uncertainty is the
void the hypothesis lives in: no document records Talbot's intent;
the command group that could have testified died with him; and
Escouchy's account — our fullest — is a moral narrative, built to
teach princes about rash credulity. What the record does establish
is that no commander in Europe knew the train's effect more
intimately: Talbot had passed through the Rouen instrument as its
hostage, his own liberty a clause in the compositions the train
compelled, and had stood on Harfleur's wall [^7-46]; and the one documented English tactical
reflex against field artillery was seizure — the archers at
Formigny, earlier in this section, took the culverins rather than
endure them. A train in park, entrenched, is unassailable; a train
limbering to move is, for one hour, capturable — and a commander
of Talbot's formation, told the French were abandoning their park,
might read "il est heure ou jamais" not as bravado but as tactical
literalism: now or never, because the war's decisive instrument
was briefly loose. The Bordeaux promise then supplies what the
hypothesis needs last — the commitment pressure that held him to
the attack after the report was contradicted in his sight. The
record cannot decide between folly and a last rational lunge at
the instrument that had ended his war; the book does not pretend
it can. But under either reading, Castillon testifies to the same
fact: the train was understood, by both sides, as the thing worth
dying for.

The mopping-up of 1453 completes the evidentiary set, because it
contains both the census's one genuine exception and its most
instructive conclusion. Cadillac, held against the king after the
revolt, is the only place in the whole record taken by storm through a
breach — and the exception proves the rule with almost pedantic care,
because the storm was manufactured by the train first: Escouchy
describes the biggest bombards of the Bordeaux siege deliberately laid
to fire "tous ensamble et à une fois" — all together, in a single
discharge [TRANS. CLAUDE] — against the outwork, then the massed guns
turned on the gate-tower and wall until the rubble filled great
stretches of the moat, the attackers each carrying a faggot to the
ditch, and only then the assault cry [^7-47]. Even the
one storming in the record is a bombardment wearing an assault's
clothes; and it was reserved for a place under punitive terms, whose
captain the king had executed [GAP-flag: Vale's TRHS article dates the
execution 17 September, Escouchy the storm Wednesday 19 September —
the discrepancy is recorded in the evidence file, unresolved]. Bordeaux
itself, blockaded by land and river — sixteen ships from La Rochelle
cleared its naval outwork on 8 October, closing the river Bureau's
Castillon advice had been about — treated at Lormont and Montferrand
through October. Escouchy preserves the negotiation in detail: the
burgesses' offer of a hundred thousand écus; the king's council
weighing, among its reasons for accepting composition, "le dangier de
l'epedimie" — the plague then running through the region and no
respecter of besieging armies [TRANS. CLAUDE] [^7-48].
The terms were the mirror of 1451's. Where the first surrender had
confirmed the city's privileges, the second stripped them: lives
spared, the ringleaders banished perpetually, the English shipped
home — and Bordeaux renouncing "tous leurs previleiges et franchises,
pour en faire au bon plaisir du Roy," all its privileges and
franchises, to be disposed at the king's good pleasure [TRANS. CLAUDE]
[^7-49]. Twelve hostages — six English,
six Gascon — secured performance from 12 October; an internal dispute
in the city deferred the handover; and French troops entered on 19
October 1453. The Hundred Years War, by the convention historians
observe, ended there, leaving Calais as England's last continental
holding. Even then the composition system showed its edges: the
garrisons of Benauges and Rions, included in the treaty's provisions,
refused point-blank to honour them and had to be dealt with
separately [^7-50] — a reminder, recorded by the same
witness, that a surrender instrument bound only those who believed
the alternative was worse, which is to say that it was, first and
last, an instrument of the train's credibility.

Set the two Bordeaux settlements side by side and the composition
instrument reveals what it had become. In 1451, with the cascade still
running and loyalty untested, the crown bought the city with
generosity — privileges confirmed, exactions forsworn. In 1453, with
the train outside the walls and the revolt to punish, the same
instrument executed a constitutional demotion: the most privileged
city in the Atlantic trade signed itself into the king's discretion.
Surrender-on-terms, iterated, was doing what no storm could have done —
converting military supremacy into legal subordination, city by city,
clause by clause. The composition was where the artillery state's
violence was translated into its law.

## VII.

A claim this load-bearing must now face its strongest objection, and the
objection comes, inconveniently, from the historian this chapter has
cited more than any other. Clifford Rogers does not think the artillery
revolution was the great rupture of late-medieval war. In his
reconstruction, the deeper break came a century earlier: the Infantry
Revolution of the fourteenth century, when common pikemen and archers —
at Courtrai, at Bannockburn, at Morgarten, above all in the English
longbow victories of Crécy and Poitiers — overturned the centuries-long
dominance of aristocratic shock cavalry. On that account the Bureau
guns are the second movement of a longer symphony, and a chapter that
isolates 1450 as *the* hinge has mistaken one movement for the score.
Worse, the infantry revolution appears to run the other way: cheap,
disciplined foot beating expensive chivalry is capability moving *down*
the wealth gradient — dispersal, not concentration. If the century's
story opened with common men humbling costly cavalry, why read its
close as the capital-intensification of decisive force?

The chapter's answer is to concede the premise and keep the
conclusion. The infantry revolution was real, and it was
constitutionally *dispersive* — which is exactly why Part I's medieval
order looked as it did. Disciplined infantry could be raised by
anybody with a valley or a town: the Swiss cantons raised it, the
English indentured retinue packaged it, the Italian *condottieri*
companies sold it on the open market — this is Chapters 3 and 4's
material, and it is precisely the military underpinning of the
dispersal the book began with. A capability any subject can raise
does not concentrate sovereignty. The infantry revolution is
therefore not a rival explanation of the consolidation; it is part of
what the consolidation had to overcome — the problem to which the
artillery revolution was the answer. The distinction that carries the
argument is affordability, and the 1442 accounts have already given
it a number: a first-rate siege train cost more than a government's
entire traditional arsenal, and the cost recurred — powder, wages,
carriage, foundry — in a way no campaign-season improvisation could
meet. Infantry was a capability; the train was an *establishment*. And
here the objection folds into testimony, because Rogers himself
supplies the constitutional consequence: the central governments of
large states could afford siege trains; "their subjects and smaller
neighbors, in general, could not" — and the governments that acquired
them suppressed regional powers, raised taxes, and expanded the trains
further, in a cycle he describes and this book merely names [^7-51]. The disagreement that remains is a ranking of revolutions —
which movement of the symphony matters most — and the chapter declines
the contest, because it does not need to win it. It needs only the
narrower claim its sternest witness has already granted: that the
artillery revolution was the step which put the price of decisive
force above what any subject could pay. (One apparent counter-example
— the magnificent Burgundian train of Charles the Bold — is deferred
to Chapter 10, where it belongs; the short answer is that Burgundy
was not a subject but a rival consolidator, and that its train did
not save it.)

The gradualist objection deserves the same respect. Curry has warned
that "the military revolution has been a moveable feast" [^7-52] — a concept stretched over so many centuries and so many
candidate technologies that its explanatory edge dulls; Rogers himself
notes how quickly the idea became "the 'new orthodoxy'" [^7-53]. Her reading of 1449–50, as we have seen, distributes the
causation across money, numbers, command, English exhaustion and
Norman politics, and this chapter has adopted more of that reading
than it has resisted. But the book's claim was never the maximal one.
It does not require that tactics changed overnight, nor that the
reconquest be monocausal. It requires two facts, both documentary:
that the price of decisive siege capability moved sharply within a
datable window, and that the price sat above what subjects could pay.
The 1442 procurement programme dates the first; the second is the
census of who, in the event, fielded such a train — a list with one
entry. Everything else in the campaign — the politics, the
exhaustion, the disaffection — describes how the asymmetry propagated,
not whether it existed.

[BRIDGE — the passage from here to the next bracket is the analogy
joint; the disanalogy is stated per §6, and the whole belongs to
Roderick's rewrite.] Two modern collapses are worth holding beside
Normandy for one property only. In May 1940 the German offensive in
the west opened on the 10th; Paris fell on 14 June; the armistice was
signed on 22 June — a continental military order, rated by its
contemporaries the strongest in Europe, gone in six weeks. In August
2021 the first Afghan provincial capital fell on the 6th; on the 10th,
American intelligence assessed that Kabul might hold for thirty to
ninety days; it fell on the 15th. Neither was an artillery
revolution. Blitzkrieg was an organisational and doctrinal shift
operating on armies, not walls; the Taliban advance was a political
collapse in which hardly a shot needed firing; and the book's
mechanism — capital-intensity repricing decisive force — is not
claimed for either. What the three cases share is narrower and
temporal: in each, a defensive order that had appeared stable weeks
earlier dissolved faster than the polity holding it could process,
because the assumption on which every local decision to resist
depended — that resistance bought time, and that time bought options —
failed everywhere at once. The garrisons of Normandy in 1450, the
French staff in 1940 and the Afghan provincial governors in 2021 were
not cowards; they were holders of an asset whose time value had gone
to zero between one season and the next, each watching the others
mark it down. The parallel is offered for the tempo, and for nothing
else. [END BRIDGE]

What the tempo teaches is the chapter's bequest to the rest of the
book. The reconquest looked, to those inside it, like weather — a
cascade with no author. But the cascade had a cause with a budget
line. The train that collapsed the time value of fortification was
not deployable by great magnates, because great magnates could not
afford it; it presupposed the *taille* levied on a kingdom and a
salaried technical corps to serve it — fiscal and administrative
capacities that had existed before the guns and had not, before the
guns, been decisive. Artillery made them decisive. The contemporaries
saw the coupling plainly enough: Charles VII, in DeVries's summary of
the reconquest's finance, "increased the royal budget to procure a
larger number of new guns, and he added heavier new taxes to his
kingdom's inhabitants to pay for this increase" [^7-54] —
guns requiring taxes, taxes requiring the administration to assess
and collect them, the administration protected by the guns. Rogers
describes the same figure as a cycle: governments that acquired
artillery suppressed regional powers, raised revenue from the
territory thereby subdued, and spent the revenue expanding the train
and the army that marched with it [^7-55]. A flywheel of this
kind, once spinning, does not stop at a frontier, and Chapter 10
will follow it into the institutions it built. That inversion —
the moment at which the sinews of administration became the sinews of
war, rather than the reverse — is Chapter 10's subject, and the
reader now holds its premise: after 1450, sovereignty had a minimum
price, denominated in guns, wages and taxes, and the only entities
that could pay it were the ones we would shortly begin calling
states.

There is, however, a nearer consequence, and it sailed for England in
the ships that carried the compositions' beneficiaries home. The
garrisons that signed at Rouen and Caen and Cherbourg marched out
with their lives and goods, as the instruments provided — thousands
of professional soldiers of a defeated establishment, landed through
the summer of 1450 into the ports of Kent and Sussex, unpaid,
unabsorbed, and carrying with them the practical knowledge of how
quickly an order that cannot meet the price of force comes apart. The
kingdom that received them had spent thirty years financing a war on
expedients and had just watched the investment liquidated in a
season. Within weeks of Formigny, the men of Kent were in arms behind
a captain who called himself Mortimer, and the bill for Normandy was
presented — not in Rouen, but in London. What a polity does when that
bill arrives, and what it means that England's answer was delivered
by an armed crowd quoting the language of reform, is the next
chapter's business.

# Chapter 7 — Cade and the Communes

## I.

The bill for Normandy was presented in the first week of June 1450, on
the high ground at Blackheath where the London road crosses the heath,
in the form of an armed encampment of some five thousand men of Kent.

They did not call themselves rebels. They were, by their own document,
the "poor commons of Kent," and their captain — a man of obscure origin
who gave his name as John Mortimer, and whom the government would
insist, then and after, was an Irish adventurer named Jack Cade — styled
himself Captain of Kent. The men behind him had not gathered as a mob
gathers. The county had assembled itself: summoned hundred by hundred
through the same constable-and-muster machinery by which the crown
itself raised Kent for war, the posse of the shire called out by its
own locally chosen officers — against, as it turned out, the men who
normally did the calling (Bohna 2003, at abstract level: the rising
mobilised through "the traditional structure of the posse comitatus or
militia under the command of locally-elected constables" [GAP: the
article's full text and exact phrasing await retrieval]). Among the
thousands who would later sue out pardons were a knight, two members of
Parliament, eighteen squires, gentlemen in numbers, yeomen,
husbandmen, craftsmen, village constables — the office-holding,
property-holding middle of one of England's richest counties [^8-1]. Kriehn, who worked through the pardon lists in the 1890s and
counted "about 2203 names of the men of Kent and Sussex alone," put
the finding in a sentence the modern scholarship has refined but not
reversed: "Cade's rebels were not drawn from the lower classes alone,
but from the middle classes, the gentry and even from the nobility"
[^8-2].

That composition is this chapter's subject, because it recurs. The
neomedieval literature this book builds on has next to nothing to say
about popular politics — its dramatis personae are states, firms,
churches and networks, and the crowd enters, if at all, as
atmosphere. This is a serious omission, because the crisis of a
sovereign order is never experienced only in council chambers and
treasuries; it arrives in parishes, in tax assessments, in the price
of justice at the county sessions — and the people it arrives among
respond in patterned ways. The pattern this chapter traces across
four polities and eighty years is what the book will call the
*propertied-anxious revolt*: a rising led and manned not by the
destitute but by the middling sort with something to lose; framing
its demands as restoration of an older, better constitution rather
than revolution; aiming its grievance at fiscal reach and corrupt
administration; and ending, not in victory or annihilation, but in
absorption — selective concession, exemplary punishment, and a
settlement that proceeds over its head. The form appears wherever the
emerging fiscal-military state presses on populations organised
enough to answer, and the book's wager chapters will need it: because
the same form, this chapter will argue, is visible now, and its
modern instances are as unlikely to prevent the coming consolidation
as their ancestors were to prevent the last one.

## II.

Take the type-case first, in its own words and its own three weeks.

The rising's context is the crisis year the reader already holds from
Chapters 5 and 6: the king's government bankrupt and Normandy falling
— Bishop Moleyns lynched by unpaid soldiers at Portsmouth in January
1450; Suffolk, the regime's head, impeached, exiled and murdered at
sea on 2 May; Bishop Aiscough, the king's confessor, dragged from
mass and killed in June. Watts's account of the court regime of
1445–50 supplies the structural background: a household government
concentrating access and patronage in a narrow circle around a king
who could not himself govern, and therefore unable to legitimate
itself across the territorial interests it taxed and commanded
[^8-3]. Kent, the county through which the wreckage of the
French war physically arrived — the truce-era discharges of 1446–48,
some two thousand soldiers put out of employment, many of whom, in
Curry's summary, "took to living off the land" [^8-4],
followed by the refugee flow from Rouen through the Kentish ports —
had particular reasons to feel the general failure, and particular
men to articulate it. The evacuation of the Norman garrisons proper,
it should be said at once, post-dates the rising: Caen's garrison
embarked only after 1 July, Cherbourg fell on 12 August, and that
second wave fed the county's continued unrest into 1451 rather than
the rising itself. The veterans at Blackheath were the discharged and
the displaced, not the just-evacuated; the distinction matters
because it shows the rising as a response to the *long* failure of
the war state, not a spasm at the news of its end (ch07 probe §3;
ch06 chronology).

A note on the evidentiary base, because the composition claim carries
weight and its documents have shapes. What we know of who marched
comes principally from two record series, and they sample the rising
differently: the pardon rolls, from which Kriehn's 2,203 names come,
capture those with enough standing and property to think a pardon
worth suing out; the King's Bench indictments for Kent, edited by
Virgoe and not yet in this book's corpus, capture those the crown
thought worth prosecuting [GAP: Virgoe, Kent Records XVIII]. Both
windows are biased, and biased in opposite directions — toward the
respectable in the one case, toward the notable in the other — and
when both are in hand they must be presented as two biased windows,
not one population. What can be said now, and it is the probe file's
disciplined formulation, is that the propertied and office-holding
character of the rising is attested by the window *least* likely to
flatter it: even the crown's own machinery of mercy processed the
host as a county of householders, not a rabble. The Shakespearean
Cade — the butcher's carnival, the heads on pikes exchanging kisses
— is Tudor memory doing political work on a rising whose actual
documents read like a grand jury presentment.

What the commons wanted is not a matter of inference, because their
manifesto survives. The *Complaint of the Poor Commons of Kent* is a
document of grievances that a modern reader will find startling in
its sobriety. The king, it
says, is surrounded by counsellors "fals and of nowght browght up" —
false, and raised from nothing — who have taught him that he is
"a bove his lawys," above his own laws; justice cannot be had "with
out bribes"; the crown's revenue has been given away until the king
"may not pay for his mete nor drynke"; and abroad, in the document's
unsparing inventory, "his marchandyse is lost… the see is lost,
Fraunce is lost" (*Complaint of the Poor Commons of Kent*, in Stow's
*Three Fifteenth-Century Chronicles*; quoted from the open
transcription). It names names — the Kentish extortioners Slegge,
Crowmer, Isle and Est, the local machinery of assessment and
office-holding turned predatory — and its remedy is the oldest in
the constitutional repertoire: that the king remove the false men
and take about him "the trewe lordys of his royall blode," the Duke
of York named first among them. Read the whole document and one
demand is conspicuous by its absence: there is no article against
the king. Deposition is not asked for, nor a new constitution, nor
any levelling of degree. The commons of Kent, in arms, demanded that
the fifteenth century work as it was supposed to work. The genuinely
revolutionary voice of 1450 belongs elsewhere — to the small Sussex
risings around the Merfolds that autumn, where men reportedly did
speak of killing the king — and the contrast is the point: the main
body of the movement, the propertied county in arms, was
restorationist to its bones.

The three weeks ran as such risings run. A royal force sent to
disperse the camp was ambushed and destroyed at Sevenoaks on 18
June, its commanders killed; the court's nerve failed; and on 3
July the rebels entered London, where the propertied city at first
received them. Lord Saye and Sele — the Lord Treasurer, the
Complaint's chief named villain — was taken from the Tower, given a
trial whose verdict preceded its process, and beheaded, with his
son-in-law Crowmer, ex-sheriff of Kent, on 3–4 July. And then the
form found its limit: the discipline of the host broke into
plunder, the city that had opened its gates changed its mind, and
on the night of 5–6 July the Londoners and the Tower garrison
fought the commons to a standstill on London Bridge. The government,
which could not defeat the rising, dissolved it instead: Bishop
Waynflete's negotiated pardons emptied the camp within days. Cade's
own pardon, issued to "John Mortimer," was void by its own fiction;
he was hunted, taken mortally wounded near Heathfield about 12 July,
and died on the cart to London. The judicial aftermath — the
"harvest of heads," in the contemporary phrase of Gregory's
Chronicle — followed through the winter, the king personally
presiding at the Canterbury sessions. Institutionally, essentially
nothing was conceded. Politically, everything the Complaint alleged
became the common coin of the decade: York's party would spend the
1450s prosecuting, in Parliament and then in the field, an
indictment the commons of Kent had drafted first.

Cade, then, in the pattern's terms: middling and office-holding
composition; restorationist demands, documented from the rebels' own
text; grievance fixed on fiscal predation and corrupted
administration; and the outcome — pardon, exemplary repression,
zero concession, total absorption of the programme by the elite
opposition. The rising shaped the politics of the decade profoundly.
It redirected the consolidation of the English state by not one
degree.

## III.

Cross the Channel and wind back ten years, and the same form appears
in its purest variant — purest, because the grievance was not
misgovernment in general but the fiscal-military state in
particular, named and resisted at the moment of its creation.

In November 1439 the Estates at Orléans produced, and Charles VII
promulgated, the ordonnance this book's Chapter 10 will treat as a
founding document: private lords forbidden to raise or maintain
troops without royal licence, captaincies reserved to royal
appointment, and the taille established as an effectively permanent
tax to pay for the king's soldiers (ordonnance of 2 November 1439).
Every clause cut something away from somebody. The great princes
lost their private armies; the free captains — the écorcheur
entrepreneurs of Chapter 3's world — lost their market; and the
taxpaying country acquired a permanent obligation where custom had
known occasional grants. Within three months the reaction was in
the field: the Praguerie of February–July 1440 — named, with the
century's gift for insult, after the Hussite risings of Prague —
brought together the dukes of Bourbon and Alençon, the disgraced
favourite La Trémoille, and a scatter of mercenary captains "who
felt their livelihood threatened," behind the figurehead of the
sixteen-year-old Dauphin Louis. It was a revolt of the over-mighty
against the machinery being built to end them, with enough popular
and professional grievance attached to give it a country. Charles
VII and the Constable Richemont broke it in five months. And the
settlement, at Cusset in July 1440, is a small masterpiece of the
absorption technique this chapter is cataloguing: pardons all
round, a pension for Bourbon, the government of the Dauphiné for
Louis — generosity precisely calibrated to detach the leadership —
and not one clause of the ordonnance withdrawn. The ordonnance was
thereafter enforced; the compagnies d'ordonnance followed in 1445;
the taille outlived every man who rode against it [GAP: the
scholarly account of the Praguerie awaits Contamine's chapters; the
sequence above is the consensus record].

The Praguerie deserves its place beside Cade because it removes an
ambiguity. Cade's commons rose against a failing state, and a critic
may fairly ask whether their revolt belongs in a book about
consolidation at all — Kent in 1450 was not resisting an artillery
state; it was demanding that a hollow one function. The objection is
fair and the answer is in the sequence. The grievances the commons
enumerated — the purveyance, the extortionate assessments, the sale
of justice, the household men enriched from the crown's shrinking
estate — are the pathologies of a war-finance apparatus that had
been extracting at state-building intensity for thirty years without
building a state: England had run the fiscal-military experiment's
costs without acquiring its product. The rising was against the
extraction; the extraction was the consolidation's price paid early
and wasted; and when the consolidation arrived in earnest under the
Yorkists and early Tudors, it would meet a population already
schooled — by exactly these grievances — in what it would and would
not bear. Cade belongs in the set as the form's response to
consolidation *mismanaged*. The Praguerie rose against a
*succeeding* state — specifically, by name, against the permanent tax and the
royal monopoly of force that constitute this book's mechanism — and
it lost in the same shape: bought at the top, dispersed at the
bottom, absorbed into a settlement that proceeded. Together the two
cases bracket the form. Whether the emerging order was failing or
succeeding, the propertied-anxious revolt appeared on schedule, made
the same restorationist case, and met the same fate.

## IV.

The Italian instance runs earlier and pinches the pattern from the
other side, and the chapter states at the outset the respect in
which it does not fit: the Ciompi were not the propertied middle.
They were the unguilded wool workers of Florence — of some fourteen
thousand souls in the industry, only about two hundred held guild
membership — the closest thing the fourteenth-century city had to a
proletariat. In the summer of 1378, out of a factional quarrel among
their betters, they rose: the palace of the Signoria was seized on
22 July, the wool-carder Michele di Lando installed as Gonfaloniere,
and by 30 July the revolutionaries had extracted the one concession
no propertied Florentine had ever contemplated — three new guilds,
enfranchising the popolo minuto for the first time in the city's
history. It lasted five weeks. The radical rump was broken in the
Piazza on 31 August; the Ciompi guild was abolished immediately, the
other two by 1382; and the oligarchic reaction that followed —
the Albizzi regime of 1382–1434 — governed Florence until Cosimo
de' Medici's return converted oligarchy into hegemony.

What earns the episode its place here is not its composition but
its legacy — and the chapter uses it for the legacy honestly, rather
than conscripting wool workers into the anxious middle. The memory
of 1378 became a permanent instrument of Florentine politics: the
spectre the propertied classes saw whenever popular politics
stirred, and the standing argument for the concentration of power
that the fifteenth century's signorial drift accomplished. Italian
propertied-anxious politics was, in the interpretive tradition this
chapter follows at flagged level, absorbed rather than defeated by
the emerging signorie — the fear of the commune's floor feeding the
acceptance of a master above its ceiling [GAP: the framing is
Najemy's; his *History of Florence* is on the acquisition list and
the argument must not harden until it is in the repo]. In the
pattern's terms, the Ciompi supply the boundary case: where the
rising came from below the propertied line, it was not absorbed but
annihilated — and its most durable political product was the
strengthened appetite of the propertied for exactly the
consolidation this book tracks.

## V.

The German lands ran the form in series, and with the cleanest
fiscal triggers in the whole set.

Along the Upper Rhine, the Bundschuh conspiracies — named for the
peasant's laced boot on their banners — recurred across a
generation: at Sélestat in 1493, against taxation, debt, arbitrary
justice and the cost of ecclesiastical privilege; at Untergrombach
under Joss Fritz in 1502, whose programme — church property divided,
and "no lord but the Emperor" — compresses the restorationist
paradox into six words, the most radical demand in the set framed as
loyalty to the oldest authority available; at Lehen in 1513 and in
Lower Alsace in 1517, each conspiracy betrayed and suppressed before
rising [^8-5]. In Württemberg in 1514, the
"Armer Konrad" rising gives the pattern its most legible fiscal
mechanics: Duke Ulrich, in debt, imposed new consumption taxes on
meat, wine and grain and adjusted the public weights to sweeten the
yield; the Remstal rose in May; the revolt was suppressed by July;
and the settlement — the Treaty of Tübingen of 1514 — had the
estates assume the ducal debt in exchange for constitutional
rights, the concession-within-consolidation pattern executed in a
single document [^8-6]. The urban
theatres ran on the same clock: Cologne's guild risings of 1481,
1512 and 1513 — the documented cases; the tradition of a wave in
the 1460s and 70s could not be verified and is not asserted here —
contested patrician fiscal administration and were reabsorbed into
adjusted civic constitutions. All of it is prologue: the great
explosion of 1525, the Peasants' War, lies just beyond this
chapter's frame, and Chapter 11 will meet its aftermath. What the
German series establishes is frequency: wherever the intensifying
fiscal state pressed on organised communities — village, valley,
guild — the same revolt formed, with the same middling leadership
(village office-holders and guildsmen, not the destitute), the same
restorationist idiom, and the same ending.

Set the cases in a row and the form's four features stand out like
a watermark. Leadership and mass from the propertied middle —
Cade's pardon rolls, the Praguerie's princes-with-a-country, the
Bundschuh's village office-holders — with the Ciompi as the marked
exception that proves the boundary. Demands framed as restoration:
the Complaint's plea that the king be advised by his true lords;
"no lord but the Emperor"; the estates of Württemberg buying their
duke's debt in exchange for their old rights. Grievance centred on
fiscal reach and administrative corruption — the taille, the tolls,
the weights, the bribes at the sessions. And absorption as the
outcome: selective concession detaching the leaders, exemplary
repression sealing the account, and the consolidation proceeding as
if the revolt had been weather. The propertied-anxious revolt is
not an interruption of state formation. It is one of state
formation's recurring by-products — and, in the concessions it
extracts, one of its shaping tools.

Why this composition, and why this idiom? The structural logic is
worth stating, because it is what makes the form portable across
five centuries. The truly poor rarely make revolts of this kind:
they lack the organisational capital — the offices, the muster
rolls, the guild structures, the literate men to draft a Complaint —
and they lack the stake that converts grievance into programme. The
truly rich do not need to revolt; they are the counsellors, or can
buy them. The revolt of the anxious middle happens because the
middle is where the emerging state's demands and its benefits
diverge most sharply: these are the men who pay the new taxes but
do not receive the new offices, who man the county's administrative
machinery but watch its profits flow to court, whose property is
substantial enough to be assessed and too modest to be exempted. And
the restorationist idiom follows from the same position. Men with
property do not lightly demand new constitutions, because
constitutional novelty endangers title; what they demand is the old
constitution *enforced* — the king counselled by his true lords,
the Emperor sovereign over his predatory intermediaries, the
weights honest and the sessions unbought. The form's conservatism
is not timidity. It is the political expression of collateral.

disanalogies stated: modern populists operate through
universal-franchise elections inside consolidated bureaucratic
states, command no hundred-musters, behead no treasurers; the
"emerging order" they resist is a fiscal-administrative-cultural
reach, not a siege train.

With that fence built, the structural findings are these. The
political-science framework already treats the wave as one
phenomenon: Mudde's thin-centred ideology of the pure people
against the corrupt elite; the Norris–Inglehart cultural-backlash
account, in which support flows from once-dominant groups whose
"hegemonic status, power, and privilege are fading" [^8-7]; Fetzer's austerity findings and Margalit's adjudication
(economic insecurity real but "modest" as sole explanation; the
backlash cultural and economic, interacting). On composition — the
limb of the pattern this book most needs — the reviewed
recalibration governs: the claim is "anxious middle over destitute
bottom," scoped to right-authoritarian populism, and it is carried
by the evidence rather than the vibe: Norris and Inglehart's own
finding that "populist voting was strongest among the petty
bourgeoisie, not unskilled manual workers," with support weakest
among the welfare-dependent; Green and Pahontu's finding that some
73 per cent of Leave voters were homeowners, property functioning
as "self-insurance"; the Trump primary electorate's median
household income of roughly $72,000 against a national median of
roughly $56,000 [^8-8]. The pattern has named
exceptions and the chapter names them: Italy's Five Star Movement
cuts against it — precarity *raised* the odds of M5S support 1.68
times, and the movement ran strongest in the distressed South
[^8-9]; the gilets jaunes complicate it — working and
lower-middle, low-credential, peri-urban; the AfD's class base is a
live scholarly exchange (Lengfeld versus Lux), cited as an
exchange; and Brexit is two-sided in a way the honest version must
state — Leave *rates* ran highest among council tenants and the
C2DE grades even as the *bulk* of the Leave electorate was older
homeowners. Both facts are true; the pattern claim rests on the
bulk, and says so.

The idiom, meanwhile, is restorationist wherever one samples it —
and this limb needs no recalibration. "Take back control" was the
Leave voters' own first-ranked reason [^8-10]; the gilets jaunes demanded the "refoundation" — not the
abolition — of representation; the carbon-tax revolt that ignited
them is traced, in the best study, to distrust of the taxing state
rather than to climate denial [^8-11]. The
grievance is fiscal reach and administrative distance; the demand
is that the state be returned to its imagined former owners; the
composition is the anxious middle. Three of the form's four limbs,
in the pattern's own terms. The fourth limb — the outcome — is the
next section's subject, and the book's wager rides partly on it.
[END BRIDGE]

## VII.

Here the chapter must be explicit about what is evidence and what
is inference, because its closing claim is the book's own — marked
(c), in this book's convention — and it should be stated with its
falsification condition attached.

The medieval record supports this much: the propertied-anxious
revolts shaped settlements and did not prevent consolidation.
Tübingen's rights-for-debt entered Württemberg's constitution;
Cusset's pensions rearranged the French court; Cade's programme
became the Yorkist platform and some of it, eventually, Yorkist
practice. But the taille outlived the Praguerie by three and a half
centuries; the compagnies followed the revolt against their
creation by five years; the harvest of heads closed the Kentish
account; and the consolidating decades of the 1470s onward — the
book's Chapters 10 and 11 — proceeded through and over the whole
family of risings. Shaped, not prevented: that is the medieval
verdict, and it is documentary.

The modern inference is that the present wave will run the same
course, and the early evidence is consistent with it. The single
sharpest exhibit is fiscal, as it should be: Brexit — the wave's
largest delivered demand, the one instance in which the
restorationist programme actually took control of a major state's
machinery — has been followed not by a retrenchment of the state's
fiscal reach but by a tax take projected by the government's own
forecaster to approach "a post-war high of 37.1% of GDP by
2028-29" [^8-12]. The gilets jaunes won their carbon-tax
retreat and a December package, and the administrative programme
they rose against continued. Where populists have taken power, the
peer-reviewed record finds institutional erosion and growth costs
[^8-13] — damage, in other words, but
not demolition: nowhere in the gathered record has the wave
durably reduced the state's fiscal-administrative reach. The
demand these movements make of the state, here as in the
fifteenth century, is not that it be smaller but that it belong
to them — and a movement whose demand is possession is absorbed
by the first settlement that offers it a share. The claim is the
book's inference, not a law; Appendix C states its indicator in
falsifiable form, and a populist wave that durably shrank the
fiscal state would count against this book. On the record to date,
none has.

The commons of Kent went home with pardons in their purses, and
the state they had risen against — hollow, bankrupt,
humiliated — was, within a generation, collecting taxes their
grandfathers would not have believed and enforcing them with an
apparatus no captain of Kent could resist. The next chapters
build that apparatus in view of the reader: first the money and
the men (Chapter 10), then the variants across the continent
(Chapter 11). But before the building, one more demolition. The
consolidating state faced not only armed commons and over-mighty
subjects but a subtler rival: the dense plural legal order — the
sanctuaries, the law merchant, the franchise courts, the
ecclesiastical immunities — inside which both Cade's Kent and the
king's own officers had learned to manoeuvre. What happened when
the new sovereignty met the old law's many houses is the business
of the chapter that follows.

# Chapter 8 — Private Justice, Private War

## I.

On 28 January 1449, by her husband's account, Margaret Paston was at
home in the fortified manor house of Gresham in Norfolk with twelve
companions when a private army arrived to evict her.

The account survives because John Paston put it into a petition — the
family's characteristic instrument, as this chapter will show — and
the petition's language is worth hearing at length, in its own
spelling: Lord Moleyns "sent to the seid mansion a riotous peple, to
the nombre of a thowsand persones… with curesse, brigaunders, jakks…
gonnes… long cromes to drawe doun howsis… with which thei myned down
the walles… and so came in to the seid mansion, the wiff of your
besecher at that tyme beyng ther in, and xij. persones with her; the
which persones thei dreve oute" [^9-1]. The thousand is
the plaintiff's number — a partisan figure in a pleading, and this
book cites it as such — but the guns, the wall-mining and the eviction
are the uncontested core, and so is what the petition says next, which
is the fifteenth century explaining its own legal system in one
clause. Paston tells the crown plainly why he has not simply gone to
law: "your seid besecher is not abille to sue the commone lawe in
redressyng of this heynos wrong, for the gret myght and alyaunce of
the seid Lord" [^9-2]. The courts existed. The writs
ran. And against a lord of sufficient might and alliance, they did
not work — a fact Paston states without outrage, as a competent man
states the weather.

Chapter 7 closed with the consolidating state facing the old law's
many houses, and this chapter walks through them. Its subject is the
plural legal environment of the fifteenth century — feud, vendetta,
sanctuary, benefit of clergy, merchant and admiralty jurisdiction,
magnate justice and the king's peace, all operating at once — and its
argument has two edges. The first: this plurality was the *operative*
law of the dispersal period, not its breakdown — a system with rules,
instruments and professionals, which contemporaries navigated as a
repertoire. The second, which Part II exists to establish: the
settlement that ended it — the sixteenth century's assertion of royal
justice as monopolist — was a *consequence* of the fiscal-military
consolidation, not its precondition. The courts followed the
companies and the taille; the law did not tame the sword until the
exchequer could pay for both. And the chapter carries Cerny's sixth
characteristic — the *zones grises*, the grey zones and the black
economy — from Part I's diagnosis into Part II's mechanism, because
the modern half of the story is running now, at a scale the reader
will find documented below in the fora's own annual reports.

## II.

Stay with the Pastons, because their file is the best-documented
navigation of legal plurality in the European record, and because
their coping was not exceptional but exemplary.

The family's response to Gresham was not to choose between law and
politics but to work both as one system, and their correspondence
shows the system's gears. Recovery of the manor followed no verdict;
it followed a political event — the fall and murder of the Duke of
Suffolk in 1450, which stripped Moleyns of the protection that had
made him lawsuit-proof. And when the Pastons pressed indictments
afterward, their agent John Osbern reported back, in May 1451, a
sentence that deserves a place in every constitutional history of
England: the sheriff of Norfolk "wold do for yow that he may, excepte
for the aquitell of the Lord Molyns men, in so meche as the Kyng hath
wrete to hym for to shewe favour to the Lord Moleyns and hese men…
Also he seythe that the Lord Molyns hath sent hym a letter, and my
Lord of Norffolk anoder, for to shew favour in these indytements, he
darnot abide the joporte of that, that he shuld offende the Kinges
commaundment" [^9-3]. The king's writ, the
magnate's letter and the sheriff's fear, operating as one machine:
royal justice was not absent from this world — it was *enlisted*, a
factional resource like any other, and the Pastons' own bill to the
Chancellor names the enlistment's legal arts in the statute's
vocabulary: "mayntenaunces, champerties, embraceries" [^9-4] —
the judicial face, as Chapter 3 observed, of the same affinity
system that retained the armed men.

The file's second act escalates from lawfare to siege. Sir John
Fastolf died in November 1459 leaving a contested deathbed will and
the Pastons claiming his Norfolk and Suffolk lands; a decade of
litigation across king's bench, chancery and the church courts (the
will was spiritual business) culminated, in August and September
1469, in the Duke of Norfolk besieging Caister Castle for some five
weeks — John Paston the younger and about thirty men defending,
artillery worked on both sides, at least one defender killed, the
duke's three thousand being, again, a Paston number carried here
with the historians' discount attached. Caister was not an anomaly
of one wicked duke. DeVries's catalogue of English private
gunpowder war in these decades — assembled, the reader will
recall, as evidence for Chapter 10's England — runs from the
Norwich riots of 1443 through Mowbray's guns against Wingfield's
house, the siege of Powderham in 1455, the Berkeley–Talbot feud
"ended… with gunpowder weaponry bombardments between the two
factions" at Nibley Green in 1469, to Hornby Castle taken in 1471
with "the cannon *Mile End*" — subjects bombarding subjects,
"without ever involving the king," and the fallen Paston obliged
to surrender his own guns to the victorious duke [^9-5]. The most advanced weapon of the age, privately owned,
privately aimed, resolving a probate dispute: Chapter 5's hollow
sovereignty, seen from below.

What the Paston file teaches is not that England was lawless — the
file *is* law, thousands of pages of it — but that law was plural
and the plurality was the practice. King's bench, chancery, the
church courts, the county sessions, the magnate's good lordship,
the petition, the marriage, and at the limit the garrison: the
family worked them as alternative instruments in a single
repertoire — a keyring of jurisdictions, no single key of which
could be relied on to turn. That is what a plural legal
environment looks like from inside, and it is the condition
against which everything in this chapter's second half must be
measured.

## III.

Cross to the Empire and the plurality is not merely practised but
codified — private war as a *legal institution*, with formalities.

The Golden Bull of 1356 — the Empire's constitutional charter — 
does not prohibit the feud. It regulates its notice period:
challenges are invalid unless "the challenge, three natural days
before, shall have been intimated personally to the challenged man
himself, or publicly in the place where he has been accustomed to
reside" [^9-6]. The *Absage* —
the formal defiance — separated lawful feud from mere robbery; the
feud was a procedure, with declaratory forms, customary limits on
targets and duration, and a jurisprudence. What to make of this is
the object of a live and instructive scholarly quarrel, staged
here as this book stages such things: Brunner read the feud as a
coherent legal order rooted in noble lordship — and wrote the
reading in the 1930s, under assumptions his critics have not let
rest; Algazi answered that the "legal institution" was lordly
violence systematised, its costs falling on peasants who appear in
the sources mainly as burnt property; Zmora's Franconian
reconstruction reframes the practice as an interplay of princely
state-building and noble stratification, feuds conducted "not
against strangers but with neighbours, relatives and their feudal
lords" [GAP: Brunner, Algazi and both Zmora monographs are on the
acquisition list; the debate is staged from open statements].
Whichever side one takes, the point this chapter needs survives
them all: nobody's feud was the war of all against all. It was
jurisdiction, claimed and exercised privately — and the Empire's
attempt to end it makes the chapter's sequencing argument in the
negative. At Worms in 1495 Maximilian's diet enacted the *Ewiger
Landfriede* — the Perpetual Peace, "noteworthy for its definitive
abolition of the right of feud" [^9-7] — and erected,
in the same session, the Reichskammergericht as the standing
substitute forum: prohibition and replacement in one act, on
paper as complete an assertion of the public sword as Europe had
seen. And it did not take. The career of Götz von Berlichingen —
the 1512 attack on Nuremberg's merchants, the imperial ban bought
off for 14,000 gulden, the second ban, the 2,000-gulden oath, and
by his own late memoir some fifteen feuds conducted in his own
name into the 1520s — is the standard demonstration that the
paper abolition ran a half-century and more ahead of the
enforceable fact. The Empire asserted the monopoly without the
fiscal-military substrate beneath it. Hold the result; the
chapter's close returns to it as the control case.

## IV.

Italy institutionalised the same plurality at its most notarised.

The spectacular case first, with its superlative correctly
attributed: on Giovedì Grasso — 27 February — 1511, the
Zambarlani faction of Udine, under Antonio Savorgnan and swollen
with peasant support, massacred and burned out the rival
Strumieri nobility, the violence propagating into
castle-sackings across Friuli — in Muir's verdict, "the most
extensive popular revolt in Renaissance Italy" [GAP: Muir's *Mad
Blood Stirring* for narrative detail; the wording is carried
from the scholarly apparatus]. The sequel teaches the structure:
fifteen months later Savorgnan himself was cut down by Strumieri
avengers outside a church at Villach — across the imperial
border, under Venetian and Habsburg jurisdictions at once,
vendetta answered through vendetta in the seams between
sovereignties. But the vendetta's ordinary life, the scholarship
insists, was less cinematic and more contractual: Dean's reading
of Bolognese and Florentine cases finds the practice
law-entangled and restrained beside its literary image; Zorzi
treats it as a practice *regulated within* communal legal
culture — timing, targets and settlement all rule-bound; and the
institutional counterpart was a mass documentary form, the
notarised *instrumentum pacis* — the peace instrument — of which
the Florentine notarial registers alone preserve five hundred
and twenty-six between 1257 and 1343 [GAP: Jansen's dataset at
page level]. Enmity, in the Italian cities, was something the
legal order *managed* — priced, timed, settled, notarised — as
much as something it forbade. The draft states the Italian
finding in exactly that calibrated form, and no further: the
communes and the princely regimes that followed them governed
through the management of enmity as much as through its
abolition.

## V.

Around and beneath these national traditions ran the pan-European
plumbing of legal plurality, and a brief inventory fixes how deep
the water stood.

Sanctuary: any felon could take refuge in any church for up to
forty days, then abjure the realm before the coroner "clothed in
sackcloth" and walk, by an assigned road, to the sea; chartered
sanctuaries — Westminster, Durham, Beverley, St Martin le Grand,
at least twenty-two houses — offered permanent refuge. The scale
is now counted: over 1,800 sanctuary seekers in the records
between 1394 and 1557; Durham's register alone, across sixty
years, holds petitions from 283 men admitting involvement in 195
homicides [^9-8]. Benefit
of clergy: the literate defendant who could read the neck-verse —
Psalm 51, *Miserere mei Deus* — passed to the church's gentler
justice; the Tudor statutes narrowed it (one use for laymen,
branded on the thumb, 1489; removed for murder and robbery,
1512 — the statute that detonated the Standish affair and
intersected the Hunne case, a state–church collision over who
may judge whom that Chapter 11 will meet again); it was finally
abolished only in 1827. The law merchant: here the chapter takes
a side in a live historiographical dispute, and says so. The
traditional account — a uniform, transnational, customary *lex
mercatoria*, administered by merchants in their own piepowder
and fair courts, ancestor of modern arbitration — is the story
the modern private-ordering literature tells about its own
pedigree (Berman; Benson; Trakman; the economists' celebrated
Champagne-fairs model). The medievalists have dismantled it.
Kadens: "The law merchant myth is false on many levels… the
most widespread aspects of commercial law arose from contract
and statute rather than custom" [^9-9]; Sachs, from the
fullest surviving fair-court records, shows the merchants of St
Ives "substantially subject to local control," custom varying
town by town. What existed was real and plural — merchant
courts, expeditious procedure, forum-shopping among
jurisdictions — but it was jurisdictional plurality under local
law, not a self-made universal code. The correction cuts
forward, and the chapter will cash it in the modern half: the
sharpest contemporary advocates of private legal ordering rest
their case on a romanticised medieval original that did not
exist. Admiralty: the crown's own courts drew knives on each
other — the Admiralty court, emerging after Sluys and civil-law
in procedure, was caged by statute in 1389, the admirals ordered
not to "meddle from henceforth of anything done within the
realm, but only of a thing done upon the sea" (13 Ric. II c. 5):
plurality inside the crown itself. The period synthesis is
Johnson's, and it can stand for the whole section: "Late-
medieval England was a world of a thousand law-courts… a society
of dense legal pluralism, with thousands of overlapping and
occasionally conflicting institutions" [^9-10].

## VI.

[BRIDGE — the medieval→modern joint runs through this section; the
disanalogies are stated at its close, and the framing is Roderick's
to set.] The modern half begins where this book's theory chapter
predicted it. Cerny, in 1998, forecast that the multilayering of
institutions would lead, "at best, to the emergence of quasi-public,
quasi-private dispute settlement regimes seeking to arbitrate
competing claims for rights and privileges in this patchwork system"
[^9-11] — and his sixth characteristic, the *zones grises*,
foresaw widening "escape routes… for those operating more or less
'outside the law'," with "exit from political society… a more viable
option for a wider range of actors and activities," mainstream
activity expanding "as much through grey zones as through legitimate
means, much as the so-called 'black economy' has done" [^9-12]. Twenty-eight years on, the audit can be run from the fora's
own annual reports.

The private judiciary of high-value commerce, by its own count: the
ICC registered 831 new arbitrations in 2024, with $103 billion in
dispute in the new cases and a pending caseload of $354 billion —
its own "all-time record"; the LCIA took 362 referrals, 95 per cent
international, from parties in 101 jurisdictions; Hong Kong's centre
503 cases; Singapore's 625, with US$11.86 billion in dispute
[^9-13]. Beneath the caseloads runs the
enforcement lattice that makes a private award better than most
public judgments: the New York Convention, 172 contracting states
[^9-14], under which the world's courts stand
ready to execute the decisions of tribunals the parties chose. (The
famous claim that "90 per cent of cross-border contracts" contain
arbitration clauses is corrected here to what the survey actually
measured: 90 per cent of surveyed practitioners *preferring*
arbitration in 2021, 87 in 2025 — a preference statistic from
inside the guild, and cited as such.) Above the commercial tier
sits the parallel judiciary of capital against sovereigns that
Chapter 1 met at Próspera: 1,440 known treaty-based investor–state
arbitrations by mid-2025 [^9-15], ICSID past its thousandth
registered case — franchise jurisdiction, the medieval merchant
would have said, chartered in treaty.

Then the tier the fifteenth century did not have: adjudication at
platform scale. Meta's Oversight Board — constituted by charter,
financed through an irrevocable $280 million trust, its content
decisions binding on the company by the company's own instrument —
received 398,597 appeals in 2023 and issued 53 decisions; in 2024,
558,235 appeals and 65 decisions, overturning Meta in roughly nine
cases in ten it chose to hear (Board annual reports). Beneath that
appellate spire, the mass jurisdiction: Meta removes millions of
pieces of content daily, conceding in its own statement that "one
to two out of every 10 of these actions may have been mistakes"
[^9-16]. Apple's transparency report — disclosed
under the *Cameron v. Apple* settlement, a provenance this book
gets right because it matters — counts, for calendar 2024:
7,771,599 app submissions reviewed; 1,931,400 rejected; 82,509
apps removed; 26,224 removal appeals; 421 restorations; and
128,961,839 customer accounts terminated [^9-17]. Read
those numbers as a legal system, because functionally they are
one: adjudication at the scale of a serious jurisdiction,
administered with the procedural depth of a customer-service
queue. And the private process can be turned on its makers: when
some 75,000 individual arbitration demands landed on Amazon over
voice recordings, the company deleted the mandatory-arbitration
clause from its own conditions of use and retreated to the public
courts (2021) — mass arbitration weaponised against the party
that imposed it.

The purest experiment ran on-chain, and its result is the
chapter's law-merchant correction repeated in real time. The
crypto movement's constitutional theory was Lessig's dictum —
code is law — and its first hard case broke it: when the DAO was
drained of 3.6 million ether in June 2016, the Ethereum
community did not accept the code's verdict; it forked the chain
(20 July 2016, block 1,920,000) and unwound the theft by social
consensus. Code-is-law was suspended by its own congregation the
first time the code produced an intolerable result. The
experiment's subsequent arc bends the same way: juried
token-staked arbitration (Kleros); the Wyoming DAO statute of
2021 — the chain seeking the state's corporate wrapper; Dubai's
digital-economy court — an offshore *state* court competing for
the business. And the grey zone proper — the offshore financial
world that is Cerny's black economy in pinstripe — is being made
legible in real time, and the honest numbers cut both ways: the
stock of offshore household financial wealth still equals
roughly 10 per cent of world GDP, but "thanks to the automatic
exchange of bank information, offshore tax evasion has declined
by a factor of about three in less than 10 years," with only
about a quarter of the offshore stock now evading tax and $12.6
trillion reported across borders in a single year (EU Tax
Observatory 2024, verbatim). Pistor's frame closes the survey:
global capital is coded in the modules of private law, and the
coding runs through "two domestic legal systems, the laws of
England and those of New York State" [^9-18] — the
plural order's true centre of gravity being not no-law but
chosen-law.

The disanalogies, stated as this book's discipline requires,
because the romanticisation objection runs in both directions.
The modern plural legal environment differs from the
fifteenth century's in three connected ways. It is, in the main,
non-violent — the Caister sieges of our era are litigated, not
bombarded. Its fora are parasitic on state law, not rivals to
it: the arbitral award is worth its enforcement in state courts;
the ISDS tribunal sits on treaties; the DAO fled into a Wyoming
LLC; Pistor's empire of private coding runs on two *state* legal
systems. And its grey zones are being made legible even as they
persist. The feud's true modern analogue is therefore not
arbitration — it is the zones where enforcement still runs on
violence, the interstitial spaces Part III maps. What the modern
order shares with the medieval one is the structure Cerny
specified: plural, overlapping, competing jurisdictions, with
the enforcement business migrating to fora the parties choose.
[END BRIDGE]

## VII.

How did the first plural legal order end? Not by argument — and
the sequence is the chapter's finding, so it is given with dates.

England: Star Chamber evolved out of the council's judicial
business across the later fifteenth century (the 1487 act, this
book notes against the textbook tradition, did not create it;
the "pro camera stellata" tag is a later annotation); the
Council of the North grew from Richard of Gloucester's council
of the 1470s–80s to its permanent Tudor form in 1537 — the year
after the Pilgrimage of Grace; the Council of Wales and the
Marches ran from 1473 to statutory footing in 1543; the Court
of Requests took form under Richard III and Henry VII. The
sanctuary and clergy privileges fell in the same generation, by
the dated sequence now in this book's corpus at source:
sanctuary abolished for treason in 1534; sanctuarymen badged
and confined in 1535; the dissolution extinguishing the great
monastic sanctuaries; the act of 1540 — "an even bolder
reform," in Baker's account — withdrawing the privilege for
murder, rape, burglary, robbery and arson and abolishing "all
special sanctuaries not expressly named"; and the end, in the
statute of 1624: "no sanctuary or privilege of sanctuary be
hereafter admitted or allowed" (Baker, *Ecclesiastical Law
Journal*, in repo). France: the provincial parlements — royal
justice built out province by province — Toulouse definitively
from 1443, Grenoble 1453, Bordeaux 1462, and Dijon in 1477,
planted in the conquered duchy in the very year Charles the
Bold fell at Nancy: the court arriving with the annexation,
Chapter 10's absorption wearing robes. Spain: the audiencia of
Valladolid reorganised by the Catholic Monarchs in 1485, its
southern twin settled at Granada in 1505; the Santa Hermandad —
a royal constabulary — instituted at the Cortes of Madrigal in
1476. And the Empire, the control case, asserted everything and
enforced little: the 1495 abolition of the feud, the standing
court, and a century of Götz von Berlichingen — the monopoly
proclaimed without the fiscal-military substrate, and therefore
proclaimed again and again.

Set the dates against Part II's spine and the sequence is
unmistakable. The taille is granted in 1439; the companies stand
from 1445; the train runs from 1449; and the courts, councils,
constabularies and abolitions arrive in the two generations
*after* — 1470s to 1540s — precisely where the fiscal-military
consolidation had laid the substrate, and nowhere else. The
Empire ran the counterfactual: assertion without substrate,
supremacy on paper. The plural legal environment was not argued
out of existence; it was outspent.

[BRIDGE — the modern translation of the sequence claim, to the
section's end.] The modern re-assertion is observable, dateable,
and early in its arc. The European bloc has begun dismantling
private adjudication inside itself: *Achmea* (2018) and
*Komstroy* (2021) precluding intra-EU investment arbitration;
withdrawal from the Energy Charter Treaty notified in June 2024
and effective a year later. The same bloc licenses and caps the
platform courts in one clause: the DSA compels internal
complaint systems and certifies out-of-court settlement bodies
which "shall not have the power to impose a binding settlement"
[^9-19] — private adjudication permitted, its bindingness
reserved to the state. The first DMA fines have issued (April
2025); the crypto experiment has been brought under a full
regulatory code [^9-20], its
flagship operators processed through the public criminal law —
Binance's $4,316,126,163 resolution (November 2023), its
founder's successor-in-interest at FTX sentenced to
twenty-five years (March 2024); the American antitrust wave has
reached remedies (September 2025). The emerging form is not
abolition of the private fora but their subordination — state
courts above platform process above private ordering — a
*hierarchical plurality*, to give the settlement's legal shape
the name Part III will use. The sixteenth century did the same:
it did not close the church courts or the manorial courts; it
ranked them, under a sovereign whose writ was funded. Whether
the modern rankings hold depends on the fiscal-military
substrate beneath them — which is Chapter 10's question, already
answered for the reader, operating in the present tense. [END
BRIDGE]

One element of the coming assertion, however, the fifteenth
century would find familiar in a way this chapter has not yet
touched. The instruments of monopoly — the councils, the
prerogative courts, the licensing statutes — were operated in
the king's name, and the king was not an abstraction but a man:
anointed, mortal, sometimes a child, sometimes mad, his two
bodies the constitutional theory of the age. The consolidating
state that Part II has been assembling was, at its summit, a
person — and what it means that the executive of the new
monopoly was personal, how the person was doubled, deputised
and eventually bureaucratised out of the constitution, is the
next chapter's business.

# Chapter 9 — The King's Two Bodies, Broken

## I.

On 25 March 1454, a deputation of English lords rode to Windsor to
speak with their king, and afterwards did something without precedent:
they put his silence on the record.

The Parliament Roll preserves their report. They had pressed Henry VI
"by all the waies and meanes that they cowede thynke" to obtain some
instruction, some preference, some sign — "but they cowede gete noo
answere ne signe" — and this fact, the fact of nothing, was formally
entered "of record" [^10-1]. Constitutions do not usually
document their own voids; the entry exists because the void had to be
proved before anything could be built over it. Two days later the
lords elected Richard of York "Protectorem & Defensorem… quamdiu Regi
placeret" — for as long as it should please the king — an office whose
very name had been chosen, as this chapter will show, for what it did
*not* claim. The reader knows the political story from Chapter 5: the
protectorate improvised, resented, dissolved at the king's recovery;
St Albans five months later. This chapter is about the constitutional
theory that made the improvisation so difficult — the most
sophisticated legal fiction of the age, and the load under which it
broke.

Chapter 8 ended by observing that the machinery of the coming
judicial monopoly was operated in the name of a person — anointed,
mortal, occasionally a child, occasionally mad. The age's own lawyers
had an answer to that mortality, and it is one of the most remarkable
constructions in the history of political thought. The king, they
held, has two bodies. Plowden's report of the Tudor cases states the
doctrine in its maturity: the body natural, subject to "the
Imbecility of Infancy or old Age and other natural Defects"; and "his
Body politic is a Body that cannot be seen or handled… utterly void
of Infancy and old Age and other natural Defects" — so that "what the
King does… cannot be invalidated or frustrated by any Disability in
his natural Body" [^10-2]. Blackstone
would carry the maxim into the classical constitution: the king never
dies. It is elegant, it is functional, and it contains a single
unexamined premise on which everything in this chapter turns: the
doctrine presumes that the natural body *executes*. The body politic
is void of defects precisely because the body natural does the work
and the fiction absorbs the flaws. Where the work outgrows the
worker — where the demands on the natural body exceed what any
natural body can supply — the fiction faces a choice it was not
designed to survive: break, or be re-founded on something other than
a person. The fifteenth century broke it. The sixteenth re-founded
it. And the twenty-first, this chapter will argue in its second
half, is running the same experiment on new continental
institutions whose constitutions are, by charter, personal. A
constitution that lives in a body can die by one.

## II.

The doctrine's genealogy matters because it shows the fiction was
built for exactly the problem that defeated it.

Kantorowicz traced the arc — from the Norman Anonymous's *gemina
persona*, the twinned person of the anointed king, around 1100;
through the thirteenth-century juristic turn, when the theologians'
*corpus mysticum* migrated from the sacramental church to the body
politic; through Bracton's lawyer-king; to the Tudor maturity
Plowden reports [GAP: Kantorowicz's chapter-level argument awaits
the book, on the acquisition list; the arc is carried from the open
scholarly apparatus]. The construction's purpose was continuity:
the medieval polity needed the crown's authority to survive
minorities, dotages and the grave, and the two-bodies fiction — 
like the parallel maxim that dignity does not die — supplied a
corporate immortality centuries before the business corporation
generalised the trick. Maitland, the doctrine's great sceptic, is
kept in view throughout this chapter as the useful acid: the two
bodies were "metaphysiological nonsense," he wrote; it was "an
idle jest to say that the king never dies" when the demise of the
crown in fact stopped "all the wheels of state" — parliament
dissolved, offices vacated, the peace itself in abeyance until the
successor's accession [^10-3]. Maitland's point is
not that the fiction was silly but that it was *strained* — a
patch over a personal constitution's structural exposure — and
strained fictions are diagnostic instruments: where they tear
shows where the load ran.

## III.

The tear of 1453–54 is documented at a level of constitutional
self-consciousness that repays close reading, because the lords of
England, forced to govern around an insensible king, reasoned
their way along the two-bodies doctrine's exact edge — and their
drafting shows it.

They could not simply replace the king's will, because the
doctrine located authority in him and the fiction made his
authority perpetual; any instrument that *transferred* the
exercise of kingship would concede that kingship was transferable
— a concession fatal to the constitution and dangerous to every
magnate who might later face a recovered king. So they built,
with visible care, a non-substitute. York's own protestation
located the emergency's authority in the peerage only "by
th'occasion of th'enfirmite of oure said Souveraine Lord," in
whom, he affirmed, "resteth th'exercice of his auctoritee" — the
exercise itself never moving. And the office they gave him was
defined by negation, in the roll's extraordinary sentence: the
lords devised "nought the name of Tutour, Lieutenaunt, Governour,
nor of Regent, nor noo name that shall emporte auctorite of
governaunce of the lande; but the seid name of Protectour and
Defensour, the whiche emporteth a personell duete of entendaunce
to the actuell defence of this land… duryng the Kynges pleaser"
[^10-4]. Not tutor, not lieutenant, not
governor, not regent — four rejected titles, each rejected
because it implied governance; and the accepted title imported
only a personal duty of attendance to defence, revocable at the
pleasure of a king who could not speak. The protectorate did not
fail as an institution. It was *designed* not to be one — a
scaffolding built around the throne on the strict condition that
it touch nothing — and the design was the two-bodies doctrine
operating as a constraint on constitutional invention. Watts's
verified formulations name what the same political nation had
already been doing for a generation: the minority council's
"artificial king," the conciliar manufacture of an "artificial
royal will in periods when the king could exercise no will in
person" [^10-5]. England could simulate the royal
will when custom licensed it — for a child — and could not
lawfully institutionalise the simulation for a broken adult.
The fiction permitted a fiction, and forbade a solution.

France, characteristically, tried to legislate the solution in
advance — and the deeper case shows the deeper failure. Charles
VI's government, after the first attacks, wrote the substitute
into ordinance: January 1393, the queen named co-guardian of the
dauphin hedged with a council of twelve; April 1403, government
during the king's "absences" — the era's exquisite word for his
mind's departures — vested in majority rule in council (the
ordinances verified via the open scholarly apparatus, footnoted
to the *Ordonnances des rois de France*) [GAP: Famiglietti and
Autrand for the court detail]. On paper, France possessed what
England refused to draft: a standing constitutional provision
for an executive without the king. In practice, intermittency
defeated law. Every lucid interval re-personalised the
constitution — the returning king could undo, re-grant,
re-favour — and every relapse re-opened the contest among the
princes whom the ordinances had made colleagues; the council
built to absorb the king's absences became the arena in which
Orléans and Burgundy fought for his person, and Chapter 5's
civil war followed. The two designs — England's deliberate
non-institution, France's legislated institution — failed in
opposite ways, and the pairing is the chapter's evidence that
the problem was structural: under a personal constitution,
neither refusing nor drafting the substitute could stabilise
it, because the constitution's own premise — authority lives in
the person — re-asserted itself at every recovery, every
relapse, every death.

The century's full experimental record confirms the pattern
across every variant tried. In 1422 the lords rejected
Gloucester's claim to a regency by blood ("no justification…
of priority of blood"), styled Bedford and Gloucester
protector-and-defender with patronage capped at minor offices
and real control vested in a quorate council, commissions
running "during the King's pleasure." In 1454, the negative
definition. In 1455–56, the same limited terms, dismissal now
by the king in parliament, the infant Prince of Wales's rights
expressly safeguarded. Across the Channel, the Treaty of
Troyes vested the regency of France in Henry V while
elaborately preserving each kingdom's separate laws — the
dual monarchy as a personal union precisely *not* an
institutional merger. Limited, revocable, council-bound, and
protective of the personal constitution each was built to
relieve: the fifteenth century ran the regency experiment
perhaps half a dozen times in two kingdoms, and every run
returned the same result. (The framing of that record — 
repeated institutional experiments, consistently failing to
substitute for an adult functioning king — is this book's
reading of an open fact-pattern, and is marked as the book's
own.)

## IV.

What finally answered the broken fiction was not a better
regency ordinance, and the chapter must now engage the debate
about what it was.

Elton's thesis, in its strong form, gave the answer a decade and
an author: in the 1530s, under Thomas Cromwell, England underwent
a revolution in government — household administration giving way
to bureaucratic departments, a privy council, a "chief national
executive"; the state, in the phrase Collinson's memoir
preserves, "took leave of the Middle Ages," and "Cromwell, not
Henry… really the government." The strong thesis was attacked at
birth (Wernham's review, 1956), besieged across the great *Past
& Present* exchange of 1963–65 (Harriss and Williams pressing
medieval continuities, Cooper the seventeenth century, Elton
replying twice), and revised from inside by Elton's own pupils —
Starkey's privy chamber restoring the personal court to the
centre, the *Revolution Reassessed* essays distributing the
change across two centuries — until Elton himself conceded, in
1991, that he may have "overstated [Cromwell's] systematic
approach" [GAP: the debate's texts are gated; the citation
chain is verified, the wording carried from the open memoir].
This book requires none of the strong version. It requires what
no party to the debate contests — call it the weak thesis, a
label that is the book's own: across the later fifteenth and
sixteenth centuries, in England as on the continent, the
personal constitution gave way under fiscal-military pressure
to an institutional one — offices that did not die, records
that did not forget, salaried officials whose authority ran
from function rather than proximity. Whether the transition
was Cromwellian and sudden or distributed and slow is a
quarrel over tempo and agency inside an agreed direction —
the same shape, the reader will notice, as the
military-revolution debate Chapter 6 staged, and it is
resolved here the same way: the book takes the direction,
which is conceded, and leaves the ranking to the specialists.
The two-bodies fiction was not repealed; it was relieved. The
political body acquired, at last, an executive that was not a
body at all.

## V.

Five centuries after the relief, the personal constitution has
returned — not in the residual monarchies, where the two bodies
survive as ceremony, but in the charters of the new continental
institutions, where it is load-bearing and growing.

The documentation is the institutions' own. Meta's proxy statement
discloses that its founder holds 99.8 per cent of the Class B
shares and 60.8 per cent of the company's total voting power, and
that Meta is, in the exchange's formal category, a "controlled
company" [^10-6]. Alphabet's founders jointly hold
some 52.7 per cent of its votes through the same dual-class
mechanism [^10-7]. Nor are these legacy
anomalies: by Ritter's count, 48.4 per cent of American
technology flotations in 2025 carried dual-class structures,
against essentially none in 1980 — the personal constitution is
the *default charter* of the new institutional generation. What
the fifteenth century achieved by anointing, the twenty-first
achieves by share class: authority vested in a named natural
body, formally insulated from the institution's other members,
and — the two-bodies problem returning on schedule — subject to
every natural defect the body politic was invented to transcend.
The extreme case concentrates the point. One individual
simultaneously heads six ventures spanning launch, orbital
communications, vehicles, tunnelling, neural interfaces and
frontier AI (Tesla beneficial ownership 19.8 per cent per the
2025 proxy; his reported control of SpaceX appears in no public
primary document and is carried here as reported only). The
episode that displays the constitutional stakes is told in its
corrected form, fully attributed, because the popular version is
false in a direction that matters: in 2022, asked by Ukraine to
enable Starlink coverage for a drone attack on the Crimean
anchorage, Musk *declined to enable* it — the coverage was never
on, and no switch was thrown — citing escalation risk (Isaacson's
revised account and Musk's own statement, in agreement on this).
Told correctly, the episode is still remarkable: a war-relevant
decision of state consequence, taken by a natural body
constrained by no office, under no command authority, on his own
strategic judgement. And the state's response arrived on
schedule and is the more instructive half: dedicated defence
contracting for the constellation, and Starshield — "owned and
controlled by the US government," in the operative description —
the improvised institutional substitute, built because the
personal constitution above a critical capability had become
intolerable to the political body that depended on it.

The pattern is not confined to the platforms, and the chapter
states its public-sector face with the same primary discipline.
The presidential systems are personalising: the American
Presidency Project's tables show the current administration
issuing executive orders at 179 a year, the highest rate since
Franklin Roosevelt. The party-state has personalised furthest:
the 2018 removal of China's presidential term limits (2,958
votes for, two against), and the concentration of policy
coordination in leading small groups chaired by the leader —
six of eleven at the count in the open literature —
"informal, off-the-books mechanisms" of personal rule, several
of which were then upgraded into formal Commissions: the
routinisation datum, personal authority being re-clothed in
institutional dress even as it concentrates [^10-8].
And the American experiment in personalised authority *inside*
the state ran its full arc within a single year, and is
recorded here as the dated episode it is: a department of
government efficiency created by executive order in January
2025, animated by the same extreme case above, exited by its
animating person that May, and disbanded by November (OPM) —
created by order, dissolved by interview, its claimed savings
contested. Across systems — proxy statement, executive order,
party constitution — the signature is identical: authority
migrating from office to person, then, under load, being
hastily re-clothed in improvised institutional dress. The
fifteenth century would recognise the wardrobe.

## VI.

Whether the improvisations mature is the pending question, and
the honest answer is that the record cuts both ways — which is
what a transition looks like from inside.

The substitutes are institutionalising. Trust-and-safety has
professionalised into a discipline with a standards body and now
an ISO standard; the European legislator has written the
counterweight into law — the Digital Services Act requires the
largest platforms to establish "a compliance function,
independent from their operational functions," whose head "shall
not be removed without prior approval of the management body"
[^10-9]: statute compelling an internal check on founder
discretion, the conciliar ordinance of 1403 re-enacted as
European regulation. The AI governance layer is acquiring
offices — the UK's institute, its American counterpart, the EU
AI Office with its growing staff — and the frontier labs have
begun writing their own regency instruments: Anthropic's
Long-Term Benefit Trust, a class of stock held by trustees who
will elect a board majority — a constitutional device for
placing the institution's long-run interest beyond any single
natural body (primary announcement). Even the securities
regulator has formalised the two-body problem: since 2009, CEO
succession is a governance matter shareholders may raise, not
"ordinary business" the board may keep to itself [^10-10].

And the substitutes still fail against the person. The test
case ran in November 2023, and this book reads it evenly
because it cuts both ways. OpenAI's nonprofit board — the
regency instrument, formally empowered — dismissed the founder
for being "not consistently candid in his communications." Five
days later he was back, and the board was gone, dissolved by
the coalition his person could still command: employees,
investors, the essential commercial partner. The substitute
existed, and acted, and could not hold — Charles VI's lucid
interval in corporate form, the returning person
re-personalising the constitution that had briefly operated
without him. Institutionalisation of the new political bodies
is pending, not achieved; the two-bodies problem of the
platform age is live, and Appendix C carries its indicators.

The fifteenth century's resolution, when it came, did not come
from constitutional theory at all. No regency ordinance, no
protectoral drafting, no conciliar formula solved the broken
fiction — the solution arrived from outside the problem, in the
form of offices, records and salaried officials: the
bureaucratic state, which relieved the natural body by making
most of government proceed without reference to any body at
all. And what forced that invention was not philosophy but
pressure — the fiscal-military pressure of the gun and the
payroll, which demanded an executive that could not pause for
a king's health, his minority, or his grief. The reader has
already seen the machine at work; the next chapter opens its
account books.

# Chapter 10 — The Artillery State

## I.

The founding document of Europe's first standing army does not survive.

We know when it was issued to within a few weeks: Contamine, who
reconstructed the sequence, places the lost ordonnance between an act
given at Nancy on 9 January 1445 and the surviving text of
Louppy-le-Châtel of 26 May 1445, and before 20 April — "on peut avancer
la fin mars," the end of March 1445 [^11-1]. We
know what it did, because the chroniclers Thomas Basin and Mathieu
d'Escouchy describe the result and the Louppy text presupposes it: the
retention, in permanent royal pay, of some fifteen hundred lances —
Basin adds that they were organised in fifteen companies of equal size
— quartered in garrison towns, mustered and inspected, salaried by the
crown, disbandable by the crown alone. But the paper itself is gone.
The act that created the *compagnies d'ordonnance* — the instrument on
which the French monarchy's next three and a half centuries stood — has
to be triangulated from its own consequences, like a planet inferred
from the orbits it disturbs.

There is a discipline in beginning with that absence. This book's
previous chapters have argued that between 1449 and 1453 the price of
decisive force moved, and moved in favour of whoever could pay for a
siege train; Chapter 6 closed with the observation that after 1450
sovereignty had a minimum price, denominated in guns, wages and taxes,
and that the only entities able to pay it were the ones we would
shortly begin calling states. This chapter is about the paying. It
leaves the battlefield almost entirely — the reader who wants walls
falling has Chapter 6 — and follows the money and the offices: the
standing army, the permanent tax, the artillery service as a
department of state, and the reign in which the assembled machine
reached maturity. It is the book's central causal chapter, because the
mechanism it describes — decisive capability forcing permanent
finance, permanent finance forcing administrative capacity,
administrative capacity suppressing every authority between the crown
and the taxable subject — is the mechanism the whole argument claims
is repeatable, and the final section will claim is repeating. And the
missing ordonnance is the right threshold to cross into that argument,
because it makes the point the institutions themselves will make
repeatedly: what mattered was never a document. It was a payroll that
did not stop. The paperwork of the artillery state could be lost,
burned, or — as we shall see, literally — made into cartridges, and
the state ground on, because the state was not in the paper. It was
in the recurring transaction.

## II.

Begin with the army, since the army came first by a few years and
carried the rest.

The reform's shape is best seen in what it replaced. The French crown
had fought the Hundred Years War with armies assembled by summons and
contract — the feudal levy's successors, the indentured retinues, the
free companies hired and unpaid and turned loose to plunder, the
écorcheur economy Chapter 3 anatomised. Assembly was slow, quality
uneven, and the off-switch catastrophic: a peace or a truce converted
soldiers into brigands wholesale. What the acts of 1445 instituted was
the opposite object: a force that existed *between* campaigns. The
lance fournie, as Contamine enumerates it from the ordinances, was
"composée de six hommes et de six chevaux" — the man-at-arms in full
harness, his coutillier, a page, two mounted archers and a valet
[^11-2]; of the six, three or four were true combatants,
and the establishment drifted upward in the manner of all
establishments — a seventh horse tolerated for baggage, up to an
eighth by 1484 [^11-3]. Fifteen hundred lances is the
well-attested strength, publicly notorious enough that an advocate
before the Cour des aides could cite it in 1449 and the Estates of
1484 could demand a return to it; the tidy structure of fifteen
companies of one hundred is Basin's precision, and this book carries
it as his [^11-4]. A royal act of 25 April 1446
confirms the scale and adds a nuance the chapter needs: it speaks of
maintaining "jusqu'à la conclusion de la paix" — until the conclusion
of peace — 1,500 lances for Languedoïl and 500 for Languedoc, *not
counting* the troops on the Normandy and Guyenne frontiers [^11-5]. Two things follow. The
establishment was larger than its famous number; and the founding
frame was still war-duration, not perpetuity. Permanence was not
proclaimed. It happened — the companies simply were not disbanded, by
Charles VII or by anyone after him, and the force grew across the
century (the tradition's figure of fifty-eight companies and some
24,000 men by 1483 is carried here at tertiary level, flagged) until
"until the peace" had quietly become "always."

Precision about the claim's rank in the European record. This was not
the first standing force in Christendom's memory — Charles V had
maintained a paid force in the 1370s, which was disbanded, and the
Ottoman janissaries predate the ordonnance companies by two
generations, a caveat this book states gladly, since the Ottoman
variant is Chapter 11's most radical case. The calibrated claim is
this: the *compagnies d'ordonnance* were the first permanent,
regularly paid royal army in Latin Christendom — permanent in the
event rather than by proclamation, and regularly paid because the pay
was the discipline. The infantry parallel followed in 1448: the
*francs-archers* of the ordonnance of Montil-lès-Tours, 28 April
1448, one archer maintained by each parish, tax-exempt in exchange
for Sunday practice — a cheap, wide, administratively ingenious
experiment whose military performance proved poor and which the
crown replaced with paid professionals in 1480–81. Even the reform's
failure is instructive: the militia principle — capability held in
the community, activated by summons — was tried by the very state
this chapter describes, and discarded in favour of the payroll.

What the payroll paid is now, since this book's corpus acquired
Contamine's tariff annexes, printable. A man-at-arms of the grande
ordonnance drew 15 livres tournois a month; his archer 7 livres 10
sous; the petite ordonnance ran at 10 and 5; and among the
specialists, the *canonnier de garnison* drew 8 livres — the gunner
priced above the archer and below the man-at-arms, a wage grade for
a technical trade that had not existed in the schedules a
generation earlier [^11-6]. And the grid's
prehistory measures what it replaced: a mandement of the Dauphin
Charles from about the summer of 1422 records war so debasing the
coinage that it had become "comme de nulle valeur" — as if of no
value [TRANS. CLAUDE] — his troops unpayable and "réduits au
pillage," and cuts the traditional tariff by a third to a half;
through 1425–37 the scales wobble incoherently; a fresh tariff
appears at the siege of Meaux in 1439; and then the 1445 grid holds
[^11-7]. Between the 1422 document and the 1445
one lies the whole transformation this chapter describes: from a
crown that could not pay its soldiers in money worth having, to a
crown whose monthly wage-grid was stable enough to hold for a
generation and detailed enough to price a gunner between an archer
and a man-at-arms. Armies had been raised before. What was new was
an army *accounted for*.

## III.

The tax came next, and the tax is where the political battle was
fought — briefly, as it turned out.

The constitutional theory of French taxation before 1439 was
consent: the Estates granted, for a purpose and a term, and the
grant lapsed. The ordonnance of 2 November 1439, issuing from the
Estates at Orléans, changed the subject-matter of the grant: the
king alone would raise troops, name captains, and levy the taille
for their pay — the reader met this act in Chapter 7 as the
document the Praguerie rose against, and met the revolt's fate
there too: broken in five months, settled at Cusset with pensions
and nothing structural conceded. What followed the revolt's failure
was not a proclamation of permanent taxation but a practice.
Through the 1440s the crown continued the taille by annual
ordinances no longer sanctioned by the Estates; around 1450 the
annual improvisation gave way to a single general statement of
finance; and by mid-century the tax was, in function, permanent —
granted once, collected forever. The sequence deserves its three
steps — 1439 the grant, the 1440s the practice, c. 1450 the
institution — because the gradualism is the finding. No one
abolished the constitution of consent. It was simply not consulted,
year after year, until non-consultation was the constitution.

Beneath the tax sat the machinery that made it collectable — and
this book's mechanism runs through the machinery, not the rate. The
crown inherited from the 1350s a lattice of *élus* sitting in
*élections* — local assessment and collection districts — and
subordinated them under four *généralités* (six by 1484), with
receivers below: a permanent, layered, professional revenue
administration reaching from the council chamber to the parish
assessment [GAP: the administrative detail at scholarly level
awaits Wolfe, *The Fiscal System of Renaissance France*]. The pump,
once primed, no longer needed the Estates' hand on the handle. The
yield arc is carried at tertiary level pending the fiscal
historians, and flagged as such: roughly 1.8 million livres at the
end of Charles VII's reign; in 1481, near the end of Louis XI's,
the taille alone bringing in 4.6 million [^11-8] — a rough tripling in a generation. And then the record
supplies its own control experiment. At the Estates of Tours in
1484 — the first Estates General in decades, meeting over a royal
minority — the assembled realm asserted itself: the taille was
granted for two years only and cut to some 1.5 million. The
machine absorbed the cut, outlived the assertion, and no Estates
General met again until 1560. A tax the constitution said required
consent had become a tax that could survive the consent's
withdrawal. That is what "permanent" means in this book: not a
legal category but a demonstrated property.

One honesty about vocabulary, because the chapter leans on a term
the period did not use. "Fiscal-military state" is John Brewer's
coinage, made for eighteenth-century Britain and extended backward
by later scholarship to about 1500; no scholarly authority applies
it to Charles VII's France, whose period-native frame is
Contamine's *l'État de guerre*. This book retrojects the term
deliberately, and says so: the claim is not that fifteenth-century
France anticipated Brewer's Britain in institutional detail, but
that the coupling Brewer named — war capability and revenue
machinery growing as one object — is documentably present in the
French 1440s, and that the French case is the coupling's first
full demonstration. The reader may treat the label as the book's
own (c)-grade convenience; the account books beneath it are (a).

## IV.

The third institution is the one the book is named for, and here
the argument must be made with the account books open, because the
claim — artillery as a *department of state*, not a technology —
is precisely what the surviving records document.

The office preceded the famous officeholders: the accounts of the
masters of the artillery run in unbroken series from 1418, through
Pierre Bessonneau's tenure to 1440, with Jean Bureau's accounts
opening in August 1439 and Gaspard's in January 1441 — the
brothers supplanting Bessonneau in practice from 1437, as Chapter
6 established from Contamine's annexes. The reader has seen the
1442 procurement programme and its arithmetic — the gunpowder
establishment at twice the cost of the traditional arsenal — and
this chapter re-deploys that budget line as its premise rather
than re-arguing it. What this chapter adds is duration and
depth: the line item became a department, and the department can
be audited.

By the end of the century the artillery ran on two permanent
accounts with professional treasurers. The *ordinaire* account of
1491 — rendered by Clerambault de Champanges, notary and royal
secretary, "trésorier et receveur général de tous les deniers
appointés pour les gages des canonniers, aides canonniers et
autres officiers de ladicte artillerie du Roy" — totalled 24,236
livres tournois for the year's wages, and it was the
*twenty-second* account of its series: the permanent
artillery-wage line runs back to about 1469 [^11-9]. The *extraordinaire* — campaign expenditure — for 1489
totalled 111,733 livres, drawn across the généralités, Normandy
and Picardy carrying the largest assignments [^11-10]. In 1489 the artillery's expenditures reached 8 per cent
of all French military spending [^11-11]. The
administration of these accounts — keeping, payment, control,
certification — cost 3,667 livres, 3.3 per cent of the total,
and employed about thirty clerks; the ordinaire was paid
quarterly, "comme c'est la règle pour tout ce qui ressortit à
l'armée permanente" — as is the rule for everything belonging to
the permanent army [TRANS. CLAUDE] [^11-12]. Pause
on what that sentence attests. There was a *rule*, and the guns
were inside it: the most advanced weapons system in Europe was a
quarterly payroll with a three-per-cent administrative overhead,
audited by clerks whose names survive.

The personnel had become a career service. The standing
establishment was small — hardly more than 150 names, swollen by
mass extraordinary labour in campaign season — and it was
long-service: of thirty-nine cannoneers attested in 1469, nine
were still serving twenty-two years later, average careers of
around twenty years [^11-13]. The offices had
specialised into a bureaucratic anatomy: a captain of the
wagon-train; a treasurer and keeper of saltpetres, sulphurs and
lead; a keeper of powders; a captain of "mines and approaches"; a
keeper of the "small engines" at Paris [^11-14]. The
master of the artillery had acquired a jurisdiction as well as a
command — in March 1487 the saltpetre-men of Paris appeared in
judgment before the master "en son auditoire ou chastel du
Louvre," the office holding court like any other great office of
the crown [^11-15]. And the logistical footprint
was a state programme in itself. The fragmentary accounts of the
1477 Artois campaign — recovered, by one of archival history's
better accidents, from the binding of a 1721 book into which the
waste paper had been folded — let the financiers' own forecasts
speak: the guns and tools of three artillery bands required 521
horses; the powder, saltpetre, sulphur, lead, shot,
bombard-stones, two mobile forges, pioneers' tools, cordage and
tents required 1,009 more; with 800 pioneers marching under the
captain of the wagon-train [^11-16]. Twelve years earlier,
during the Public Weal crisis, the Milanese ambassadors had
described the royal train on the move: "la plus belle du monde,"
its convoy six leagues long, drawn by 1,200 horses — enough, they
wrote home, "de quoi faire trembler le monde entier," to make the
whole world tremble [TRANS. CLAUDE] [^11-17]. Diplomats do not exaggerate to their own
principals. The train was already, in 1465, an instrument of
state communication: it said, at walking pace and six leagues of
road, what the account books said in livres.

The through-line runs to the century's end and out of this
book's period. On the night of 31 December 1494, Charles VIII's
artillery entered Rome: more than thirty-six pieces drawn by
horses "à une incroyable vitesse" over any ground, the greatest
of them bronze cannon eight feet long and six thousand pounds
[^11-18] — the mobile train whose
Italian debut announced to every chancery in Europe what the
French account books had been quietly compounding for fifty
years. Contamine's own summary of the position on the eve of
Italy can close the institutional case: Charles VIII "a pu se
laisser persuader qu'il possédait un instrument qu'aucune
coalition ne pouvait briser" — an instrument no coalition could
break [TRANS. CLAUDE] [^11-19]. The claim proved
optimistic in Italy's politics; about the instrument's nature it
was exact. What no coalition could break was not the guns. It
was the two account series, the quarterly rule, the thirty
clerks and the twenty-year careers — the apparatus that made the
guns a permanent fact rather than a campaign's improvisation.

## V.

Louis XI is the chapter's exemplar because his reign (1461–83)
is where the assembled machine was stress-tested by the men it
was built to make obsolete — and the test's result is legible in
a way no narrative of royal cunning quite captures.

The War of the Public Weal, in 1465, was the over-mighty
subjects' last systemic bid: a league of the great princes —
Charolais of Burgundy at their head — in arms against the king,
with the reform of the realm as its stated programme and the
dismantling of the fiscal machine as its evident one. Militarily
the league did well. Montlhéry (16 July 1465) was indecisive —
the Burgundian guns, DeVries notes, managed no more than ten
salvos [^11-20]; the artillery duel before Paris that
August and September "may have been the most fearsome artillery
duel of the century," with Commynes, who was present, supplying
the eyewitness superlative: "I have never seen so much shooting
in such a few days" [^11-21]. The towns, a recent
study of the urban dimension shows, held for the crown. And at
the treaties of Conflans and Saint-Maur that October the princes
won on paper nearly everything: Normandy to the king's brother,
the Somme towns to Charolais. Then the machine did what the
machine was for. Louis declared the treaties null and void as
signed under duress, and retook Normandy within months — and
nothing followed. The princes could still win a campaign and
extract a treaty; they could no longer *hold* either against an
adversary whose army did not disband, whose revenue did not
lapse, and whose capacity to resume the contest was, unlike
theirs, a standing fact. The Public Weal failed not at a battle
but at the exchequer.

The rest of the reign is the working-out. Charles the Bold's
death before Nancy in January 1477 — the counter-case §VI treats
on its own terms — opened Burgundy, and Louis seized the duchy
and Picardy, holdings confirmed at the Treaty of Arras in 1482;
Anjou, Maine and Provence had come to the crown by 1481 (the
last count of Maine naming Louis heir the day before he died —
the date tradition varies and is flagged [GAP: pin to a Louis XI
biography on retrieval]). The appanage system — the great
princely endowments that had structured French politics for a
century, and nearly destroyed France under Charles VI — was
absorbed piece by piece, not by battle but by the patient
application of superior permanence: the crown could wait, fund,
litigate, pension and outlive, because the crown's resources
were institutional and every rival's were biographical. The
enemies understood this perfectly, and said so in the century's
most quoted insult. The image of the king as spider — weaving,
patient, venomous at the centre of a web of paid threads — is
Burgundian propaganda: Chastelain's image, in a ballade of 1467,
generalised by Molinet as "l'universelle aragne," the universal
spider. This book quotes the name as what it is — not a neutral
epithet but the losing side's description of the fiscal state's
operator, and precisely for that reason the best contemporary
testimony to what it felt like to stand outside the machine and
watch it work. Commynes, who defected from Burgundy to the
spider's service and knew both courts, left the judgement of the
age from the inside: "of all the princes that I ever knew, the
wisest and most dexterous to extricate himself out of any danger
or difficulty in time of adversity, was our master King Louis
XI" [^11-22]. Wisdom and dexterity had a
balance sheet: the tripled taille, the standing companies, the
enlarged train. Louis XI's France is the first polity in this
book's story in which the crown is systematically stronger than
any of its subjects — and the difference is not charisma, or
luck, or even cunning. It is a budget line no subject could
match, compounding annually.

## VI.

A mechanism worth the name must survive its counter-cases, and this
chapter has three — each a polity that held one part of the artillery
state's triad and lacked another, and each failing in the direction
its missing part predicts.

Burgundy first, because Burgundy is the objection every informed
reader has been holding since Chapter 6. Charles the Bold's state had
superb artillery — probably the best-organised army in Latin Europe on
paper, and the paper was extraordinary. His military ordinances — 
Abbeville, 31 July 1471, instituting 1,200 nine-man lances with
regulated training, discipline, equipment and pay; refined at Bohain
in 1472; the famous drill ordinance issued outside Trier at
St-Maximin in 1473, with its squadron structures, uniforms,
roll-calls and punishments; Lausanne in 1476 — have been read by
modern scholarship [^11-23] as
marking the state's values prevailing over chivalry's in the
ordering of an army [no verbatim printed: the quotation reaches
this book only through an intermediary, and per the attribution
rule it is paraphrased until pinned at source — GAP: Heimpel at
source, or cut at assembly; ordinance labelling and detail to pin
against Vaughan's *Charles the Bold*]. The artillery matched: Commynes calls
it "very large and powerful"; La Marche counts "more than 300 carts
of guns, not counting his culverins or 'haquebusses' which were
'without number'"; at Neuss an eyewitness saw culverin fire "thicker
than rain" [^11-24]. Nor was the apparatus behind
the guns inferior: Burgundy instituted a dedicated *recette de
l'artillerie* in 1458, its accounts auditable at the Chambre des
comptes of Lille, its budget estimates passing through multiple
correcting hands — the état for the Neuss campaign of November 1474
bears Charles's own signature [^11-25]. The duke even paid
better: his 1471 permanent army, raised "sur le modèle français,"
gave its man-at-arms 38.60 grams of gold a month against the French
36.70, with a squad-leader's supplement the French grid lacked,
"destinée à assurer une rigueur plus grande" [^11-26]. By every measurable input — organisation, guns, accounts, pay —
Burgundy was running the artillery state's programme, and running
parts of it better than France.

It lost anyway, and the manner of the losing is the anti-determinist
lesson this book most needs to state. At Grandson, on 2 March 1476,
the speed of the Swiss advance "did not give the Burgundians time to
make much use of their artillery," and a repositioning manoeuvre
read as retreat collapsed the army into rout; the Swiss took the
camp whole — the treasury, the diamonds, and by tradition some four
hundred guns, a figure this book carries as the tradition's, not the
archives' [^11-27]. Casualties at Grandson were small on both
sides; the defeat was of an army's cohesion, not its bodies. At
Morat, on 22 June 1476, operational surprise caught the Burgundian
army divided and undeployed, and the dead ran somewhere between six
and twelve thousand — perhaps a third of the army, with the range
stated because the sources will not narrow it. At Nancy, on 5
January 1477, Charles was killed; the body was found days later,
stripped, frozen, split by a halberd and part-eaten by wolves,
identified by old scars and the long fingernails. Cheap,
disciplined, distributed infantry had beaten the best
capital-intensive army in Europe three times inside ten months.
Anyone tempted to write the fifteenth century as gun-determinism
must stop here and stay as long as necessary.

But the sequel is the finding. Burgundy's fall did not disperse
power; it transferred the whole apparatus intact — lands, revenues,
recette and guns — into the French and Habsburg fiscs, the two
consolidating states flanking it. What Burgundy lacked, on the
evidence of its own accounts, was not administrative capacity —
Depreter's work shows the capacity in detail — but a *survivable
dynastic centre*: one battlefield death unwound a state built to the
highest contemporary specification, because the specification still
routed everything through a single mortal node. (This book will
carry that refinement forward: apparatus is necessary but not
sufficient without a continuous political centre — a finding with
work to do in Part III.) And the archive itself supplies the closing
irony, which the draft declines to improve upon: all but one of the
recette de l'artillerie's accounts were destroyed during the French
Revolution — made into cartridges for the revolutionary army
[^11-28]. The fiscal-military state's paperwork, fired at
its enemies by its successor: the metaphor writes itself, and is
therefore left exactly as the archivists found it.

The Swiss are the second counter-case, and the deeper one. The
victors of Grandson and Morat had just demonstrated the century's
most decisive field capability — and no Swiss artillery state
followed. No permanent central taxation; no standing army; no
chancery state. The Confederation nearly broke apart over the spoils
and the admission of new members, saved at Stans in December 1481 by
the mediation of Nicholas of Flüe; and its military excellence was
monetised not as sovereignty but as *export* — the pension and
capitulation economy, regular foreign payments flowing to cantonal
authorities from the late fifteenth century, the first formal
Franco-Swiss troop convention following in 1521. The Swiss won the
battles and sold the instrument; the fiscal states bought it. The
lesson, and it is the book's own synthesis (c) resting on the sourced
components above: pike squares, unlike siege trains, required no
permanent fisc, no arsenal, no technical service — and so they
*forced no institution-building* on the polity that fielded them.
Decisive military technology consolidates sovereignty not through
the battles it wins but through the institutions it compels. Where
the technology compels nothing — where excellence can be sustained
by valley communities training on Sundays — victory is politically
sterile. The Swiss case is not an exception to the mechanism. It is
the mechanism's control group.

The third counter-case is England, and it comes gift-wrapped from
the scholarship's own sceptic. DeVries — whose resistance to
military-revolution determinism is on record — examined political
control of gunpowder weapons in France, Burgundy and England, and
found in the first two "a pattern showing the decline of feudalism
and the rise of the central state… Local control and ownership of
these weapons was removed by the central state, which would later
return to use the weapons against those who had once owned them"
[^11-29]. England ran the opposite experiment. The
English crown had *early and exclusive* royal control of guns —
from Edward III through Henry V, "the king alone had control… and
he used them in an almost absolute manner" — and then, in the
mid-century crisis this book's Chapters 5 and 7 narrated, lost it:
royal artillery control collapsed with the rest of the hollow
crown's executive capacity, private artillery appeared in
subjects' quarrels — the sieges of Caister and the fight at Nibley
Green, gunfire between English gentlemen's retinues, a scene
Chapter 8's legal world makes comprehensible — and, in DeVries's
summary of Henry VI, "after almost fifty years of civil war not
only had he lost control over gunpowder holdings in his kingdom,
he had in fact lost his kingdom" [^11-30]. England had
the monopoly without the fiscal-administrative substrate; the
monopoly did not survive contact with dynastic crisis. Three
counter-cases, three missing parts, three failures on schedule:
Burgundy had the apparatus without the survivable centre; the
Swiss had the victories without the compelling technology; England
had the monopoly without the fisc. The triad — capability,
finance, administration — fails if any leg fails. That is what a
mechanism looks like from its wreckage.

## VII.

The chain can now be stated compressed, and — this book's recurring
evidentiary move — stated almost entirely in the words of the
scholars who resist its stronger versions.

Artillery gravitated to the centre because of what it cost: "too
expensive for the nobility to purchase, and hence tended to become
a monopoly of the Crown. The superior military technology of the
day both gravitated to and reinforced the political center"
[^11-31]. The centre that acquired it
entered a self-reinforcing cycle: trains suppressed regional
powers, suppression raised revenue, revenue bought larger trains
and armies, "and so on" [^11-32].
And the capability at the cycle's heart was categorically
state-sized: the artillery revolution "could not be provided by
any other organisation than the state, or at least not at the
level required to make an impact" [^11-33]. Rogers the
punctuationist, DeVries the anti-determinist, Curry the gradualist
— the three positions in the military-revolution debate, each
conceding the fiscal-threshold mechanism while contesting the
framing around it. Even the neomedieval literature's own founder
attests the chain from the far side: Cerny locates the end of the
first durable disorder in "the creative destruction of fifteenth
to seventeenth century warfare," under the exogenous pressure of
"the inter-dynastic struggles of the Hundred Years' War,"
producing states that "could consolidate because they were
multitasking and because they could afford to make increasing
side-payments" [^11-34]. The book's mechanism
chapter closes its historical half on that convergence: the
account of how the first dispersal ended is not this book's
novelty; it is the shared premise of the sceptics, stated in
their several vocabularies. 

Curry's causal reversal deserves its own answer rather than
absorption, because it is the strongest form of the objection:
strong, well-resourced governments produce military revolutions,
she argues — "not the other way around" [^11-35]. The
chapter's answer, held since Chapter 6's review: the chain is a
loop with a threshold step. The fiscal instruments demonstrably
predate the triumphs — the taille grant is 1439, the companies
1445, the reconquest 1449–50 — so the book does not claim the gun
conjured the fisc from nothing. It claims the gun did two things
no earlier capability had done: it set the entry price of
decisive force above what any subject could pay [^11-36], and it made the
crown's pre-existing fiscal machinery *decisive and irreversible*
— locking the loop in one direction. The four polities of this
chapter are the loop's proof by variation: France ran it entire;
Burgundy ran it without a survivable centre, and the loop's
output was inherited by its neighbours; the Swiss won outside the
loop and built nothing; England broke the loop at the fiscal link
and lost the kingdom. One mechanism, three failure modes, one
success — and the success became the template Europe copied for
three centuries.

## VIII.

[BRIDGE — this closing section is the chapter's translation into the
present; the synthesis is tagged (c) throughout — the components are
primary-documented and dated, the four-polity conclusion is the
book's own — and the analogy joints are Roderick's to set.]

Run the 1442 test against the present. The test, recall, was a
budget document: a state pricing a decisive capability against its
traditional arsenal and finding the new item costing twice the old —
and paying it, annually, because the capability could not be
improvised, stockpiled cheaply, or raised by summons. The question
for our own decade is which polities carry the equivalent lines: 
decisive-capability expenditure, in the central budget, at a scale
no subordinate authority could sustain, across the four layers of
what Part III has called the stack — the foundry, the orbital
layer, the munitions base, and frontier compute.

The foundry line is the clearest, and it has recently escalated
past subsidy into something older. The United States legislated the
CHIPS and Science Act on 9 August 2022 [^11-37]: $52.7
billion appropriated for semiconductors — $39 billion in
manufacturing incentives, $11 billion for R&D, a 25 per cent
investment credit — with awards finalised in late 2024 to Intel
($7.86 billion, plus a separate Secure Enclave award with the
Defense Department of up to $3 billion), TSMC Arizona ($6.6 billion
with $5 billion in loans), Samsung, Micron and GlobalFoundries, and
statutory guardrails prohibiting recipients' material expansion in
China for ten years. Then, on 22 August 2025, the pattern crossed a
threshold the fifteenth century would have recognised at once: the
United States converted $8.9 billion of grants into equity —
roughly 10 per cent of Intel, with a warrant over a further 5 per
cent tied to Intel's keeping control of its foundry business. The
artillery state bought guns; this state is buying the foundry. The
European Union's equivalent (Regulation 2023/1781, in force 21
September 2023) decomposes honestly into about €35 billion of
member-state aid plus matching, with the Commission directly
controlling only some €4.5 billion — and the flagship Magdeburg
fab, with its €10 billion German subsidy, was cancelled in July
2025. China's Big Fund reached its third phase in May 2024 — RMB
344 billion, about $47.5 billion, the largest yet, the Ministry of
Finance the lead shareholder — against an American export-control
regime (the October 2022 BIS rule and its expansions) that
constitutes the negative image of the same state programme:
one polity financing its foundry layer, another using
administrative power to deny a rival's. The concentration being
contested is extreme by any historical standard: as of the
industry's 2021 baseline, "100% of the world's most advanced
(below 10 nanometers) semiconductor manufacturing capacity is
currently located in Taiwan (92%) and South Korea (8%)" [^11-38] — a snapshot the CHIPS programmes exist to erode —
and the lithography beneath the leading edge has exactly one
supplier on earth, no EUV system ever having shipped to China.

The orbital line: two polities today fly proliferated
military-relevant constellations in low earth orbit — the United
States [^11-39] and China (Qianfan and Guowang, dual-use state
systems, some four hundred satellites between them and plans in
the tens of thousands). Europe has signed a concession — IRIS²,
€10.6 billion, December 2024 — for services from about 2030. The
munitions line carries this section's honest counter-weight, and
the book keeps it deliberately: the American 155mm shell ramp,
from roughly 14,500 a month before 2022 toward a 100,000 goal,
stood at about 36,000 actual monthly output as of March 2026 by
the Pentagon Inspector General's finding — the state's own
industrial-strategy document having already conceded, in the
first National Defense Industrial Strategy (January 2024), that
the base "does not possess the capacity, capability,
responsiveness, or resilience required to satisfy the full range
of military production needs at speed and scale." The
artillery-state response is observable; its execution is
struggling — a gap the fifteenth century also knew, and one
Appendix C tracks as an indicator rather than buries. And the
compute line runs from the unambiguous (El Capitan, the
weapons-lab machine that led the world's rankings from November
2024 at 1.742 exaFLOPS, its stated mission the stewardship of
the nuclear deterrent) to the genuinely anomalous, and the
anomaly is stated plainly because the chapter's credibility
depends on it: frontier AI compute is majority-privately
financed — the frontier-lab anomaly Chapters 3 and 4 flagged —
and private American AI investment ($109.1 billion in 2024,
against China's $9.3 billion) dwarfs the public lines. The
book does not claim the modern stack is state-financed
throughout; it claims the *military-relevant* stack is
state-financed at threshold scale, and that the private
frontier is an unresolved instability in the pattern, not a
refutation of it — tracked, not overclaimed.

Score the four layers and the four-polity conclusion follows —
as the book's own synthesis, offered for falsification. Two
polities clearly carry all four lines: the United States and
China. One carries them partially, and by canonical rather than
military instruments — the European Union, its foundry flagship
cancelled, its constellation contracted for the decade's end,
its munitions instrument counting capacity rather than output; a
special case Part III treats on its own terms. One is
conditional: India, assembling the fiscal and legibility
apparatus faster than the military-industrial one — a
$25-billion semiconductor mission building a 28-nanometre-class
fab a decade behind the leading edge, a space budget an order of
magnitude below the American, no proliferated military
constellation; "possibly," as the outline says, and no more.
The rest of the world's polities will buy, license, or align —
which is Chapter 6's capitulation-without-siege operating as
procurement policy, and the sentence that thought is resting on
has already been earned: where the price of decisive capability
exceeds what a polity can pay, the rational act is composition
with a polity that can.

The fifteenth-century half of this chapter ended with an
instrument no coalition could break, and the phrase was
Contamine's, describing what the French account books had built
by 1494. The modern half ends earlier in the arc — nearer 1445
than 1494: the programmes legislated, the accounts opened, the
first awards finalised, the execution uneven, the outcome
unproven. What the mechanism predicts from here — which
settlements, inside which blocs, on what timetable, and what
becomes of the polities and authorities that cannot meet the
price — is the business of the two chapters that remain: first
the five moves by which the last consolidators actually
consolidated (Chapter 11), and then the wager itself (Chapter
12). The account books are open. The question is who can keep
paying.

# Chapter 11 — The New Monarchies

## I.

In the last years of Henry VII's reign, George Neville, Lord
Bergavenny — a great lord of the Kentish marches — was indicted for
retaining four hundred and seventy-one men in his livery, contrary to
the statute of 1504, which had made retaining lawful only "by the
vertue of the kynges plagart," the king's licence. At the statutory
rate of five pounds per man per month, the fine assessed came to
£70,650 — a sum comparable to a year of the crown's whole ordinary
revenue, a sum no subject in England could pay. It was never meant to
be paid. On Christmas Eve the fine was commuted to £500 a year for
ten years, and Bergavenny was forbidden to enter Kent, Surrey, Sussex
or Hampshire — the counties of his power. He paid two instalments.
When the old king died, the new one cancelled the rest [^12-1]. Observe the
instrument in full: an impossible debt, lawfully assessed, deliberately
suspended — a fine never meant to be collected, only to convert a
magnate into a bondholder of his own obedience.

That instrument, in its dozens of iterations, is what consolidation
actually looked like from inside — and the man who operated it has
long had a historiographical family. The name was coined in the
Victorian synthesis: J. R. Green wrote of "the New Monarchy," the
character of "the English sovereignty from the time of Edward the
Fourth to the time of Elizabeth" [^12-2], and the early twentieth
century generalised it — Henry VII, Louis XI, Ferdinand and Isabella
as one centralising moment. Modern scholarship has qualified the
frame heavily, and this book declares its position at the outset: the
"New Monarchies" are contested historiography, not settled fact; the
five-move schema this chapter runs across them is the book's own
synthesis, marked as such; and the inclusion of the Ottoman state —
which the Latin-Christendom-bound literature never admitted — is the
book's own extension. What the chapter claims is narrower than the
old textbook narrative and sharper: between roughly 1470 and 1530,
four polities — England, France, Spain, and the Ottoman Empire —
executed documentable variants of the same five moves, and the moves
are the settlement that Part II's mechanism predicts. Fiscal reform;
subordination of great subjects; domestication of the market for
force; canonical assertion over rival jurisdictions; and the
mobilisation of the age's new communications technology for
legibility and doctrine. The chapter runs the five comparatively,
and then — the book's predictive centre — runs them again, against
the present, from the current instruments' own texts.

## II.

Henry VII first, because he was the technician — less spectacular
than the universal spider, and institutionally more instructive.

The fiscal move, in its English form, was the Chamber: from about
1487 the King's Chamber displaced the Exchequer as the working
treasury of the realm — "in effect, a national treasury," in the
modern project's description — under treasurers Lovell and then
Heron, with the distinguishing feature that the king audited it
personally: Henry's own sign manual runs through the account books,
signing off payments weekly, the sovereign as his own comptroller
[^12-3]. The paradox is the
chapter's first lesson: the fisc was *personalised* — routed
through the king's private rooms — precisely in order to be
institutionalised, pulled out of the old departmental inertia into
an apparatus the centre could actually steer.

The subordination move ran through paper. Lander's corrected
tallies: of sixty-two peerage families, thirty-six gave bonds or
recognizances to the crown — well over half the peerage bound —
and some three-quarters lay under one coercive instrument or
another: bond, attainder, fine or constraint [^12-4]. The machine's administrative organ
was the Council Learned in the Law, sitting from the mid-1490s
under Bray and then Empson and Dudley, processing the bonds
without appeal. And the machine's political price was paid in
the succession: Henry VIII opened his reign by arresting Empson
and Dudley within days (24 April 1509) and executing them the
following year — the successor sacrificing his father's
instruments while retaining, to the letter, his father's
machine. Bergavenny's cancelled fine and Dudley's head were the
same transaction seen from opposite ends: the new king buying
legitimacy with the old king's enforcers, and keeping the
enforcement.

France's limb is established — the reader holds it from Chapter
10 — and needs only its extension: the mature apparatus taken
abroad. Charles VIII crossed the Alps in 1494 with the
institutions this book has audited — the standing companies, the
tripled fisc, the artillery at eight per cent of military
expenditure — and Giovio watched the train enter Rome at night,
"more than 36 pieces that the horses drew at an incredible
speed"; Contamine's verdict, already quoted and worth its
repetition here, was that the young king believed he possessed
"an instrument no coalition could break" [^12-5]. The first continental adventure of the
completed fiscal-military state: the settlement exporting
itself, a generation after its assembly.

## III.

Spain executed the same moves through the rival universalism's
own legal forms — absorption performed with the Church's own
silverware — and the Spanish cases are the chapter's cleanest
demonstrations that the settlement's characteristic method was
annexation, not demolition.

The domestication of local order: at the Cortes of Madrigal in
1476 the Catholic Monarchs reorganised the old municipal
brotherhoods into the Santa Hermandad — a crown-run constabulary
under a royal council, which policed the roads, and, not
incidentally, financed the Granada war; when the work was done,
the central council was dissolved (1498). A royal instrument
stood up, used, and wound down: the crown borrowing the
communes' own institution, running it at national scale, and
retiring it — capability absorbed, no standing rival created.

The military orders: Santiago, Calatrava and Alcántara were the
wealthiest corporate militaries in Iberia — crusading
institutions with lands, fortresses, revenues and elective
masterships, exactly the over-mighty corporate subjects the
consolidating crown could least tolerate. They were absorbed in
two steps, both papal. First, the administratorships came to
Ferdinand personally as the masterships fell vacant — Calatrava
in 1487, Alcántara under a bull of 1492, Santiago in 1499 (the
date this book carries against the older 1476 tradition). Then,
a generation later, Adrian VI's bull of 1523 annexed all three
masterships to the crown of Castile in perpetuity. No battle,
no dissolution, no confiscation statute: the rival's own
supreme authority — the papacy — issued the instruments by
which the rival's Iberian arms became crown patronage forever.

And the canonical move proper: the Inquisition. Sixtus IV's
bull *Exigit sincerae devotionis* (1 November 1478) granted the
monarchs the exclusive authority to name the inquisitors —
canonical machinery, royally staffed; the first tribunal sat at
Seville within two years; Torquemada, Inquisitor General from
1483, extended a single royally-appointed hierarchy across
Castile *and* Aragon — making the Holy Office the only
institution besides the monarchy itself that operated in every
Spanish kingdom. The chapter asserts the institution from its
founding bull and asserts no casualty figures — the scale
literature is gated and contested [GAP: Kamen; Contreras &
Henningsen] — and records the uniformity-and-legibility
extremity of the Spanish variant in its dated instruments: the
Alhambra Decree of 1492, the conversion pragmatic of 1502,
their demographic arithmetic contested and flagged. A church
court under royal appointment, spanning kingdoms the crown's
own lay institutions could not span: the canonical assertion,
executed as annexation.

## IV.

The Ottoman variant closes the comparative set because it is
the limit case — the most complete answer to the over-mighty
subject the fifteenth century produced anywhere.

The instrument was the *devşirme*: the periodic levy of
Christian boys — canonically one per forty households —
converted, cut from family and region, and trained: the
strongest into the Janissary corps (its strength under Mehmed
II roughly five to ten thousand, the register-based counts at
the lower end of the traditions), the ablest through the palace
schools into the administration itself, as *kul* — slaves of
the Porte, whose whole existence ran from the sultan's
household and whose loyalty ran to the office that had made
them. Where Henry VII bonded his magnates and Ferdinand
absorbed his orders, Mehmed II manufactured his service class
from raw material, and owed his nobility nothing because he
had none. The decisive act came in the conquest year: on 1
June 1453, three days after Constantinople fell, Mehmed
executed his grand vizier Çandarlı Halil Pasha — the first
grand vizier in Ottoman history to be executed, and the last
of the old aristocratic vizieral dynasty — and replaced him
with the kul Zaganos Pasha. After 1453, in the open
scholarship's formulation, the sultans stopped appointing
scholars and aristocrats to the vizierate, "preferring viziers
of kul… origin": the over-mighty-subject problem solved by
decapitating not the subject but the office's aristocratic
tenure itself. The dynasty codified its own logic in the
kanunname attributed to Mehmed, whose most notorious clause
licenses fratricide "for nizām-i 'ālem" — for the order of the
world — a text whose authenticity was long contested (Dilger
and Berki the named sceptics) and is now generally accepted
[^12-6]; and it reached for
the fiscal base as well, converting private and endowed lands
toward state land for redistribution as service tenures — with
the revisionist caveat carried as this book carries such
things: much "confiscation" altered revenue status rather than
ownership [^12-7], and Bayezid II partially restored what
his father had taken. The disanalogy is stated plainly: the
devşirme presupposed an imperial-household logic — the ruler's
slave-family as the state — that no Latin monarchy possessed
or could have adopted; the Ottoman case is in this chapter not
as an option the West declined but as the demonstration of the
pattern's outer bound. [GAP: İnalcık's framing and the
per-levy figures await retrieval; the mechanism is carried
from open T2.]

## V.

The fifth move needs a technology, and the technology arrived on
schedule.

Gutenberg's press was working at Mainz by the early 1450s — the
42-line Bible complete by 1455, two years after Constantinople and
Castillon, a synchrony this book notes and does not convert into a
connection — and the diffusion ran at the pace of a conquest:
presses in roughly 250 to 280 towns by 1500 (the open counts vary
and are given as a range); some 27,500 incunabula editions in the
international catalogue; total output in the first half-century
somewhere between fifteen and twenty million copies [GAP: the
Febvre & Martin volume behind the totals is on the acquisition
list]. That print could be *politically* decisive was demonstrated
within a generation of our period's close, and the demonstration
is quantified in open scholarship: Luther's works alone made up
roughly a fifth of all pamphlets published in the Empire between
1500 and 1530, and the German presses "produced nearly five times
as many German works by Luther as by all the Catholic
controversialists put together" [^12-8]. But the state's
harness is the chapter's subject, and England documents it
statute by statute. The Act of Supremacy (November 1534) made the
crown the head of the Church; Cromwell's injunctions of 5
September 1538 ordered "one book of the whole Bible of the
largest volume in English" set up in every parish church; the
Great Bible followed within the year (April 1539); and the royal
proclamation of 16 November 1538 required pre-publication
licensing of English books — the state, in a single
twelve-month span, *mandating* the vernacular canon and
*licensing* the means of its reproduction. The Stationers'
charter (1557) then delegated enforcement to a chartered guild;
Rome's mirror-move, the Index, followed in 1559. The argument,
and it is the book's: print did for the sixteenth-century state
what artillery had done for the fifteenth — it changed the
economics of political community, and only polities that could
license, mandate and administer the new medium at national
scale could exploit it. Henry VIII's supremacy — the state
swallowing the Church's functions, Chapter 2's foreshadowed
settlement — was *executable* because print had made a national
vernacular religion administrable: the canon could be
standardised, distributed, placed in every parish and policed.
The great historiographical quarrel over print — Eisenstein's
revolutionary "print culture" against Johns's insistence that
fixity and credibility were made by practice, not by the press
itself — is in this book's corpus from the protagonists' own
AHR exchange, and it is staged here the way this book stages
such quarrels, because it has the now-familiar shape: Johns
grants that standardisation, output and fixity were "precisely
the elements" Eisenstein's book articulated, and re-attributes
their production. The dispute is over *how* print's effects
were made, not whether the state could harness them — and the
harness is all this chapter needs. The sceptics concede again.

The five moves can now be restated as the settlement's pattern,
with the book's ownership declared: fiscal reform — permanent
central taxation with an administrative apparatus; subordination
of great subjects — by bond, annexation, or manufactured
replacement; domestication of the market for force — by
licence, absorption, or monopoly purchase; canonical assertion
over rival jurisdictions — church, orders, franchises, cities;
and mobilisation of the new communications technology for
doctrine and legibility. Between roughly 1470 and 1530, England,
France, Spain and the Ottoman state each executed variants; the
Habsburg composite followed its own path a generation later. The
schema is the book's synthesis (c), its ancestry in Green's
contested "New Monarchy" declared, its Ottoman extension its
own. What makes it more than taxonomy is Part II standing behind
it: each move spends the fiscal-military surplus the artillery
state generated, and none was executable without it.

## VII.

The prediction can now be stated in the form the whole book
has been assembling, and with the legal refinement the
threads were carried to deliver.

The consolidating states will not break their platforms.
They will nationalise their *functions* — by
security-clearance and procurement integration in the United
States; by canonical regulation in Europe; by fused
ownership in China; by sovereign-stack substitution in
India — while the platforms' corporate forms persist, as the
Church persisted after Henry: intact, wealthy, and
subordinate in the functions that matter. And the ch04
thread now specifies the settlement's legal texture. The
absorbed platforms may remain *operative but never
invested* — running state-critical infrastructure under
licence, commission and programme of record, without ever
receiving the formal standing that would make their position
constitutional: no golden shares, revocable directives,
commissions in the reserve. Sforza ruled Milan for sixteen
years while the empire withheld the investiture; the
platforms may run the stack for decades on the same terms.
The Anthropic episode shows the converse edge, and the book
keeps both: the state withheld investiture *and was
compelled by its own courts to keep the relationship
operative* — function delegated, legitimacy retained, on
both sides, neither able to complete the divorce. That is
what "leashed rather than destroyed" looks like when it is
written down in government releases and docket entries. The
five moves are observable in every bloc at the instrument
level; the interstitial spaces — the zones the settlements
will not reach — stay neomedieval, and Chapter 12 maps
them. One question remains, and it is the book's last
historical question asked of the present: every settlement
in this chapter had a consolidator — a Henry, a Louis, a
Ferdinand, a Mehmed — and an instrument whose economics
forced the pace. Who, then, is Henry VII — and what is his
artillery?

# Chapter 12 — Not Modernity, But Tudor

## I.

In the first days of March 2025, the best-armed cheap army in the
history of war discovered what it could not do alone.

The sequence ran inside a single week, and it is drafted here as the
dated episode it was: on 3 March the United States suspended military
aid to Ukraine; on or about the 5th, intelligence sharing was paused —
the CIA director's own word; on the 8th, the National
Geospatial-Intelligence Agency cut Ukrainian access to US-purchased
commercial satellite imagery. Within days the effects were tactical
and specific: HIMARS targeting degraded, Patriot cueing impaired — the
precision instruments of a military that had made itself the world's
laboratory of drone warfare, blunted not by any enemy action but by a
patron's pause. Restoration followed the Jeddah statement on 11 March
[GAP: pin the Jeddah wording at press]. Ukraine flew its millions of
drones that week as before. What the week demonstrated was the layer
the drones fly *within*: the communications backbone (some 42,000
Starlink terminals, "the essential backbone" in the standard
description), the satellite reconnaissance, the interceptor stocks,
the deep-strike permissions granted in November 2024 and revocable at
will — all of it another polity's property. A drone-rich army learned,
in one week, that its cheap strike complex aimed through an ally's
targeting layer, and that the layer had an owner.

Hold that week; the chapter returns to it, because it is the closest
thing this decade has produced to a controlled experiment on the
book's central question. This chapter is the synthesis, and its
burden is to state — plainly, falsifiably, and within the disciplines
every prior chapter has established — what the whole argument
predicts. The mechanism is behind us: the fifteenth century's
repricing of decisive force (Chapter 6), the fiscal-military state it
compelled (Chapter 10), the five-move settlement the new monarchies
executed (Chapter 11). The diagnosis is behind us: the dispersal,
the churches, the retinues, the contract polities, the hollow crowns
(Part I; Chapter 5). What remains is the wager: that the same
mechanism, operating now, produces not a restored Westphalia and not
a stable neomedieval plateau but three or four parallel Tudor
settlements, executed inside civilisational blocs, with durable
disorder persisting in the spaces between. Not modernity, but Tudor.

## II.

The settlement, seen from outside, is already legible in the blocs'
own declarations — and the variable whose absence defined the
neomedieval diagnosis has returned to the books.

Cerny's second characteristic, the reader will recall from Chapter
1, was the *lack* of exogenous territorialising pressure — his own
referent for the pressure being "the inter-dynastic struggles of the
Hundred Years' War." Audit the mid-2020s against it. At The Hague,
in June 2025, the Atlantic alliance pledged its members to "invest
5% of GDP annually on core defence requirements as well as defence-
and security-related spending by 2035" [^13-1] —
a level unseen since the Cold War's depths. The European Union
announced ReArm Europe: up to €800 billion — €650 billion of fiscal
flexibility and €150 billion of SAFE lending, disbursing from early
2026 [^13-2]. China's fifteenth Five-Year Plan (March
2026) headlines "greater self-reliance and strength in science and
technology" (primary); the American chip-policy turn of 2026 pairs
eased licensing with tariff instruments on advanced imports (carried
at T3/T4 — browser pins owed; flagged). And the decade's sharpest
exogenous datum now has its documentary record [RE-CHECK AT PRESS —
the whole passage; source: CRS R48887, "U.S. Conflict with Iran,"
26 March 2026, via the EveryCRSReport mirror, to be pinned against
the congress.gov original]: the United States struck three Iranian
nuclear facilities on 22 June 2025, absorbed Iran's retaliatory
missile attack on Al Udeid the next day, and — after the Tehran
demonstrations of December 2025 — opened, with Israel, a full
campaign against Iranian targets on 28 February 2026, in which the
Supreme Leader was killed; by 25 March Central Command reported
more than 10,000 targets struck, and the operation's cost, by the
official figure the CRS report carries, exceeded "$11.3 billion in
the first six days." A great-power war of exactly the kind the
neomedieval diagnosis's second characteristic said had left the
system: the pressure is not returning; it has returned. The pattern
across every bloc at once: rearmament, industrial-base panic,
frontier technology treated as sovereign capacity — the exogenous
consolidating pressure, back on the ledgers. Chapter 1 marked its
absence as the diagnosis's weakest joint and predicted its return
would announce the pendulum. The announcements are now annual
budget lines.

Between the consolidating blocs, the interstices persist, and the
book's frame requires their persistence be stated as prediction,
not embarrassment: the territorial hollow sovereignties of Chapter
5, the alternatively governed zones, the grey economies whose
untaxed fraction shrinks while their stock endures — durable
disorder, fenced rather than abolished, patrolled at the edges by
settlements that have no fiscal reason to absorb it. The Tudor
state, the reader will recall, never governed the marches evenly
either; it built councils *for* them.

## III.

Seen from inside, the settlement's texture is already documented,
and three of its features can be stated from instruments the prior
chapters put in evidence.

Its law is hierarchically plural — Chapter 8's finding, now
statutory. The plural fora persist: arbitration at record caseloads,
platform adjudication at nine-figure annual volume, the private
orderings of code and contract. Above them, the state has written
its supremacy into the instruments themselves: compulsory internal
process, certified external bodies expressly denied binding power
[^13-3]; and the hierarchy enforced in both directions —
the same season's docket showing the state fining its platforms
and the state's own exclusion directive enjoined by its own courts
(the Anthropic arc, Chapter 11). Hierarchical plurality is not a
compromise on the way to monopoly; it is the settlement's mature
legal form, as it was the Tudor form — church courts and manorial
courts and merchant courts all surviving, ranked.

Its legibility is infrastructural and persistent. The automatic
exchange of financial information across 116 jurisdictions and 171
million accounts; a subcontinent transacting twenty billion
identity-linked payments a month; dollar legibility routed through
regulated private stablecoin rails under the GENIUS Act; the
e-CNY's operations centre; the identity stacks. The medieval
subject met the state's gaze episodically — at the assize, the
muster, the tithe barn; his modern successor is registered
continuously. The disanalogy is stated with the structural point:
registration is not the levy, audit is not the auto-da-fé, and the
comparison licenses nothing beyond the observation that uniform
legibility is a governing *capacity* — the capacity the
Inquisition and the devşirme built by force and the API builds by
default [ANALOGY-ONLY beyond that point].

And its summit is personal, atop bureaucracies that are
improvising counterweights — Chapter 9's two-body problem, live:
the dual-class charter as the default constitution of the new
institutions; the compelled independent compliance function [^13-4]; succession reclassified as governance; the regency
instruments existing, acting, and — the OpenAI week — failing
against the person. The settlement's legal form for its absorbed
platforms carries the thread the book promoted from Milan:
*operative but never invested* — function delegated through
licence, clearance, commission and programme of record;
legitimacy retained by the state; and, the Anthropic episode's
converse lesson, the courts holding both sides to the bargain,
the state unable to complete the expulsion, the platform unable
to compel the investiture. Sforza ruled Milan for sixteen years
on those terms. The platforms should study the case.

## IV.

Who, then, is Henry VII — and what is his artillery? The casting
is the book's own (c); the components are the prior chapters'
documented record; and the four candidacies resolve unevenly.

The United States is the most probable case, and the least
self-aware: the five moves executed haphazardly, half of them
under doctrines that would deny the pattern. Its fisc extends
through its own instruments while exempting itself from everyone
else's — the stablecoin rails legislated, the global minimum tax
carved out for its own parented groups. Its subordination runs
through its own courts, wins and losses alike — remedies against
Google, contempt against Apple, defeat in the Meta case — the
crown ruled by the law it rules through. Its market for force is
domesticated by procurement and personnel, without an equity
stake in the gunmen — though with one, now, in the foundry. Its
canonical assertion turns inward against its own states as much
as outward. And its artillery is the stack Chapter 10 audited:
the CHIPS programme with its ten per cent of the national
champion, the Secure Enclave, the weapons-lab exaflops, the
proliferated constellations, the munitions ramp with its
shortfall honestly audited by its own inspector general. Henry
VII kept his own books; the American Henry publishes his, which
is not the same discipline, but is a discipline.

China has substantially executed the settlement already — the
discipline of 2020–23 completed and normalised in sequence, the
fused-ownership absorption mode, the legibility instruments, the
canon now exported through a treaty organisation with
twenty-nine signatures — and its candidacy is qualified mainly
by the question this book cannot answer from documents: whether
a settlement executed *as* personal rule reproduces the
two-body fragility Part II diagnosed. The European Union is the
hard case, and the book's own analytical instrument — the
Depreter refinement, carried from the Burgundian accounts —
governs the assessment. Burgundy taught that apparatus without
a continuous dynastic-political centre does not survive
contact. The EU is the refinement's harder application:
canonically the strongest actor of the four — the most
developed regulatory apparatus on earth, upheld by its own
courts — and structurally the weakest: no single fisc, no
single executive, its dynasty a treaty; its flagship fab
cancelled, its constellation contracted for the decade's end,
its munitions instrument counting capacity, its high-risk
canon deferred to 2027–28, its enforcement powers held
unexercised. The observable counter-moves are real and are
named as such — ReArm, the five per cent pledge, SAFE, the
own-resource proposals: the substrate being built, under
exactly the exogenous pressure the theory requires. The
assessment therefore stands as a live conditional, scoreable
in Appendix C's terms: either the assertion-to-enforcement
lag closes as the fisc arrives, or the Union completes its
century as the American bloc's canonical province —
Burgundy's apparatus, inherited by the neighbour with the
centre. And India is the possible fourth: the most complete
population-legibility stack outside China, running a decade
ahead of its military-industrial one; conditional, as every
chapter has held. Everyone else buys, licenses, aligns, or
inhabits the interstices — Chapter 6's
capitulation-without-siege, operating as procurement policy.

## V.

The strongest objection to everything above wears a drone's
rotors, and the chapter meets it with the full Ukraine record —
kept, per this book's measurement discipline, in three distinct
numbers that must never be blended.

The low end of violence has decentralised, massively and
genuinely. Ukraine produced 2.2 million drones in 2024
[^13-5] and received some three million FPVs
in 2025 against a 4.5-million procurement plan [^13-6]; a basic FPV runs a few hundred dollars. Their
share of the war's effects is measured three ways, each
attributed and dated: tactical UAVs "account for 60–70% of
damaged and destroyed Russian systems" — a *materiel* claim
[^13-7]; roughly 70 per cent of *casualties*, per a Rada
defence-committee official and, at 60–70 per cent, front-line
medics [^13-8]; and over
60 per cent of all *strikes* [^13-9]. Systems,
casualties, strikes: three measurements, three sources, one
conclusion — the cheap end of decisive violence has been
democratised to the parish level, and any account of the
present that ignores it is propaganda for the big platforms.

Two facts stand against the inference the objection wants, and
the first is that the longbow at scale is itself an
industrial-state product. Russia's Shahed campaign scaled from
some two hundred launches a week to more than a thousand by
March 2025 [^13-10]; the Alabuga complex
documents a six-thousand-drone production programme [^13-11]; the night of 6–7 September 2025 put
805 drones into the air — "the largest attack since the
beginning of the full-scale invasion" [^13-12] —
and five-hundred-drone nights are routine. Both sides' cheap
drone forces are mass-production programmes of fiscal-military
states — the modern francs-archers, raised by the parish, paid
and standardised by the crown — and cheapness per unit is not
cheapness as a system: attritional war, in Vershinin's
formulation, tests a country's industrial capacity. The second
fact is the week this chapter opened with. The decisive layer —
communications, reconnaissance, interceptors, permissions —
remains concentrated, patron-owned, and revocable on days'
notice: the Patriot stockpile at roughly a quarter of the
Pentagon's own planning requirement (reported July 2025),
production in the hundreds per year; the deep-strike
permissions granted and withdrawable; the sovereign
substitutes real but partial (the Flamingo's production
figures are the maker's claims, flagged). The analytical
statement is Bronk and Watling's, and it is quotable because
it is careful: UAVs "may redistribute the balance of
missions… but they do not eliminate the requirement for
traditional artillery," and a mass precision strike complex
"is not just a question of UAVs… EW, geospatial and other ISR
assets are fundamental." The counter-position — Hammes's
small, smart and many against the few and exquisite — is
staged fairly and answered on two fronts: Ukraine's longbow
has raised the price of aggression without defeating a
full-stack adversary, and the cheap layer's own scaling
re-concentrates it into industrial policy. The possibility
that open models and distributed compute erode the decisive
layer is real, unproven, and scoreable — Appendix C keeps it
honest.

And the concentrated layer now has its own direct exhibit,
integrated on review and carried with this book's measurement
discipline — every figure attributed, categories never blended
[RE-CHECK AT PRESS; CRS R48887 throughout]. In the Iranian
campaign of February–March 2026, a full-stack power struck, by
its own combatant command's report, more than 10,000 targets in
under a month; the president's claim — quoted as a claim — was
"complete and total control of the skies over Iran"; the
operation's official cost exceeded $11.3 billion in its first
six days; American fatalities stood at 13 service personnel (as
of 23 March), against more than 3,000 Iranian fatalities
*reported* — a claim category, not a count. Targets struck,
costs, own casualties, enemy casualties: four different kinds of
number, from four different kinds of source, and the book keeps
them separate. What the exhibit shows, within those limits, is
the asymmetry's other face: where Ukraine demonstrates that the
cheap layer cannot decide without the concentrated one, Iran
demonstrates what the concentrated layer does when it decides —
at a six-day burn rate approaching the annual defence budgets
of mid-sized states, which is itself the chapter's point: the
deciding layer is priced where only artillery states shop. The
answer to the military-technology question is
therefore asymmetric, and it is this chapter's compressed
finding: distributed denial below, concentrated decision
above. The Cade rebellions and the artillery states of our
age, running simultaneously — which is exactly what the
fifteenth century looked like from inside.

## VI.

The book can now state what it claims to have added, as claims.

First: the platforms treated as institutional universalisms —
canon, sanction, jurisdiction, documented from their founding
instruments (Chapter 2) — with the state's answer documented in
the same register (Chapter 11): absorption of functions,
executed differently per bloc, in the operative-but-never-
invested legal form. That is the treatment Kobrin's 1998
insight pointed toward and the literature never wrote. Second:
the re-consolidation claimed with a mechanism rather than a
mood — the priced-sovereignty chain of Chapters 6, 10 and 11 —
and attested, at every contested joint, from inside the
objections: Rogers supplying the artillery-tax-centralisation
cycle while ranking the revolutions otherwise; DeVries
conceding France and Burgundy conform to the pattern he
resists; Curry conceding the state-only provider inside her
reversal of the arrow; Cerny locating the first consolidation
in the very warfare this book's Part II reconstructs. The
sceptics-concede thread is the book's evidentiary signature,
and it is offered as method, not as trick: an argument
assembled from its critics' concessions is an argument its
critics can only refine.

## VII.

*(The CLAUDE.md §9 boundary governs this section: comparative
historical analysis and asset-class direction; no actionable
guidance.)* What did the last Tudor settlement do to wealth?
The ledger is stark and it is the only forecast instrument
this book will hand its professional readers. Monastic wealth
— the largest corporate property system in England — was
dissolved and redistributed inside a decade. The franchise and
liberty jurisdictions, with their fee streams, were
extinguished. Chartered-company forms were invented where the
crown wanted capacity it could licence but not run. Position
migrated, in one generation, toward the crown-adjacent, the
licensed and the assessed — and away from the cross-border,
the immune and the interstitial. The directional analogues,
stated as pattern: in a parallel-settlements world, value
migrates toward bloc-interior, licensable,
procurement-adjacent positions and away from cross-bloc
arbitrage and interstitial jurisdictions; compliance and
reporting burdens function as the new tithe — resented,
universal, and constitutive of legitimacy; the licensing
premium — clearances, gatekeeper status, charters — becomes a
durable rent; and interstitial assets carry a durable-disorder
risk that consolidation fences without removing. For the
constituencies this book's author has spent a career briefing,
the fifteenth century's ledger is offered as the reference
class. Nothing more specific is offered, because nothing more
specific would be honest.

## VIII.

Return, last, to the field where the book began, better armed
now than at the first telling.

He was about sixty-six, not the legend's eighty. He had passed
through the machinery of the new order as its hostage, his
liberty a clause in a composition; he had given a promise to a
city because the city had opened a gate for him. On the
morning, he was hearing mass when the report came — the French
are abandoning their park; now or never — and the report was
false, and a man of his own nation who had seen much war told
him so to his face, and was struck for it. He charged a
fortified artillery position whose budget line ran back
through two account series to a procurement memorandum of
1442, written by the man whose counsel had sited the guns —
and the horse went down under him, and the age's most
celebrated soldier was killed on the ground by the cheapest
kind of man on the field. Pollard's factors — faulty
intelligence, momentum, honour, the position of his unfurled
banner — are the human causes, and this book has offered its
tagged hypothesis besides. But the structural cause was
stated by the whole of Part II: capital-intensive decisive
capability, requiring fiscal-administrative capacity the old
order could not raise, producing consolidation the old order
could not resist, ending a dispersal the old order was. That
is the pattern. It has run once, to completion, on the
record. The claim of this book is that it is running again —
inside blocs, not across them; through licences and
clearances as much as through guns; against churches made of
code and magnates made of capital — and that claim is
falsifiable, on schedule, by named indicators with named
baselines. They are collected in Appendix C. The reader who
would prove this book wrong now knows exactly where to
strike; the reader who finds, year by year, that the
indicators keep coming in — the pledges kept, the stacks
consolidated, the fines paid, the interstices fenced — will
know what season it is. Not modernity, but Tudor: the walls
have already been priced. What remains to be seen is only
who signs the compositions.

# Coda — A Note on Method and Sources

A book that predicts deserves to have its machinery inspected, and
this note exists so that the inspection need not be forensic. It
states what kind of claim the book makes, how the evidence was
tiered, what rules governed the comparisons, what the book does not
claim, and where the argument's founder was right and is respectfully
departed from.

## I. Mechanism under conditions, not determinism

The book's causal claim has a precise logical form, and everything
turns on it: a *mechanism under conditions*, not a law of history.
The mechanism — capital-intensification of decisive force compelling
permanent finance, permanent finance compelling administrative
capacity, administrative capacity suppressing intermediate
authorities — operated once, in the documented case Part II
reconstructs. The book's wager is that its enabling conditions have
recurred: a decisive capability whose price sits above what any
subject can pay; polities with latent fiscal-administrative capacity
to mobilise; and exogenous pressure sufficient to force the
mobilisation. Where the conditions fail, the mechanism idles — the
book's own counter-cases are the demonstration, and they were chosen
for that work: the Swiss won the battles and built no state, because
pike squares compelled no institutions; the Empire asserted the
monopoly of justice without the fiscal substrate and enforced it a
century late; Burgundy built the apparatus and lost the continuous
political centre, and the apparatus passed to its neighbours; England
held the gun monopoly without the fisc and lost both. One mechanism,
several failure modes, one completed run — that is the shape of a
conditional claim, and it is why the predictions of Part III are
conditional too: *if* the stacks continue to concentrate, *if* the
blocs sustain the fiscal effort, *then* the settlement pattern
follows. History does not repeat on command. Prices, however, are
facts, and the book's core empirical assertion — that the price of
decisive capability has moved beyond subordinate reach — is checkable
at any time against public budgets.

## II. The comparative register, stated as rule

No single-polity argument appears anywhere in the book; the rule was
doctrine before a word was drafted, and it is auditable. Every
English-leaning chapter carries at least two continental comparators:
Henry VI is read beside Charles VI and the Ottoman succession crises;
Cade beside the Praguerie, the Ciompi legacy and the German risings;
the Pastons beside the *Fehde* and the vendetta; the artillery state
is French, with Burgundy and the Swiss as counter-cases and England
demoted to the control experiment; the new monarchies run four
polities with the Ottomans included precisely because the older "New
Monarchy" literature excluded them. Appendix B tabulates the register
chapter by chapter. The rule's purpose is not decorative breadth. It
is the book's defence against its own convenience: a mechanism that
only worked in one kingdom would be a national history wearing a
theory; a mechanism whose failure modes appear exactly where its
conditions fail is a mechanism.

## III. The three-tier sourcing discipline

The book runs on three distinct bodies of evidence, and they were
never allowed to vouch for one another.

The *neomedieval spine* — Bull, Friedrichs, Cerny, Kobrin, Slaughter,
Kaplan, Williams, McFate — was read as a literature being extended,
and quoted from its own texts wherever they were in hand, with every
second-hand quotation named as such [^14-1]. The *medieval historiography* was held to the hardest
rule in the book: no gated work cited as if consulted; every claim
graded by tier; omission preferred to invention. Where the research
corrected the project's own working assumptions, the corrections are
in the text: the "sixty fortified places" of the early outline
became Rogers's sourced one hundred strongpoints with the
surrender-not-siege caveat that improved the argument; the Bureau
brothers were demoted from inventors to institutionalisers, which
strengthened the thesis; Formigny was downgraded from decision to
provocation; the drone-share figures of the last chapters are kept
as three separate measurements because that is what they are. And
the *contemporary record* was held to primary documents — statutes,
regulations, court filings, proxy statements, budget lines, the
institutions' own annual reports — with commentary as framing,
never as load-bearing support; every live episode is drafted as
dated events, attributed, and flagged for re-verification at press.
Two special disciplines ran across all three tiers. Foreign-language
sources are quoted verbatim in the original, with translations
either the scholarly edition's or marked as the pipeline's own for
checking. And a recurring evidentiary pattern was promoted, once
noticed, into the book's method: at every contested joint of the
medieval mechanism, the argument rests on the *sceptics'* own
concessions — Rogers's spiral, DeVries's conforming France and
Burgundy, Curry's state-only threshold, Cerny's fifteenth-century
attribution — so that what remains in dispute with the specialists
is ranking, tempo and sequence, never direction.

One disclosure belongs in this note as a matter of the same
discipline. The research and drafting pipeline for this book used
AI models, including Anthropic's; the Anthropic–Pentagon episode
discussed in Chapters 3 and 11 entered the book on documentary
criteria — government releases and docket entries — and carries the
standard live-event flag, like every other contemporary episode.
The reader should weigh the book's treatment of that episode with
the disclosure in view; the sourcing rules above were built so that
nothing in the treatment depends on trust.

## IV. The wager's narrowing — a delta table

The falsifiability passage in the Introduction is not the wager the
project began with; it is the wager the research left standing. The
narrowing was always in one direction — each condition tightened,
none loosened — and the method essay is the place to show the
deltas, because the direction of revision is itself evidence of how
the book was built.

| # | The outline's original condition | The narrowed condition as published | What forced the narrowing |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Cheap distributed autonomy defeats concentrated capital | A full-stack power defeated by cheap distributed systems *fielded without a patron's intelligence, connectivity and interceptor layers behind them* | The Ukraine record: the March 2025 suspension week showed the cheap layer aiming through a patron's decisive layer (ch12) |
| 2 | Privacy-preserving systems become the default | Bloc-internal fiscal and reporting reach *falls in fact*; the offshore untaxed share *rises again*; plural privacy-default stacks displace bloc stacks *as the operative default* | The legibility record: the offshore stock persists while the untaxed fraction collapsed threefold — the honest baseline required a sharper falsifier (ch08) |
| 3 | Platforms consolidate independence | An actor with decisive-relevant capability and a majority-commercial fiscal base escapes the state's instruments *and resolves its disputes with states in fora states do not control* | The legal history: plural fora proved parasitic on state law; forum-migration is the discriminating test (chs. 8, 11) |
| 4 | No identifiable Tudor moves | The five moves decomposed into scoreable forms across all four candidates, *plus* the Depreter limb: canon alone consolidating a bloc would falsify the mechanism even while producing a settlement look-alike | The comparative work (ch11) and the Burgundian accounts (ch10): apparatus without a continuous centre fails — so the EU case must be able to cut against the book |

A wager that tightens itself under research is behaving as a wager
should; a wager that loosens is becoming unfalsifiable. The reader
is invited to hold the book to the published version and to none
softer.

## V. What the book does not claim

Four disclaimers, stated flatly because each has been earned by a
chapter. The book does not predict specific events — no dates for
settlements, no named winners of elections or wars; its unit of
prediction is the pattern and its horizon is fifteen years of named
indicators. It does not claim any polity's success: the American
candidacy is called probable and half-conscious, the Chinese
substantially executed but exposed at the summit the book's own
two-bodies chapter diagnoses, the European conditional on a fiscal
substrate that does not yet exist, the Indian possible; all four
could fail, and condition four of the wager scores exactly that. It
does not claim the settlement is desirable: the Tudor turn, where
it ran, ran through bonds, expulsions, dissolutions and a harvest
of heads, and its modern forms — whatever their legality — will
concentrate power in ways the book's readers may reasonably dread;
the book predicts, and declines to bless. And it does not claim
that history repeats: it claims that a mechanism has recurred, and
it stakes the claim on measurements.

## VI. The debt to Bull, and the precise disagreement

The debt first, because it is total: without the tenth chapter of
*The Anarchical Society* there is no literature for this book to
extend, and without Bull's own scepticism there is no model for how
to hold a bold hypothesis honestly. Bull did the two things this
book has tried to imitate — he specified the alternative to the
states system with enough precision to test, and he then declined
to claim it on the evidence of his decade. The disagreement is
correspondingly precise. It is not about the diagnosis: on the
evidence assembled in Part I, Bull's five trends have matured past
his own threshold, and the book's first chapter argues that his
caution, right in 1977, would be wrong now. It is about the
*stability* of the condition the diagnosis names. Bull entertained
the new medievalism as a possible successor system — a durable
alternative form of universal political organisation. The book's
history says otherwise: the first medieval order was durable for
centuries precisely because no actor could pay the price of ending
it, and it ended within two generations of that price becoming
payable. A dispersal is not a settlement; it is what a settlement
looks like before the bill for decisive force arrives. Where Bull
saw a possible destination, this book sees a waypoint with a
well-documented exit — and it has tried to honour him in the only
way that matters, by saying exactly what evidence would prove the
dispersal stable after all, and publishing the list.

# Appendix D — A Reader's Guide

*ASSEMBLED (first assembly, 2026-08-02). Routes through the book for
readers who arrive with different questions; the annotated literature
is Appendix A, the comparative register Appendix B, the scoreboard
Appendix C.*

**If you have one evening.** Read the Introduction, Chapter 6, Chapter
10 and Chapter 12. That is the spine: the wager, the repricing, the
machine, the prediction. Chapter 11 is the first addition if the
evening stretches.

**If you come from international relations.** Part I is written for
you, and its quarrel is with your shelf: Chapter 1 accepts and runs
the Bull–Friedrichs–Cerny apparatus; Chapter 2 amends Friedrichs's
second universalism; Chapter 12 §VI states what the book claims to
have added. Read the Coda for the precise disagreement with Bull
before deciding the book has misread him.

**If you come from medieval history.** The book's medieval claims are
deliberately narrower than its title suggests, and every chapter
states its debts and its gaps: Chapter 6 rests on Rogers, Curry,
DeVries and the chronicle editions, and its central mechanism
statement was built to reconcile the first two; Chapter 10 rests on
Contamine's monograph and articles at page level; the objection
stress-tests are in each chapter's critiques file, steelmanned first.
The place to attack is the mechanism's threshold step (Chapter 10
§VII) and the falsifiability passage's condition 1; the book would
rather be corrected at page level than admired in general.

**If you come from policy or strategy.** Chapters 5, 10, 11 and 12
are the working sequence: the failure modes of executive capacity;
the mechanism; the five moves with their current instruments, every
claim dated; the candidates and the military-technology answer.
Appendix C is the standing scoreboard. Nothing in Chapter 12 §VII is
investment guidance, by doctrine as well as disclaimer.

**If you come from the technology industry.** Read Chapter 2 to see
your institutions described functionally, Chapter 3 §V and Chapter 4
§V for the absorption pattern operating on your contracts and your
colleagues' commissions, Chapter 9 for the two-body problem in your
charters, and Chapter 11 §VII for the settlement the book expects —
operative but never invested. The Sforza case (Chapter 4 §III) is
the précis: it is about you.

**If you read for the history of ideas.** The book's method is the
sceptics-concede thread (introduced in the Introduction's second
inoculation, executed at Chapters 6 §VII, 10 §VII, 11 §V, and
summarised at 12 §VI), the falsifiability discipline (Introduction
§III; the narrowing deltas in the Coda §IV), and the provenance rules
(Coda §III). The book stands or falls on whether those disciplines
were kept; the flags in the text — [GAP], [RE-CHECK AT PRESS] — are
deliberately left visible in the working drafts so the keeping can be
checked.

**A note on the bundled sources.** The open-access neomedievalism
dossier that seeded Part I (Cerny 2005, Kobrin, McFate's thesis,
Williams, Slaughter chs. 5–6, Kaplan, Wæver, Khanna) is indexed in
this appendix's folder; Appendix A grades it and records what has
since been superseded by first-hand retrievals.
