# Critiques — Ch. 8: Private Justice, Private War

Objection 1 is the outline's named stress test (the romanticisation
complaint, inverted per the brief). Objections 2–5 surfaced in research.
Each steelmanned before the answer (§6). Perplexity run logged at the foot.

---

## Objection 1 — The romanticisation complaint (named; inverted)

**Steelman.** Chapters like this one invariably end up romanticising the
plural legal environment: the plucky Pastons navigating their options, the
feud as a colourful *Rechtsinstitut*, the law merchant as bottom-up order,
vendetta as social regulation — a medieval libertarian idyll against which
the Leviathan's monopoly looks like loss. And the modern half compounds
it: describing arbitration, platform courts and crypto experiments in the
same register invites the reading that private ordering is a natural and
adequate substitute for public justice. The historian's complaint and the
political complaint converge: the actual operation of feud and vendetta
was violent, arbitrary and grim, and any chapter that files them under
"legal pluralism" has prettified them.

**The chapter's answer — the inversion, run all the way through.** The
chapter *agrees*, and builds on the agreement. Its thesis is that the
plural environment was grim precisely because enforcement tracked private
power: Margaret Paston was carried out of Gresham by armed men and her
husband could not "sue the commone lawe… for the gret myght and alyaunce
of the seid Lord" — the sheriff himself, in a private letter, pleads the
king's interference (memo §1, both T1). The erosion of that environment
was a genuine achievement of the consolidating state, and the chapter
says so flatly. The inversion then does the forward work: if the plural
environment was grim, its return should be examined in the same light,
not celebrated as innovation. The chapter's three-part disanalogy (memo
§5) is the discipline that keeps the inversion honest: the modern plural
environment is mostly non-violent, state-parasitic, and increasingly
legible — so the indictment is not "arbitration = feud" but that forum
migration re-prices justice by private power, which is exactly what the
fifteenth century teaches. Romanticism is refused in both directions: no
idyll of the feud, and no idyll of the monopolist state either — the
public sword of §6 arrives as the instrument of the fiscal-military
consolidation, not as a moral hero.

## Objection 2 — The chapter's own medievalism is a myth (the Kadens problem)

**Steelman.** The chapter mocks modern private ordering for citing a
mythical medieval law merchant — while itself running a chapter-length
medieval analogy for modern private justice. If Kadens and Sachs are
right that there was no transnational customary merchant law, then
"medieval regulatory arbitrage" is equally a modern projection, and the
chapter saws off the branch it sits on. One cannot deploy the
myth-busting literature against ISDS apologists and then keep the myth's
furniture — piepowder courts, forum-shopping merchants — as one's own
analogical stock.

**The chapter's answer.** The claims are different in kind, and the
chapter must keep them visibly distinct. What Kadens demolishes is a
*substantive* claim: a uniform, universal, custom-generated commercial
law independent of polities. What the chapter asserts is a
*jurisdictional* claim: that many real courts — royal, ecclesiastical,
manorial, borough, staple, admiralty — operated concurrently, and that
litigants moved among them. That claim does not rest on the myth; it
rests on the institutional record and is stated in exactly those terms by
the current specialist literature (Johnson: "a world of a thousand
law-courts… dense legal pluralism," memo §4) and demonstrated at T1 by
the Paston corpus. Kadens herself does not deny the courts existed; she
denies their law was a coherent private *lex*. The chapter's modern
parallel is likewise jurisdictional (forum migration to arbitration,
platform process, offshore courts), not a claim that a new universal
private law has formed — indeed Pistor's point, which the chapter
adopts, is that the "private" order runs on two *state* legal systems.
The draft states this distinction once, explicitly, where the Kadens
material is introduced (memo §4 caution).

## Objection 3 — Nothing here is neomedieval: the state never left

**Steelman.** Every modern institution the chapter cites is a creature of
state law. Arbitration awards run only through the New York Convention —
a treaty among 172 *states* — and are set aside by state courts; ISDS
exists by treaty and is being dismantled by treaty (Achmea, the ECT
exit); the Oversight Board is corporate governance, DSA Article 21 bodies
are state-certified and expressly non-binding; the DAO fled into a
Wyoming LLC; even the offshore system is being drained by an
OECD-coordinated information-exchange regime — the Tax Observatory's own
headline is that evasion fell by a factor of three. Cerny's sixth
characteristic, applied to this material, is a category error: this is
not a plural legal environment; it is a single state-law environment with
subcontractors. The chapter's premise dissolves on its own evidence.

**The chapter's answer.** The objection restates the chapter's close as
if it were a refutation of its opening. Two replies. First, the medieval
plural environment was *also* formally under a single supreme
jurisdiction — the king's peace covered England in 1450 as completely on
parchment as state law covers commerce now; the Empire "abolished" the
feud in 1495 and Götz feuded for another generation. Pluralism is a
claim about where the enforcement business actually goes, not about
formal supremacy — and the caseload numbers (ICC's record $354bn
pending; 1,440 ISDS cases; half a million platform appeals against
sixty-five decisions) show where it goes. Second, the state's current
reach is not a rebuttal of the chapter but its final section: the
re-assertion (Achmea → ECT exit, DSA 20–21, DMA fines, MiCA, Binance) is
*dated, recent, and incomplete* — it is the beginning of the settlement
the book predicts, arriving in the sequence the book predicts
(consolidation first, judicial monopoly after). What would actually
falsify the chapter is the objection being true *throughout the period*:
if the enforcement business had never migrated, there would be nothing
to re-assert over. The 10%-of-world-GDP offshore stock, still standing
while its untaxed fraction collapses, is the two halves of the chapter
in one statistic.

## Objection 4 — Platform "adjudication" is customer service, not jurisdiction

**Steelman.** Calling App Review or content moderation "private justice"
inflates a helpdesk into a court. There is no lis inter partes, no
independent adjudicator, no law but a changeable policy document, and no
finality — Apple restored 421 of 26,224 appealed removals, which is not a
justice system's reversal rate but a vendor's discretion. The Oversight
Board hears 0.01% of appeals and can be defunded by its founder. A
jurisdiction whose "judgments" are terms-of-service enforcement is a
metaphor, and the chapter's own ch02 already spent the canon-law
metaphor; running it again here is double-counting.

**The chapter's answer.** The chapter's claim is functional, and the
numbers are the argument, not the embarrassment. For the merchant on
Amazon, the developer on iOS, the publisher on YouTube, the *operative*
determination of property and livelihood — can I trade, is my stock
frozen, is my channel demonetised — is made by these processes and
almost never reviewed elsewhere: 7.77 million submissions and 128.9
million account terminations against 421 restorations is precisely the
point, the scale of a jurisdiction with the process of a helpdesk. That
is what "legal grey zone" means in Cerny's sixth characteristic: rule
and sanction without the rule-of-law apparatus. The Board's 0.01% is
cited *for* the argument (an appellate spire on a mass jurisdiction, of
which it overturns ~90% of what it examines — evidence of the error rate
below); the DSA's compulsory internal complaints and certified-but-
non-binding external bodies are the state conceding both that the
adjudication is real and that it must be caged. And the distinction from
ch02 is kept: ch02 established the *doctrinal* apparatus (canon law,
excommunication); this chapter counts the *enforcement business* — the
same division the book uses between the medieval Church's law and its
courts. The disanalogy (contract not sacrament; exit exists in principle)
is stated per §6; passages leaning on the robes rather than the function
are [ANALOGY-ONLY].

## Objection 5 — Partisan sources, English weighting

**Steelman.** The chapter's most vivid evidence is special pleading
preserved by accident: the Pastons' own petitions (a thousand attackers!
three thousand besiegers!), a sheriff's reported excuses, Götz's
self-serving memoir written for posterity, Muir's massacre known chiefly
through one historian's gated monograph. The comparative register leans
on England because England kept the paper; the Empire and Italy sections
ride on translated set pieces. A chapter about the unreliability of
partisan justice is built on partisan documents.

**The chapter's answer.** Conceded as to the documents; managed as to the
method — and the concession is itself material. The memo applies source
criticism in-line: the "thousand persones" and "3,000" are flagged as the
Pastons' own numbers and are to be attributed, never asserted; the
sheriff's words arrive through Osbern's report and are cited as such;
Götz's fifteen feuds are counted from his own memoir with its date and
publication history given. Partisan provenance is not a defect here but
part of the phenomenon: in a plural legal environment, the petition *is*
the legal instrument — pleading to chancery in exactly the register the
chapter quotes was how the system was worked. On the weighting: the
Empire and Italy carry the two structural points England cannot (the
feud as codified legal institution with statutory notice periods; the
notarised pax as mass private settlement), each on open T1/T2 anchors
(Golden Bull ch. 17; GHDI 1495; Zorzi; the Jansen dataset via reviews),
with the gated monographs on the retrieval list rather than silently
paraphrased. That is the Batch-1/2 discipline (the ch09 "Kantorowicz at
second hand" answer) applied again: bounded, declared exposure, primary
evidence first-hand where it is load-bearing.

## Revisions (2026-09-13 — integration run, PENDING HUMAN REVIEW)

1. **Payling and Baker — "outspent" is the wrong verb for
   England.** Steelman: Payling's Crown was not priced out of
   justice; it was UNWILLING — "royal justice was evenhanded in
   its negligence" (p. 10); the deterrent that worked was
   "private vengeance" (p. 16); and the pardon, the crown's own
   instrument, was the leak. Baker: the merchant courts were
   absorbed by PROCEDURE — assumpsit, writ of error, the 1477
   statute — "no 'incorporation of the law merchant' into the
   common law before the eighteenth century" (p. 320); nothing
   fiscal or military anywhere in the story. If the plural legal
   order was tolerated rather than unaffordable, and absorbed by
   professionals rather than by money, §VII's "not argued out of
   existence; it was outspent" fails for England. Answer (the
   assessments' grading preserved): claim sequence, not payment —
   the settlement bought enforcement, and the courts' reach
   followed the fisc's (Baker's dates fall inside the window;
   Payling's 1557 threshold follows the Tudor fisc) — with the
   England line carried as the book's own argument at (c), graded
   PLAUSIBLE, UNPROVEN: toleration was the price of a crown that
   could not pay for the alternative; it stopped tolerating when
   it could. State it so, or drop the verb.

2. **Dean — the law came a century before the fisc.** Steelman:
   the criminalisation of vendetta, the composition→punishment
   turn, ex officio inquisition and the Bargello are communal
   achievements of c. 1300–1340 ("from early in the fourteenth
   century, legal vocabulary changes … 'pax non prosit'," p. 125;
   Florence's Bargello 1315/1335); "the absence of linear
   evolution" (p. 51) denies any single trajectory; none of it
   waited on gunpowder or a permanent tax. Answer (graded
   ADEQUATE, three moves): (i) Dean's own qualification — the
   communal machine was "'fierce', but 'inefficient'" (p. 18),
   assertion without enforcement, the same shape as the 1495
   Landfriede control case; (ii) where enforcement hardens it is
   princely, salaried and dated to the hinge decades (Naples
   1430s; Mantua 1446–47; Bologna 1450); (iii) the communes are
   the precocious small-scale case already conceded in ch04.
   Reformulation for §I at renovation: the LEGISLATIVE claim to
   monopoly long predates the settlement in Italy; what the
   settlement supplied was enforcement. "The law did not tame
   the sword until the exchequer could pay for both" survives;
   "the courts followed the companies" does not, for Italy. And
   the management-of-enmity sentence needs a tense: right for
   the thirteenth century, increasingly wrong after 1300.

3. **Tilly — the disarmament came two centuries after the courts,
   and the control case is where he puts the gun.** Steelman: §VII
   dates the subordination of the plural legal order to "the two
   generations after — 1470s to 1540s — precisely where the
   fiscal-military consolidation had laid the substrate, and
   nowhere else." Tilly's dates for the subordination of private
   force are the Tudors', Richelieu's ("in the 1620s … another
   half-century to become effective") and the Empire's after 1648;
   France's *grands* assembled armies in the Fronde a full two
   centuries after the parlement of Toulouse; and the single
   sentence in either essay that names artillery — Gerhard's "the
   razing of castles, the high cost of artillery, the attraction of
   court life, and the ensuing domestication of the nobility" —
   describes the Empire after the Thirty Years' War, the chapter's
   "assertion without substrate." If the mechanism's own vocabulary
   fits the control case two centuries late, the chapter's sequence
   is a sequence of paper — courts planted, fora ranked — and the
   thing the chapter's title names, private war, ended on Tilly's
   clock, not the chapter's. **Answer** (graded ADEQUATE): (i) the
   chapter's object after ruling (o) is jurisdiction, not arms —
   the courts' *reach*, the ranking of fora — and the draft already
   separates "the law had claimed the sword long before" from "did
   not tame it until the exchequer could pay for both"; what the
   objection forces is that "nowhere else" be confined in terms to
   the ranking and that the disarmament be dated separately, later
   and by Tilly; (ii) the Empire is not a counter-example but the
   mechanism at a different scale: Gerhard's domestication came
   when "only the territorial princes had the right of levying
   troops," that is, when the substrate arrived at the level of
   the princes and not the Emperor — which is what "without
   substrate" means for the imperial level, and the draft should
   say so; (iii) Tilly's fifteenth century is absent from both
   essays, so he neither states nor denies the chapter's sequence
   — the quarrel about the artillery step is ch10 §VII's (Bean).
   No spine ruling reached; ruling (o)'s register survives with the
   two clauses.

4. **Chastellain — Burgundy 1457: a prince without a permanent
   fisc enforced his justice over a town's franchise and the
   licensed bankers.** Steelman: the chapter's finding is that the
   settlement supplied *enforcement* and the courts bit "once the
   exchequer could pay the men who did the ranking." In May 1457
   Philip the Good — ch10 §VI's case of offices without an
   irreversible fisc — inventoried the fled Lombards' houses,
   confiscated the burgesses' usurious deposits "tout à luy," fined
   them, took Masset out of Valenciennes' franchise over the town's
   privilege because the case "trop touchoit au grief de la chose
   publique," held him "par longs ans," and put every bank in his
   lands to composition (Chastellain III, pp. 315–16). Enforcement
   did not wait on the permanent tax; it paid for itself from the
   seizure. **Answer** (graded ADEQUATE): (i) one case, against a
   fugitive bankrupt whose crime the plural order itself excepted
   from refuge ("larrecin non rémissible") — a prince's reach, not
   a system; the chapter's evidence for systematic enforcement is
   salaried men in office (Dean's Naples, Mantua, Bologna), and
   Burgundy's instrument here was seizure, which is Tilly's racket
   in one chapter (the state producing the shield and the danger,
   and taking a price for both); (ii) the limit of the reach is in
   the same chapter: "le duc enfin estoit constraint de les y
   remettre par la nécessité publique" — the prince's justice ran
   over the persons and stopped at the function, which is the
   dependence term ch10 §VIII carries and the reason the assessment
   places the passage there; (iii) the extraction motive
   (assessment §5(2)) must travel with any use here, or the passage
   flatters the state. If the Lombards enter this chapter (8-b),
   they enter with the caution in the same sentence. No spine
   ruling reached.

5. **Ertman — the men who did the ranking bought the right to do
   it.** Steelman: §VII's closing line — "It was outranked — once
   the exchequer could pay the men who did the ranking" — assumes
   the exchequer paid them. For France, Ertman's finding is the
   reverse: the fifteenth-century administration, "judicial or
   financial," was "dotted with clans and dynasties of officials
   who treated their offices as their own property" (p. 81), and it
   was "primarily to these and other officials, rather than to
   bankers or large-scale tax farmers, that the French governments
   of the late middle ages turned for loans and advances" (p. 76).
   The parlements planted at Toulouse, Grenoble, Bordeaux and Dijon
   were royal in name and proprietary in staffing; the ranking was
   done by a new intermediary stratum that lent the crown its own
   revenue, and the local landscape stayed "riddled by seigneurial
   and ecclesiastical jurisdictions" (p. 92). The line is true of
   Dean's salaried Italians and false of the chapter's France.
   **Answer** (graded, on the assessment's pattern for the Ertman
   objection, GOOD on the mechanism and WEAK on the line): (i) the
   mechanism's claim is subordination, not Weberian rationality —
   ruling (b) — and p. 92 confirms it in the chapter's own register:
   the jurisdictions survived, ranked; (ii) the chapter's France is
   the courts' *reach*, and a venal parlement reached — the crown
   sold the office and kept the appeal; (iii) but the closing line
   cannot stand unqualified for France: the sentence before it must
   concede that the crown paid Italy's rankers and sold France's,
   and that the settlement's administrative capacity was, in
   Ertman's words, appropriated by the stratum it created — which
   is ch10 §III's venality limb and ch12's hybrid, entered there.
   No spine ruling reached from this chapter; the doctrine gloss
   (administrative capacity as collecting capacity) is Roderick's
   at ch10.

6. **Goldsmith & Wu — the modern re-assertion is not early; it was
   done by 2006, cheaply, by coercion of intermediaries.** Steelman:
   §VII's bridge calls the re-assertion "observable, dateable, and
   early in its arc" and dates it 2018–25. Goldsmith and Wu date it
   2000–06: Yahoo before the Paris court, the 2002 self-discipline
   pact, "notions of a self-governing cyberspace are largely
   discredited" (p. 14) — and the instrument was not a fisc but
   law on local chokepoints: "the Internet has made the network
   itself the intermediary" (p. 70); "Local intermediaries are a
   defining, and therefore ineliminable, aspect" (p. 71); the ATF
   "simply ordered Visa, MasterCard, and AmEx to stop taking online
   cigarette orders … without ever laying a finger on online
   sellers" (pp. 76–77). Read straight, the platform courts of §VI
   were subordinate from the day they opened, the "hierarchical
   plurality" of §VII existed before the DSA named it, and the
   chapter's modern half describes as an arc beginning what was a
   condition accomplished — with no artillery-scale investment
   behind it. **Answer** (graded, per the assessment, GOOD on (i)
   and (ii), ADEQUATE on (iii)): (i) category — their chokepoints
   are the canon-and-register layer, which the manuscript already
   holds cheap (ch11's fifth move: "the harness was the canon and
   the register, not the plant"); the DSA's Art. 21 cap is that
   move in statute, and §VII's arc is the *ranking of fora* —
   arbitration precluded, platform process capped, the chain
   codified — which is later and distinct from the coercion of
   intermediaries; the sentence should say so in a layered tense
   (8-e); (ii) what cheap coercion cannot do is the book's subject —
   it can order the router and cannot build it — and §VI's
   disanalogies already state that the modern fora are "parasitic
   on state law," which is Goldsmith and Wu's thesis conceded in
   advance; (iii) their scale limit ("less effective in small
   nations," pp. 81–82) is the bloc geometry from the legal side,
   and confines §VII's "hierarchical plurality" to polities whose
   markets hold the intermediaries' assets — a concession the
   modern bridge should make in a clause. The spine §7 amendment
   (a layered tense) is book-level and ch12 §IV's; this chapter
   takes its instance. No spine ruling reached here.
