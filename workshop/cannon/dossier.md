## 1. Standing beside a bombard

The great pieces of the mid-century are best measured by the ones the
sources weigh. The bombard broken up at Dijon in 1467 weighed 9,000
pounds — four and a half tons of metal, recast into sixty-three
lighter guns (Depreter 2011, doc. 1). The pieces cast at Caen in the
1370s ran past 2,000 pounds and threw stone balls of a quintal — a
hundredweight, a ball a strong man cannot lift to his chest
(Contamine 1972, T1 ~pp. 122–23). The mature bronze cannon Giovio
watched enter Rome in 1494 were "eight feet long, six thousand
pounds," and moved — this was the marvel — "à une incroyable vitesse"
behind horses (Contamine 1964: 221). At Harfleur in 1449 there were
sixteen of the great sort ranged against one town wall (Chartier ii.
178). A bombard's muzzle stands at about the height of a man's chest
on its frame, and the bore takes a kneeling man's head and shoulders
[COLOUR — UNSOURCED]. The sound the sources give us is second-hand
but consistent: Blondel's "horrenda fulgura" — dreadful lightnings —
and the "intolerabili tormentorum fragore," the intolerable din,
that alone opened Valognes (Blondel, in Stevenson 1863: 155, 213); at
Neuss an eyewitness saw culverin fire "thicker than rain" (DeVries
1998: 138, Burgundian). A great gun's discharge is felt in the
breastbone before it is heard, and the smoke of a battery hangs in a
low sweet-stinking bank until the wind takes it [COLOUR — UNSOURCED].

**The named guns.** The French royal train christened its pieces: at
Montlhéry in 1465 the Burgundians captured a great cast couleuvrine
called **L'ostesse** — "the Hostess" — and "ung gros canon de cuivre
nommé **l'ung des douze pers**": one of *the Twelve Peers*, which
means there was a series — twelve great guns named for the twelve
paladins of France (Contamine 1972, T1 fn, citing Perroy, Montlhéry,
p. 188). The English named theirs too: Hornby Castle fell in 1471 to
"the cannon *Mile End*" (DeVries 1998: 144). A gun with a name has a
keeper, a reputation, and a biography of sieges; the men certainly
spoke of them as individuals [COLOUR — UNSOURCED, the practice
inferred from the naming].

## 2. The founding, as a scene

The founders were bell-men first — "souvent fondeurs de cloches"
(T1 ~p. 297) — and the craft transfer is the scene's engine: the same
pit-and-mould art that gave a town its tenor bell gave the king his
bombard. The sources put founding at Paris, Saint-Lô and Caen under
"maistres des canons, à la fois fondeurs et canonniers" — founder and
gunner in one man, who cast the piece and then served it (T1 ~pp.
122–23). The scene itself must be built from the craft: the casting
pit dug beside the furnace and the mould buried muzzle-up in rammed
earth; the days of drying; the tapping of the furnace and the long
bright pour; the week the metal cools before the mould is broken; the
boring and proofing [COLOUR — UNSOURCED as to the French royal
practice; the bell-founder transfer itself is sourced]. Proofing was
the moment of truth — a new gun fired double-charged behind an
earthwork, the founder's reputation standing next to it
[COLOUR — UNSOURCED]. Recasting closed the loop: a cracked or
obsolete great gun went back to the furnace, as at Dijon, 9,000
pounds becoming sixty-three new barrels in the modern calibres
(Depreter 2011).

## 3. The train on the road

Use Perroy's numbers as spectacle, because they were spectacle to
contemporaries. The 1465 train: a convoy **six leagues long** — a
day's march from head to tail — drawn by **1,200 horses**, "la plus
belle du monde… de quoi faire trembler le monde entier," in the
words the Milanese ambassadors sent home to a court that knew
artillery (Perroy 1943). The 1477 establishment, from the
financiers' own forecasts: 521 horses for the guns and tools of
three bands, 1,009 more for the powder, saltpetre, sulphur, lead,
shot, bombard-stones, two mobile forges, cordage and tents, and 800
pioneers marching with picks and shovels under the captain of the
wagon-train (Perroy 1943, pt. 2 ~p. 294). The towns fed and
sweetened the passage — gifts to the passing gunners are in the
accounts (Perroy 1943) — wine on the wagon-heads at the gates
[COLOUR — UNSOURCED as to the wine]. On the move, the light
couleuvrines went "traînées par un à quatre chevaux" (T1 fn); the
great bombards each in a matched team, walking pace, the stone-carts
behind, the road breaking under them in wet weather so that the
pioneers worked ahead of the guns as much as behind
[COLOUR — UNSOURCED as to the road-mending]. Rivers were the real
roads: land haulage cost ten times water, and 220,000 pounds of
materiel once moved fifty kilometres overland for 605 l.t. — a
number a clerk thought worth recording (Contamine 1964: ~258).

## 4. The arsenal as a place

Paris held the depots: the keeper of the "petits engins" had his
charge there (Contamine 1964), and the master of the artillery held
court — literally — "en son auditoire ou chastel du Louvre," where
the salpêtriers of Paris came to judgment in March 1487 (1964: 232
n. 40). Picture the Louvre yard as a working precinct: powder in
the tower magazines away from the forges, the proof-butts by the
river, the smell of horse, tar and sulphur in the carriage-sheds
[COLOUR — UNSOURCED]. The account-books were the arsenal's true
architecture: two permanent registers, quarterly pay, thirty
clerks, 3.3 per cent overhead (Contamine 1964: 231–32) — and at the
Burgundian mirror-arsenal, a dedicated receiver from 1458 audited
at Lille, whose one surviving account bears the duke's own
signature (Depreter 2011).

## 5. The gunners as men

About 150 of them at the standing establishment, and they stayed:
of thirty-nine canonniers on the 1469 register, nine were still
serving twenty-two years later — careers of twenty years, fathers
plausibly enrolling sons [the careers sourced, Contamine 1964:
226–27; the sons COLOUR — UNSOURCED]. The garrison canonnier drew 8
l.t. a month — more than an archer, less than a man-at-arms
(Annexe VI); at Cherbourg in 1454 two canonniers were counted for
three men's pay (Annexe VI fn 76) — scarce skill, priced. The
craft asked three literacies: found a piece, mix the powder from
charcoal, saltpetre and sulphur, and hold "quelques rudiments de
balistique" (T1 ~p. 297). The risks were the trade's own: Giraud
de Samain was captured at Montlhéry standing with his guns —
L'ostesse and the Peer taken with him (T1 fn); and every gunner
served a barrel that might burst — the early pieces were fired
under-charged for fear of it [the bursting risk carried at
tertiary level in the book's memos; the gunner's fear
COLOUR — UNSOURCED]. At the top, the trade ennobled: Jean and
Gaspard Bureau "reconnus nobles par Charles VII" (JJ 178 n° 256,
via T1 fn) while Basin sneered at the "homo plebeyus… artifex" of
embankments and ditches (via T1 fn 94) — and Chartier, watching
Jean at Harfleur, called him "fort subtil et ingénieulx, et en
plusieurs autres choses" (Chartier ii. 178). Their devotions are
on the record at the soldiers' level: particular saints —
"saint Michel, sainte Barbe, saint Sébastien, sainte Catherine" —
with rites and superstitions Contamine can only gesture at (T2
~p. 512). Barbara — the tower, the lightning-struck executioner —
was the one the powder-trades made their own; that she is listed
among the soldiers' saints is sourced; that the French gunners of
the 1440s already claimed her *particularly* is
[COLOUR — UNSOURCED, though the later tradition is universal].

## 6. The powder mill

The mill is the world's least documented room and the most
sensory. What the sources give: the three elements and the
canonnier's own hands (T1 ~p. 297); the keeper of the powders as
an office (1964); Rouen and Reims delivering by royal order
(Perroy); consumption climbing from hundreds of pounds to tens of
thousands within a lifetime (T1 ~pp. 122–23). The room itself is
[COLOUR — UNSOURCED throughout, marked once for the block]: the
brimstone stink that gets into wool and stays; the charcoal dust
that blacks the men like colliers; the mortar-and-pestle batches
worked damp for fear of the spark; the sieves and the drying
trays; the rule that iron never enters the room; the boy at the
door with the lantern set behind horn. Corning — the wet
granulation that made powder keep and burn true — belongs to
this room's next generation; its chemistry is Hall's territory
(retrieval list), and the book's own corned/serpentine
distinction rests on the general literature, not the French
accounts.
