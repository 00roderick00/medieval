## 1. Bronze: copper and tin

The founders came from the bell trade: the crown's cannoneers were
"souvent fondeurs de cloches" — often bell-founders — required to know
how to found a piece as well as serve one (Contamine 1972, T1 ~p.
297). Founding was urban and dispersed before it was central: in the
1370s guns came "de Paris, de Saint-Lô et de Caen, fabriqués sous la
direction de 'maistres des canons', à la fois fondeurs et canonniers"
— at Caen alone at least 32 pieces were cast, the largest over 2,000
livres in weight, throwing stone balls of a quintal (T1 ~pp. 122–23).
The metal itself appears in the sources as product, not as trade
route: a great copper cannon at Montlhéry (below, §10), bronze pans in
the ordonnance billet standard (T2 ch. XVI ~p. 508), and the mature
bronze siege pieces of 1494 — eight feet long, six thousand pounds,
"appelées canons" (Giovio, via Contamine 1964: 221). **Gap stated:**
none of the in-repo sources documents the copper- or tin-sourcing
routes (Central European copper, Cornish/Iberian tin are the standard
scholarly background) — this is precisely the materials detail Smith
& DeVries 2005 (retrieval list, BUY) exists to supply.

Recasting was routine practice at the Burgundian mirror: at Dijon in
1467 a 9,000-lb bombard was broken up and re-founded as sixty-three
serpentines (18 of 200 lb, 27 of 100 lb, 18 of 50 lb) — calibres
modernised downward toward mobility (Depreter 2011, doc. 1).

## 2. Wrought iron

The wrought classes run through the procurement lists — the 1442
programme's veuglaires and serpentines beside the cast bombards
(Contamine 1972: 666) — and the field repair capacity travelled with
the train: the 1477 Artois campaign carried **two mobile forges**
among powder, saltpetre, sulphur, lead, shot and tools (Perroy 1943,
pt. 2 ~p. 294). The trades appear in the T2 index of matériel:
forgeron (~p. 508), taillandier (edge-tool maker, ~p. 452). **Gap
stated:** no in-repo source details the iron-hoop-and-stave
construction of the great wrought bombards — Smith & DeVries again.

## 3. Saltpetre

The oldest documented input: from **1339** the master and clerk of
the crossbowmen of France were buying "des canons, du salpêtre, du
soufre vif" (Contamine 1972, T1 ~p. 123) — saltpetre purchases a
century before the Bureau train. By the mature institution it had its
own office: the **"trésorier et garde des salpêtres, soufres et
plomb"** — treasurer and keeper of the saltpetres, sulphurs and lead
(Contamine 1964). The trade was regulated at the crown's own court:
on **24 March 1487 the salpêtriers of Paris appeared in judgment
before the master of the artillery "en son auditoire ou chastel du
Louvre"** (Contamine 1964: 232 n. 40) — saltpetre-men as a recognised
Paris trade, answerable to the artillery's own jurisdiction. Scale:
the 1442 programme costed **20,000 livres (weight) of powder at 2,200
l.t.** (Contamine 1972: 666). **Gap stated:** the sources do not
describe the niter-bed/collection economy itself (cellars, dovecotes,
the digging rights) — period-standard background, not in the corpus.

## 4. Sulphur

"Soufre vif" bought from 1339 (T1 ~p. 123); one of the canonnier's
"trois éléments de base : le charbon de bois, le salpêtre et le
soufre" (T1 ~p. 297); stored under the same keeper as the saltpetre
(Contamine 1964). Civic defence stocks knew it too — Nîmes in 1355
prepared vessels of ash, oil, sulphur and tow against escalade (T1,
cited for the earlier period). **Gap stated:** import routes
(Sicilian/volcanic sulphur) not in the corpus.

## 5. Charcoal

"Charbon de bois" — the third base element (T1 ~p. 297); the wood
trades ride in the train's own registers: scieur de bois (wood-sawyer,
T2 index ~p. 312). **Gap:** no dedicated charcoal-supply accounts in
the corpus.

## 6. Powder manufacture

Manufacture was a canonnier's competence, not an outsourced product:
the gunners "doivent en effet savoir fondre une pièce, mais aussi
fabriquer de la poudre, selon les recettes traditionnelles qui, peu à
peu, s'enrichissent et se perfectionnent" (T1 ~p. 297). The scale
curve is Contamine's: powder consumption **decupled** between the
1350s–60s and the 1370s–80s; "vers 1375, on comptait, dans
l'artillerie royale française, par centaines de livres de poudre;
désormais, on compte par milliers ou dizaines de milliers" (T1 ~pp.
122–23). Supply in campaign flowed from the towns: powder delivered
at royal order by **Rouen and Reims** for the 1477 operations, with
provisioning staged through Orléans and Paris (Perroy 1943). A
dedicated custodian existed: the **garde des poudres** (Contamine
1964). **Gap stated, important:** *corning* — the wet-granulation
process — appears nowhere in the in-repo primary material; the
corned/serpentine distinction in the book rests on the general
literature (ch06 memo §2), and the chemistry belongs to Hall
(retrieval list).

## 7. Stone and iron shot

The stone-cutters marched with the army: **tailleur de pierres** in
the train's trades (T2 index ~p. 311). Stocks were counted by the
hundred: 200 dressed stones "avec possibilité d'en tailler d'autres"
in the Pisan-era defence standard, 1,170 stones for 128 guns in the
siege standard (Contamine 1972: 665–66); the Caen-cast great pieces
threw balls "d'un quintal" (T1 ~pp. 122–23). Lead travelled for the
plommées — 3,000–5,000 lb in the standards (pp. 665–66) — under the
saltpetre-and-lead keeper. Iron shot: the book's reviewed position
(stone dominant in the Bureau era; iron not displacing it until the
mid-sixteenth century) stands; the corpus adds no Bureau-era iron-shot
accounts. **Gap:** quarry contracts not in the corpus.

## 8. Carriage timber, wagons, transport

The wood-and-wheel trades thread the registers: charpentiers at every
period rate (Annexe VI: maître charpentier 4 s.t./day, valet 2 s.t.,
in the 1339–40 special categories; the série runs to the ordonnance
era, T2 index); the wagon establishment under its own officer, the
**capitaine du charroi** (Contamine 1964; Perroy 1943). Transport
economics, costed: hauling **220,000 lb (weight) of materiel overland
Gien→Cépoy (~50 km) cost 605 l.t.**, land carriage running about ten
times the cost of water per unit (Contamine 1964: ~258) — the
riverine logic that made Castillon's river-first strategy (ch06)
economic sense. Innovation reached the top: at Alençon in March 1450
the king took delivery of "un charriot d'un nouveau modèle, destiné
au transport de l'artillerie," built to his order mid-campaign
(Beaucourt V).

## 9. Horses and draught animals

Perroy's 1477 forecasts give the ratios: the guns and tools of three
artillery bands plus 26 old-ordonnance couleuvrines required **521
horses**; the consumables — powder, saltpetre, sulphur, lead, shot,
bombard-stones, two forges, pioneers' tools, cordage, tents — **1,009
more**; with **800 pioneers** marching under the capitaine du charroi
(Perroy 1943, pt. 2 ~p. 294). A dozen years earlier the Milanese
ambassadors watched the 1465 train move: "la plus belle du monde,"
its convoy six leagues long, drawn by **1,200 horses** — "de quoi
faire trembler le monde entier" [TRANS. CLAUDE: enough to make the
whole world tremble] (Perroy 1943, citing the despatches). At the
light end, couleuvrines were "traînées par un à quatre chevaux"
(Contamine 1972, T1 fn ~p. 3xx). Stabling was designed into the
billet standard: six horses per lance, with loft, wood, straw and
forage (T2 ch. XVI ~p. 508).

## 10. The named trades, offices and men

The office series (accounts from 1418; Bessonneau to 1440; Jean
Bureau from Aug 1439; Gaspard from Jan 1441 — Annexe VIII); the
titulature ("maistre de l'artillerie du roy" / *magister artillerie
regis* / "garde et gouverneur"); the mature specialised offices —
capitaine du charroi; trésorier et garde des salpêtres, soufres et
plomb; garde des poudres; capitaine des "mines et approches"; garde
des "petits engins" at Paris (Contamine 1964). The master's older
commercial face: in 1406 "fabricant et garde-magasin," contracting
with other artilleurs and founders "d'où sans doute d'assez gros
profits" (T1 ~p. 29x). The establishment: ~150 standing personnel;
careers averaging twenty years (9 of 39 canonniers of 1469 still
serving in 1491); garrison canonnier at 8 l.t./month — above the
archer, below the man-at-arms (Annexe VI; Contamine 1964: 226–27).
The men at the top were made noble for it: Jean and Gaspard Bureau
"furent reconnus nobles par Charles VII" (A.N. JJ 178 n° 256, via
T1 fn ~p. 405), against Basin's sneer — "homo plebeyus et… aggerum
et fossarum faciendorum artifex" (via T1 fn 94). Named guns existed:
at Montlhéry (1465) the Burgundians took four couleuvrines, a great
cast couleuvrine called **"L'ostesse,"** and "ung gros canon de
cuivre nommé **l'ung des douze pers**" — one of the Twelve Peers
(T1 fn, citing Perroy, "L'artillerie royale à la bataille de
Montlhéry," p. 188).

## 11. Costs, assembled

1442: guns 4,198 l.t. + powder 2,200 l.t. vs 3,100 l.t. traditional
(p. 666; ratio ≈2.06). Early 1400s: ~10,000 l.t. (canons, boulets,
poudre) vs ~9,000 l.t. (trebuchet + trait) — the crossover already
made (Annexe XII E). 1489: artillery at 8% of all military
expenditure; ordinaire wages 24,236 l.t. (1491, 22nd account);
extraordinaire 111,733 l.t. (1489); administration 3.3% of total,
~30 clerks; paid quarterly "comme c'est la règle pour tout ce qui
ressortit à l'armée permanente" (Contamine 1964: 231–32, 261).
Transport: 605 l.t. per 220,000 lb per 50 km overland; water ~10×
cheaper (1964: ~258). Burgundian mirror: a dedicated recette de
l'artillerie from 1458, audited at Lille (Depreter 2011).

## 12. The mirror table — medieval input ↔ modern full-stack analogue

| Medieval input (sourced above) | Modern analogue | Disanalogy flagged |
|---|---|---|
| Bronze founding (bell-founders; Paris/Saint-Lô/Caen; recasting at Dijon) | Leading-node fabrication (the foundry; TSMC/Intel; CHIPS) | Founding skill diffused through a craft guild-world; leading-node fab is a two-firm planetary monopoly — concentration is *higher* now |
| Saltpetre (the trade, the keeper, the Louvre jurisdiction) | Rare earths and specialty chemistry (refining chokepoints, export controls) | Saltpetre was collectable anywhere organic decay ran; rare-earth *refining* is geographically concentrated — the chokepoint moved from craft to geology-plus-policy |
| Sulphur and charcoal (bought inputs; the three-element recipe) | Energy and consumable feedstocks (power for compute; propellants) | Closest analogue; both are commodity inputs whose scarcity binds only in war |
| Powder manufacture (canonnier-made; Rouen/Reims deliveries; garde des poudres) | Munitions/energetics production (the 155mm ramp; solid propellants) | Then made by the end-user's own craftsmen; now a separated industrial base whose delivery gap is itself an indicator (C10-3) |
| Stone shot (tailleurs de pierres in the train) | Precision munitions stockpiles | Stone was infinitely replenishable in-theatre ("possibilité d'en tailler d'autres"); interceptor stocks are not — the modern train can exhaust |
| Carriage timber, charroi, 1,530 horses | Lift, logistics contracting, launch cadence | The 10× water/land cost ratio maps onto sea/air lift economics with surprising fidelity |
| The salaried canonniers (~150; 20-year careers; 8 l.t.; ennobled masters) | The cleared engineering workforce (clearances as livery; Detachment 201's commissions) | Scale inverted: ~150 then, ~a million cleared contractors now — but both are *career* services the state cannot improvise |
| The arsenal (garde des petits engins at Paris; the Louvre auditoire; Lille's audited recette) | The foundry-and-launch complex (Secure Enclave; El Capitan; Starshield) | The medieval arsenal was auditable in thirty clerks; the modern complex is auditable only in aggregate — legibility has scaled worse than capability |
| The named great guns (L'ostesse; the Douze Pers) | Named national systems (El Capitan; the constellations) | Unchanged human habit; no disanalogy worth the name |
