## 1. The man at sixty-six

Born within a year of 1387 — the same year as the king he served
first and best (Pollard ch. 1, fn 19, ~p. 7) — he was approximately
sixty-six in his last summer, not the eighty of the legend (ch. 1,
p. 1). Fifty years of warfare: Wales in his twenties, Ireland at
twenty-seven, France from thirty-three, "a large part of a lifetime
devoted to the forlorn struggle to stave off his country's military
disasters" (p. 1). The richest baron in England by the 1436 return
(£1,205 net, rated with earls; ch. 1, ~p. 8), an earl since 1442, a
marshal of France [DIGEST-CARRIED], twice a prisoner. A man of that
age and service carries a map of old wounds; the sources do not
inventory them [COLOUR — UNSOURCED as to the wounds].

## 2. Rouen, from inside the composition system

October 1449: the assault on the walls repelled under his own banner
— "le seigneur de Tallebot… vint à toutte bonne puissance de ses gens
d'armes, et avoit sa bannière emprès lui… et s'y porta et conduisit
de sa personne très chevallereusement" (Escouchy, i. 215–16 [TRANS.
CLAUDE: came in full strength, his banner beside him, and bore
himself most chivalrously]) — and then the capitulation he could not
prevent: Somerset's treaty of 29 October, the 50,000 écus, and
Talbot himself handed over among the hostages (Beaucourt V; Blondel).
What he saw next, he saw as the composition system's prisoner: the
estuary fortresses his side had pledged delivered one by one; and
when Curson refused to deliver Honfleur, the system processed *him* —
"processu legitime confecto," by due process completed, the hostage
adjudged the French king's prisoner (Blondel, in Stevenson 1863:
142). He was inside French lines through the winter Harfleur fell to
the seventeen-day battery and the sealed treaty (Chartier ii.
178–79) — a professional of sieges watching the new siege economy
work from the creditor's side of the table [the vantage inference is
mine; his precise location and what he was shown —
COLOUR — UNSOURCED]. Falaise bought his liberty by making it a term
of surrender (Pollard digest; DNB); the tradition adds a Rome
pilgrimage in the jubilee year [DIGEST-CARRIED] and an oath never
again to bear arms against the French king [BLOCKED — awaits the

## 3. The return

## 4. The last morning — Castillon, 17 July 1453

Build the scene from four sourced beats and mark the joins.

**The report.** At dawn his vanguard had cut through the
francs-archers in the abbey; then came the messenger: "les Franchois
habandonnent leur parc et s'enfuyent; il est heure ou jamais" —
they are abandoning their park; now or never (Escouchy, ii. 38–39
[TRANS. CLAUDE]). It was false — dust of the camp-followers
[TRADITION/INFERENCE — the movers' identity is a [GAP] even in the
manuscript].

**The mass.** He was hearing mass when the report came, and broke it
off with the vow Escouchy preserves: "Jamais je n'oray la messe, ou
aujourduy averay rué jus la compaingnie des Franchois" — I will
never hear mass again, or this day I shall have overthrown them
(Escouchy, ii. 38–39 [TRANS. CLAUDE]). The chapel furniture, the
chaplain's face — [COLOUR — UNSOURCED].

**The ride and the refusal.** An old gentleman of the English
nation, "qui avoit beaucoup veu des fais de la guerre," told him to
his face the report was untrue and he would gain nothing; Talbot
struck him across the face with the flat of his sword and rode on
(Escouchy, ii. 39 [TRANS. CLAUDE]). He went, according to some
accounts, "on a small white horse, wearing no armour at all"
(Pollard, ch. 1, p. 1 — the biographer's own hedge kept). If the
release oath is confirmed, the unarmoured back is not vanity but
law: a man forbidden his harness against the French crown,
directing a field he was sworn not to fight [the reading depends on
the BLOCKED oath; until then it is hypothesis — see hypothesis.md].

**The park.** "A line of 250 guns of all calibres massed wheel to
wheel" (Pollard, ch. 1, p. 1); the defenders had trained their
pieces on his line of approach — "afusterrent leur artillerie au
lez devers dont véoient venir ledit Talbot" (Escouchy, ii. 39). The
English cry — "Talbot! Talbot! Saint-George!" (Escouchy, ii. 39) —
into "the hail of shot and ball at point-blank range" (Pollard, p.
1). The attackers held the rampart for one moment — the
standard-bearer fell planting the standard — then broke; a small
party stood around the old commander; his horse went down; "his
skull smashed by an axe" (Pollard, p. 1). The axeman has no name in
the sources.

**The identification.** The tradition holds that the herald found
and knew the body by the missing left molar [TRADITION — NOT

to "battlefield and burial evidence" in the later chapters — verify

would be identified the same way a generation later (ch10's record)
— the age recognised its great men by their scars and teeth

## 5. Épilogue matter

France rejoiced — "his death was greeted with wild rejoicing in
France" (Pollard, ch. 1, p. 1); Gascon mothers were still hushing
children with "the Talbot cometh" four centuries on (ch. 1, ~p. 2).
Castillon's town surrendered two days later under the continuing
fire (ch06's record); Bordeaux signed in October; the war was over,
and the paperwork of its last composition took away the privileges
of the city that had opened him its gate (Escouchy, ii. 75–77).
