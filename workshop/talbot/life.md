## 1. Chronology

- **b. 1386–88** ("Thus 1387 is unlikely to be more than a year out" —
  Pollard ch. 1 fn 19: over 21 when summoned to parliament Oct 1409,
  probably not yet 21 in Oct 1407; in his mother's household Christmas
  1401; own household at Montgomery Dec 1404). Same birth-year as
  Henry V (ch. 1, ~p. 7). Second son of Richard, 4th Lord Talbot;
  mother Ankaret Le Strange, heiress of the Le Stranges of Blakemere
  (ch. 1, ~p. 7). Possibly blooded at Shrewsbury, 1403 (ch. 1, ~p. 8).
- **~1407 marriage** to Maud, heiress of Thomas Nevill, Lord Furnival;
  summoned as Lord Furnival 1409; Wales service in the Glyn Dŵr
  pacification; **Ireland as Henry V's lieutenant 1414**; first served
  in France 1420; inherited the Talbot barony 1422 (brother d. 1418;
  the sole heiress niece died in childhood) (ch. 1, ~pp. 7–8).
- **NOT at Verneuil 1424** — "no strictly contemporary evidence"; in
  all likelihood in England on 1 Sept, nominating attorneys before
  going to Ireland (ch. 1 fn 36 — a negative the DNB/CP tradition
  repeats wrongly).
- **1424/25 second marriage:** Margaret, eldest daughter and joint
  heiress presumptive of Richard Beauchamp, earl of Warwick (ch. 1,
  ~p. 8).
- 1427 the French war proper; Patay 1429 (captured); ransom and
  release 1433 [DIGEST-CARRIED: ransomed from Poton de Xaintrailles
  for 12,500 réaux; exchange negotiations]; the 1430s–40s reliefs and
  surprises; **Harfleur retaken 1440** [DIGEST-CARRIED: "the last
  major success"]; created **earl of Shrewsbury 1442** — "as much a
  consequence of his wealth as a recognition of his services" (ch. 1,
  ~p. 8); marshal of France [DIGEST-CARRIED]; Rouen 1449 — hostage
  under the capitulation (Blondel, in Stevenson 1863: 142: when
  Curson refused Honfleur's delivery, Talbot "processu legitime
  confecto" adjudged Charles VII's prisoner); released 1450 on terms
  including, per the tradition, a Rome pilgrimage in the jubilee year
  [DIGEST-CARRIED; the reported oath never again to bear arms against
  the French king — BLOCKED, awaits the monograph's chapters];
  **returned from Rome 20 Dec 1450** [DIGEST-CARRIED]; Kent
  commissions 1451 [DIGEST-CARRIED]; **Bordeaux, entered ~17–20 Oct
  1452** (DNB: the city rose, a gate opened 20 Oct); **Castillon, 17
  July 1453** — killed, skull smashed by an axe (Pollard ch. 1, p. 1);
  his son Lord Lisle died with him.

## 3. The money

- **The 1436 income-tax return: £1,205 net** from lands, offices and
  annuities in England — "among the ranks of the wealthiest laymen in
  England, the richest of the barons," rated roughly equal to the
  earls of Northumberland and Salisbury (ch. 1, ~p. 8). Fn 23's
  caveats: probably understated; ~£200 p.a. more stood in the hands
  of his sons and his brother's widow; Welsh and Irish lands
  excluded; Northumberland's under-assessment more than double his.
- Ransom after Patay [DIGEST-CARRIED: 12,500 réaux to Xaintrailles;
  "made ransom a continuing fact of Talbot's finances"]; the war's
  net balance [DIGEST-CARRIED: "probably made only a slight net cash
  profit, but war transformed his family's status: baron became
  earl"].
- Pollard's own summary formula (ch. 1, ~p. 8): "Talbot should never
  be [thought] of as a mere soldier of fortune. He was a rich and
  powerful peer of the realm, from old noble stock, linked closely by
  family tradition and personal ties to the Lancastrian dynasty."

## 7. Reputation, English and French

- French: "he had for a long time been one of the most formidable
  scourges and sworn enemies of France, and had proved to be her
  dread and terror" (a Frenchman, quoted ch. 1, p. 1); remembered in
  Gascony and Normandy as "the terror of the French"; "as recently as
  the nineteenth century the people of Gascony… remembered the deeds
  of 'Le Roi Talbot' and mothers still used to check their children
  with the cry, 'the Talbot cometh'" (ch. 1, ~p. 2).
- English: the London chronicler — "fierce in fight and most dred of
  all other in France"; Hall's amplification — "his name and frame
  was spiteful and dreadful… women in Fraunce to feare their yonge
  children would cry — the Talbot cometh, the Talbot cometh";
  Polydore Vergil — "a man amongst men… his name was redowted above
  all others" (all ch. 1, pp. 1–3). Worcester's *Book of Noblesse*
  and Caxton's chivalric plea made him the emblem of the "right noble
  chivalry" in decay; Shakespeare's *1 Henry VI* the legend's apogee
  (ch. 1, ~pp. 3–4).
- The critical tradition, verbatim: Tait (DNB, 1890) — "Talbot was a
  sort of Hotspur, owing his reputation more to dash and daring than
  to any military genius… Patay was a rout from the beginning and
  Castillon a miscalculation" (ch. 1, ~p. 5); and the field's settled
  middle — "the most generous recent assessment remains that he was
  but the ablest commander during a period of declining military
  power and ultimate defeat" (ch. 1, ~p. 6).
