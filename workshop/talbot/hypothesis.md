# The Seize-the-Train Reading — Workshop Depth
## The full evidence inventory, the staff-method reconstruction, and what could settle it

## 1. The hypothesis, stated

That Talbot's charge at Castillon was not (only) honour-bound error
but a deliberate lunge at the war's decisive instrument: reading the
false report as a fleeting window — a train limbering to move is,
for an hour, capturable — he committed his vanguard to take the guns
whole, and the Bordeaux promise then held him to the attack after
the report was contradicted in his sight.

## 2. The inventory FOR

1. **The gun-seizure reflex was real English doctrine-in-practice.**
   At Formigny the archers charged out of a prepared position to
   *take* the two culverins — and took them, holding them until the
   counter-charge (Berry, in Stevenson 1863: 337; Blondel at 172).
   The one documented English tactical response to field artillery
   was capture, not endurance.
2. **No commander in Europe knew the train's effect better.** He had
   defended a wall against escalade at Rouen and lost the city to
   composition anyway (Escouchy i. 215–16); he had been a *term* in
   the surrender instruments — hostage, then adjudged prisoner when
   the system's clauses were breached (Blondel, in Stevenson 1863:
   142); Harfleur's seventeen-day fall ran while he was in French
   hands (Chartier ii. 178–79). The man understood, from inside,
   that against the train walls bought weeks, not seasons.
3. **The false report's tactical logic is exact.** A parked,
   entrenched train behind a ditch with 250 guns wheel to wheel
   (Pollard ch. 1, p. 1) is unassailable; a train limbering onto
   the road is artillery at its most vulnerable — dispersed teams,
   guns unlaid, powder cased. "Il est heure ou jamais" (Escouchy,
   ii. 38–39) is, on this reading, literally correct: the window
   for seizure is *only* the hour of movement.
4. **The commitment mechanism is documented.** The Bordelais had
   pressed his own ten-thousand-fighters promise back on him
   (Escouchy, ii. 34–35); a commander who turns back after
   proclaiming "now or never" before witnesses does not keep
   Gascony's confidence. Sunk honour is a real force; Pollard's own
   factor list includes "the position of his unfurled banner"
   [DIGEST-CARRIED].
5. **The unarmoured detail fits the reading rather than fighting
   it** — *if* the release oath is confirmed. A man sworn never
   again to bear arms against the French king (reported terms of
   his 1450 release — [BLOCKED: awaits Pollard chs. 2–8]) could
   direct but not fight; directing a *seizure* — a coup de main to
   be executed by others' hands — is exactly the shape of battle
   such a man could still intend. The white palfrey and the absent
   harness (Pollard ch. 1, p. 1, "according to some accounts")
   read, on this account, as compliance, not fatalism.

## 3. The inventory AGAINST — Pollard's counter-case at full strength

1. **The senior contemporary judgment is against it.** Basin:
   Talbot attacked "against the better advice of his lieutenants"
   because he was accustomed "to drive the enemy into flight by
   impetuous daring… thinking that his name alone would cause the
   enemy to take flight" (via Pollard ch. 1, ~p. 5). On Basin's
   reading the objective was rout-by-terror, not the guns.
2. **The critical tradition compounds it.** Tait: "Patay was a rout
   from the beginning and Castillon a miscalculation" (ch. 1, ~p.
   5); the field's middle position — "the ablest commander during a
   period of declining military power" (~p. 6) — treats Castillon
   as the limit of an operational, not a conceptual, talent.
3. **Pollard's own factor set suffices without intent:** faulty
   intelligence, momentum, honour, the unfurled banner
   [DIGEST-CARRIED, ch. 8's summary] — a complete causal story in
   which the guns are the *hazard*, not the *objective*.
4. **No positive evidence of intent exists.** No order, no letter,
   no council minute, no captured-officer interrogation states the
   guns as the objective. The hypothesis is built entirely from
   behavioural priors and situational logic — which is why the
   book stakes it only as a wager.
5. **The frontal form is wrong for a seizure.** A coup de main on
   limbered guns wants cavalry on the flanks of the column, not
   banners-forward infantry against a rampart. Counter-answer: the
   vanguard *did* reach the rampart (the standard planted for a
   moment — Pollard p. 1), and the form may reflect the ground —
   the ditch and river left one approach. The exchange is
   honest-scored: this point cuts against, and the reply is
   partial.

## 4. A commander's-intent reconstruction, by modern staff method

Frame it as a staff college would: mission analysis from the
commander's information state at H-hour minus one.
- **Known to Talbot:** his vanguard had broken the abbey outpost;
  the report said the park was emptying; his main body was hours
  behind; the Bordelais coalition expected battle; French
  reinforcement columns were converging on the Dordogne (Escouchy's
  narrative).
- **Courses open:** (a) wait to concentrate — loses the reported
  window, risks the reinforcement race; (b) withdraw on Bordeaux —
  politically near-impossible after "now or never"; (c) attack with
  the vanguard — only rational *if* the park is genuinely limbering
  (seizure) or *if* the garrison's morale is assumed brittle
  (Basin's terror-theory).
- **The discriminator** between (c)-seizure and (c)-terror is what
  he did at contact with disconfirmation: the old gentleman's
  report (Escouchy, ii. 39). A terror-theorist presses because
  terror needs momentum; a seizure-theorist *should* abort once the
  guns are known to be laid, not limbered. Talbot pressed. That
  favours Basin — unless the commitment mechanism (§2.4) had by
  then absorbed the decision. The reconstruction, honestly run,
  ends where the book ends: underdetermined, with the balance
  tilting by whichever prior you licence.

## 5. What could ever settle it — the sources that would need to exist

- **His orders.** A written disposition for the assault (objective
  clauses: "to take the ordnance" vs "to defeat the enemy") — none
  survives; nothing suggests one was written.
- **The council.** Minutes or a participant's memoir of the
  Anglo-Gascon command's dawn deliberation — none exists; the
  English side of Castillon left no narrative (the survivors'
  debrief, if taken, is lost).
- **The interrogations.** French examination records of captured
  English officers — Escouchy writes "selon la rellacion de ceulx
  qui bien seurent la vérité" (i. 216, of Rouen) — his Castillon
  informants are unnamed and their statements unpreserved.
- **The release instrument.** The 1450 oath's exact text (never to
  bear arms against the French king?) — if it exists in the French
  royal archives or in Pollard's chs. 2–8 citations, it settles
  §2.5's limb and re-weights the whole inventory.
- **Wardrobe/armoury accounts** for the 1452–53 expedition — could
  confirm or kill the no-armour tradition Pollard carries hedged.
Absent these, the reading is permanently a wager — which is what
the SPINE ranks it, and why the manuscript stages it against
Pollard rather than over him.
