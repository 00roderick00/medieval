## 1. The railroads — ICC 1887, Hepburn 1906 [T1 IN CORPUS: 24 Stat. 379; 34 Stat. 584]

**Dependency.** Carrier discretion over rates and access — who ships,
at what price, which town lives. The statute's own targets: "any
special rate, rebate, drawback, or other device" (§2, 24 Stat.
379–80); "undue or unreasonable preference or advantage to any
particular person, company, firm, corporation, or locality" (§3, p.
380).

**Instrument.** Common carriage and rate regulation with a standing
commission — no structural act. §1: all charges "shall be reasonable
and just; and every unjust and unreasonable charge for such service
is prohibited and declared to be unlawful"; §11: "a Commission is
hereby created and established to be known as the Inter-State
Commerce Commission" (p. 383). Approved 4 February 1887. The 1887
text contains no divestiture, dissolution or forfeiture-of-assets
provision (searched; none). The 1887 ICC could prohibit and
investigate; the power to PRESCRIBE came with Hepburn (approved 29
June 1906), amended §15: the Commission "authorized and empowered…
to determine and prescribe what will be the just and reasonable rate
or rates… to be thereafter observed in such case as the maximum to
be charged" (34 Stat., §4), orders in force up to two years. Hepburn
also made oil pipelines common carriers (the off-switch probe's §3
anchor).

**The buried structural rule — the probe's first finding.** Hepburn
carries a genuine line-of-business separation INSIDE the regulatory
statute, the commodities clause: "From and after May first, nineteen
hundred and eight, it shall be unlawful for any railroad company to
transport… any article or commodity, other than timber and the
manufactured products thereof, manufactured, mined, or produced by
it, or under its authority, or which it may own in whole, or in
part, or in which it may have any interest direct or indirect,"
excepting articles for its own carrier use. The carrier may not
carry its own goods — a self-preferencing ban in structural form,
enacted five years before the Standard Oil dissolution.

**Severed / left.** Severed: unilateral tariff discretion, the
rebate, the preference, and (1908) carriage-of-own-commodities.
Left: ownership of the roads, private operation, the revenues.
**Fortune:** not addressed by these statutes; no in-repo T2 speaks
to the railroad fortunes — [UNVERIFIED — RETRIEVAL], not needed.

**Sequence.** Regulation IS the whole case: the series opens with
twenty-four years of carriage regulation before any structural
dissolution anywhere. The lag finding's baseline.

## 3. The Money Trust, 1913–14 [Clayton and FTC Acts T1 IN CORPUS; the Federal Reserve Act NOT — [UNVERIFIED — RETRIEVAL]]

**Dependency.** Coordinated control of credit and corporate
governance through interlocking directorates and a private liquidity
syndicate. The in-corpus T1 evidence for how Congress framed it is
the instrument itself: Clayton §8 bans the shared-governance device
directly.

**Instrument — three legs, none structural.** (i) A public
substitute institution: the Federal Reserve Act (Pub. L. 63-43, 38
Stat. 251, 1913) — its text is NOT in corpus; the claim that it
displaced the private liquidity function is [UNVERIFIED — RETRIEVAL:
FRASER NARA scan, URL in retrieval-master]. (ii) The interlock ban,
verbatim (Clayton Act, approved 15 October 1914): "from and after
two years from the date of the approval of this Act no person shall
at the same time be a director or other officer or employee of more
than one bank, banking association or trust company… [with]
deposits, capital, surplus, and undivided profits aggregating more
than $5,000,000"; and "no person at the same time shall be a
director in any two or more corporations, any one of which has
capital, surplus, and undivided profits aggregating more than
$1,000,000… if such corporations are or shall have been
theretofore, by virtue of their business and location of operation,
competitors" (§8, 38 Stat. 732–33). Alongside it §7: "no corporation
engaged in commerce shall acquire, directly or indirectly, the whole
or any part of the stock or other share capital of another
corporation engaged also in commerce, where the effect of such
acquisition may be to substantially lessen competition" (38 Stat.
731–32) — the stock-acquisition gate DuPont–GM would run through
forty years later. (iii) A standing commission: "unfair methods of
competition in commerce are hereby declared unlawful," the FTC
"empowered and directed to prevent" them by complaint, hearing and
cease-and-desist order (FTC Act §5, 38 Stat. 719–20, approved 26
September 1914). Remedies across both statutes are injunctive and
prospective; nothing transfers property.

**Severed / left.** Severed: the lawful basis of governance-network
coordination — the seat on the rival's board; prospectively, the
stock route to combination. Left: the banks, the partnerships, the
capital. **Fortune:** no in-repo source — [UNVERIFIED — RETRIEVAL].

**Sequence.** No structural act, ever: the republic answered a
control NETWORK with a public institution, a governance rule and a
standing commission. Regulation not preceding structure but
replacing it.

## 4. Glass–Steagall, 1933 [T1 IN CORPUS: Pub. L. 73-66, 48 Stat. 162, FRASER scan]

**Dependency.** The universal bank: "government-supported deposits,
lending relationships, securities promotion, and speculative risk
under one roof" is the playbook's gloss; the statute's own device is
the definition of incompatible combinations.

**Instrument — the line-of-business firebreak, verbatim.** Approved
16 June 1933. §16: dealing in investment securities "limited to
purchasing and selling such securities without recourse, solely upon
the order, and for the account of, customers, and in no case for its
own account, and the association shall not underwrite any issue of
securities" (with US/state/municipal exemptions; effective one year
after approval). §20: "no member bank shall be affiliated… with any
corporation… engaged principally in the issue, flotation,
underwriting, public sale, or distribution… of stocks, bonds,
debentures, notes, or other securities" (after one year; $1,000/day
penalty; forfeiture of charter or Fed membership after six months'
warning). §21: any organisation "engaged in the business of issuing,
underwriting, selling, or distributing… securities" barred from
"the business of receiving deposits" (after one year; criminal
penalty; binds EVERYONE, not just member banks). §32: officer and
director interlocks between member banks and securities firms banned
from 1 January 1934, "unless… there is a permit therefor issued by
the Federal Reserve Board."

**Severed / left.** The extraction's conclusion, verified against
every operative section: "No provision transfers any property or
requires sale to the state… the statute's entire enforcement
apparatus is… definitional restriction… civil per-diem penalties…
forfeiture of federal charter/Fed membership… criminal fine…
prohibition subject to a permit. The state acquires nothing."
Owners choose sides within the one-year windows; the statute never
uses "divest." Severed: the combination. Left: the equity and
either business. **Fortune:** not addressed at T1; [UNVERIFIED —
RETRIEVAL].

**Sequence.** The structural statute operates THROUGH the
pre-existing regulatory membrane: its sanctions are forfeitures of
membership in a system built in 1913, and its escape valve is a
regulator's permit. Supervision precedes; the firebreak is bolted
onto it.

## 8. Paramount, 1948 [OPINION T1 IN CORPUS: 334 U.S. 131, U.S. Reports scan]

**Dependency.** The first-run exhibition bottleneck plus copyright
licensing practices. The Court's figures (at 167): the five majors
held 3,137 of 18,076 theatres (~17%), but "In the 92 cities of the
country with populations over 100,000 at least 70 per cent of all
the first-run theatres are affiliated with one or more of the five
majors… In 38 of those cities there are no independent first-run
theatres." "The main contest is over the cream of the exhibition
business — that of the first-run theatres" (166–67).

**Instrument.** Sherman §§1–2. Practices condemned and enjoined:
horizontal and vertical price-fixing of admission prices (141–44),
unreasonable clearances, pooling agreements ("Clearer restraints of
trade are difficult to imagine," 149–50), joint ownership between
exhibitor-defendants dissolved (149–51), formula deals and master
agreements ("The pooling of the purchasing power of an entire
circuit in bidding for films is a misuse of monopoly power,"
154–55), block-booking ("All we hold to be illegal is a refusal to
license one or more copyrights unless another copyright is
accepted," 159). Vertical integration itself was held NOT per se
illegal (173–74). The Court struck the district court's
competitive-bidding remedy ("involves the judiciary so deeply in
the daily operation of this nation-wide business… it should not be
undertaken," 162) and remanded divestiture under the fruits
standard: the court's "function includes undoing what the
conspiracy achieved… the fruits of the conspiracy which are denied
the independents must also be denied the five majors" (171–72).

**Severed / left — the probe's second finding.** BY THIS TEXT:
practices severed, no assets. The opinion orders no divestiture; the
only consent decree it mentions is the pre-trial decree of 20
November 1940 (142 n.3); the theatre divestitures rest entirely on
the 1948–49 decrees, which are NOT in corpus — [UNVERIFIED —
RETRIEVAL: the Paramount consent decrees]. At this text's close the
majors keep production (monopoly in production "eliminated as an
issue," 166), distribution and the theatres; even the
anti-expansion injunction was vacated for a "fresh start" (175).
**Fortune:** not addressed; [UNVERIFIED — RETRIEVAL].

**Sequence.** A behavioural consent decree (1940) precedes; no
sector regulator ever existed for exhibition; the structural act
post-dates the opinion. Judicial line only — the one verified case
where carriage-style regulation played no part.

## 9. Alcoa, 1945 [OPINION T1 IN CORPUS: 148 F.2d 416, CourtListener text]

**Dependency.** Over ninety per cent of virgin ingot — the primary
input on which all fabricators depended ("They were its market,"
*431) — maintained by pre-emptive capacity expansion.

**Instrument.** Sherman §2, liability from structure plus intent to
hold it. The percentages (at *424): "That percentage is enough to
constitute a monopoly; it is doubtful whether sixty or sixty-four
percent would be enough; and certainly thirty-three per cent is
not." The exclusion holding (at *430–31): "It was not inevitable
that it should always anticipate increases in the demand for ingot
and be prepared to supply them… we can think of no more effective
exclusion than progressively to embrace each new opportunity as it
opened, and to face every newcomer with new capacity already geared
into a great organization." The concession that bounds it (at
*429–30): "monopoly may have been thrust upon it… The successful
competitor, having been urged to compete, must not be turned upon
when he wins."

**Severed / left — the probe's third finding.** BY THIS TEXT:
nothing structural. Hand expressly deferred dissolution:
"Dissolution is not a penalty but a remedy; if the industry will not
need it for its protection, it will be a disservice to break up an
aggregation which has for so long demonstrated its efficiency"
(*446). Relief actually granted: injunctions against the price
squeeze and the 1936 cartel; the prayer that common shareholders of
Alcoa and Aluminium Ltd divest one or the other was DENIED (*447).
The whole of Alcoa stayed in its owners' hands. **Fortune:**
untouched by this text, definitionally.

**Sequence — the series' unique row.** The remedy Hand waited on was
the STATE'S OWN CAPACITY: the Surplus Property Act disposal of the
government's war-built aluminium plants would set "a plan or design
for the industry as a whole," and only if that failed would
dissolution arise — "then, but then only" (*446–47). Public
capacity-building as the structural remedy's substitute: the row in
the pattern where the republic answered a private bottleneck by
having built plant of its own.

## 10. DuPont–GM, 1957 [OPINION T1 IN CORPUS: 353 U.S. 586, U.S. Reports scan]

**Dependency.** A strategic equity link converting ownership into
preferred access: du Pont's 23% of GM (1917–19) and "du Pont's
commanding position as General Motors' supplier of automotive
finishes and fabrics… promoted by its stock interest and… not
gained solely on competitive merit" (605); the stock "purposely
employed… to pry open the General Motors market" (606); Raskob's
1917 Treasurer's Report quoted at 601–02 ("will undoubtedly secure
for us the entire Fabrikoid, Pyralin, paint and varnish business").

**Instrument.** Clayton §7 (pre-1950 text), reaching a
thirty-year-old acquisition: "the Government may proceed at any time
that an acquisition may be said with reasonable probability to
contain a threat" (597–98); "any acquisition by one corporation of
all or any part of the stock of another corporation, competitor or
not, is within the reach of the section" (592).

**Severed / left — the probe's fourth finding.** BY THIS TEXT:
nothing yet. The opinion reverses the dismissal and remands "for a
determination, after further hearing, of the equitable relief
necessary and appropriate" (607–08); the divestiture belongs to the
later remedy proceedings (the 1961 decision — its citation, 366
U.S., appears in corpus only as quoted in *Microsoft*, 253 F.3d 34:
divestiture "has traditionally been the remedy for Sherman Act
violations whose heart is intercorporate combination and control").
The remedy decision itself is NOT in corpus — [UNVERIFIED —
RETRIEVAL]. **Fortune:** not addressed; the instrument targets the
LINK, not the holdings' value — but that reading completes only
with the 1961 text.

**Sequence.** The gate was legislated in 1914 (Clayton §7, in
corpus); the acquisition predates it (1917–19 — a §7 applied to a
pre-existing position through the "any time the acquisition
threatens to ripen" doctrine); suit 1949; liability 1957; structure
later still. The longest regulation-to-structure lag in the series
— four decades.

## 11. AT&T, 1956 and 1982 [MFJ OPINION T1 IN CORPUS: 552 F. Supp. 131; the 1956 decree known only through the MFJ's recitation]

**Dependency.** "The principal means by which AT&T has maintained
monopoly power in telecommunications has been its control of the
Operating Companies with their strategic bottleneck position" (*171)
— every rival's calls originated and terminated on AT&T's local
exchanges.

**Instrument — two stages.** (i) 1956 (as this opinion describes it;
the decree itself NOT in corpus): "an injunction was issued which
precluded AT&T from engaging in any business other than the
provision of common carrier communications services; precluded
Western Electric from manufacturing equipment other than that used
by the Bell System; and required the defendants to license their
patents to all applicants upon the payment of appropriate
royalties" (*137–38); non-exclusive licences for "all existing and
future Bell System patents" (§X, at *178). (ii) 1982: divestiture
of the twenty-two Operating Companies; MFJ §II(D) quarantining the
divested BOCs ("no BOC shall… provide interexchange
telecommunications services or information services; manufacture or
provide telecommunications products or customer premises
equipment…; or provide any other product or service… that is not a
natural monopoly service actually regulated by tariff"); §II(A)
equal access "on an unbundled, tariffed basis, that is equal in
type, quality, and price to that provided to AT&T and its
affiliates."

**Severed / left.** Severed: the bottleneck itself — and by spin-off
to the owners, in the decree's own words (§I(A)(4)): divestiture by
"a spin-off of stock of the separated BOCs to the shareholders of
AT&T," the decree declining to fix the number of successor
entities. Left: Long Lines, Western Electric, Bell Laboratories,
and freedom from the 1956 confinement — "AT&T would be free to
compete in all facets of the marketplace" (*170–71). **Fortune:**
the wealth-preserving mechanism is IN the decree at T1 — the
shareholders receive the successors' stock, exactly the Standard
Oil design. (Subsequent value: not addressed; not needed.)

**Sequence — the lag thesis's fullest confirmation, in the court's
own words.** Rate regulation from the Communications Act framework;
the 1949 suit; the 1956 confinement-and-licensing decree; then (at
*170): "The antitrust suit brought by the Department of Justice in
1949 ended in 1956 with a consent decree which imposed injunctive
relief that was patently inadequate… Future regulatory and
injunctive remedies are unlikely to be more successful than were
similar efforts in the past" — and the FCC's own former Common
Carrier Bureau chiefs testifying "that the Commission is not and
never has been capable of effective enforcement of the laws
governing AT&T's behavior" (*168). Regulation preceded, was tried
for decades, failed on the record, and structure followed. Greene
also supplies the book's lineage sentence, quoting Senator Sherman
(21 Cong. Rec. 2457 (1890)): "If we will not endure a king as a
political power we should not endure a king over the production,
transportation, and sale of any of the necessaries of life" — the
over-mighty citizen, in the instrument's own legislative record.

**Playbook checks.** (i) "The 1984 reorganization created seven
Regional Bell Operating Companies" — the SEVEN was AT&T's
reorganisation plan under the decree, not a decree term: §I(A)(4)
expressly provides "nothing in this Modification of Final Judgment
shall require or prohibit the consolidation of the ownership of the
BOCs into any particular number of entities"; the opinion records
AT&T's plan for seven (n.41; *201). (ii) The playbook's "confined
AT&T largely to regulated communications and government work": the
"government work" element is NOT in this opinion's summary of the
1956 decree — [UNVERIFIED — RETRIEVAL: the 1956 decree text] before
any draft carries it.

## 13. Microsoft, 2001–02 [T1 IN CORPUS: 253 F.3d 34; Final Judgment, D.D.C., 12 Nov 2002]

**Dependency.** The OEM distribution channel and the middleware/API
layer protecting the operating-system monopoly (>95% share; the
applications barrier to entry — liability "in its entirety" on
monopoly power, 253 F.3d at *51).

**Instrument.** Litigated Sherman §2 monopoly-maintenance liability,
affirmed (*46); the district court's two-way BREAKUP VACATED for
three stated reasons — no evidentiary hearing, inadequate reasons,
"we have drastically altered the scope of Microsoft's liability"
(*107) — with the structural-remedy caution ("divestiture is a
remedy that is imposed only with great caution," *80) and the tempo
warning (*49): "six years seems like an eternity in the computer
industry. By the time a court can assess liability, firms,
products, and the marketplace are likely to have changed
dramatically." Then the 2002 consent Final Judgment, conduct only:
§III.E compulsory communications-protocol licensing "on reasonable
and non-discriminatory terms"; §III.D API disclosure; §III.H
end-user/OEM freedom to remove Microsoft middleware and designate
rivals' defaults; §§III.A/F anti-retaliation; §III.G
exclusive-dealing ban; §IV.B a three-expert Technical Committee; §V
a five-year term. The extraction confirms: "no divestiture,
break-up, spin-off, or structural reorganisation provision anywhere
in the Final Judgment."

**Severed / left.** Severed: the closed interface and the
distribution chokehold — temporarily (five years) and by conduct
rule, not by ownership change. Left: Windows whole, the company
intact, the fortune untouched. **Fortune:** not addressed at T1;
[UNVERIFIED — RETRIEVAL] — though the decree's non-structural form
makes the inference trivial, the column stays honest.

**Sequence — the counter-lag case.** Structure was attempted FIRST
(the 2000 breakup order) and abandoned at the threshold; conduct
remedies replaced it. Where AT&T's court reached structure after
regulation demonstrably failed, Microsoft's court retreated from
structure to conduct because the market's tempo outran the
litigation's. The one verified case where the republic blinked at
the structural act — load-bearing for the Parrott engagement
(critiques.md, this date).

## Instruments beyond the cases [T1 IN CORPUS]

Two toolkit statutes verified for the mirror table: **HSR 1976** (90
Stat. 1383, approved 30 September 1976) — §7A pure
notice-and-waiting machinery ("no person shall acquire… unless both
persons… file notification… and the waiting period… has expired";
30 days, 15 for cash tenders; filings to FTC and DOJ jointly;
prohibits nothing substantively — the gate before consolidation).
**Telecommunications Act 1996** (approved 8 February 1996) — §251(c)(3):
"nondiscriminatory access to network elements on an unbundled basis
at any technically feasible point on rates, terms, and conditions
that are just, reasonable, and nondiscriminatory…" — access
mandated, ownership intact (the statute's own possessives locate
every element in the incumbent).

**The First Amendment limit (T1 in corpus: Moody v. NetChoice, 603
U.S. ___ (2024), slip op.).** The pattern's instruments reach
economic switches; they do not reach doctrine. "Expressive activity
includes presenting a curated compilation of speech originally
created by others" (slip op. 14); "The government may not, in
supposed pursuit of better expressive balance, alter a private
speaker's own editorial choices about the mix of speech it wants to
convey" (19–20); "On the spectrum of dangers to free expression,
there are few greater than allowing the government to change the
speech of private actors in order to achieve its own conception of
speech nirvana" (27–28). The boundary the Court itself draws is the
one the mirror table must respect: "Curating a feed and
transmitting direct messages, one might think, involve different
levels of editorial choice, so that the one creates an expressive
product and the other does not" (11) — the Court's own list of
maybe-non-expressive functions (direct messaging, Gmail filtering,
Etsy reviews, Venmo, Uber; 10–11) marks where the economic
instruments may still run. Precision for the apparatus: the
curation-as-expression discussion is guidance on remand — the
judgments were vacated on facial-analysis grounds — and the
expressive/non-expressive line is unresolved per function, which is
precisely why the mirror confines the pattern's speech-layer
instruments to process, portability, interoperability and
non-expressive infrastructure. The Henrician disanalogy conceded at
ch12 memo Revisions 3 stands: the American state may absorb the
platforms' functions; it cannot rule their creed.
