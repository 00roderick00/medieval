## II. Theoretical Reconstruction

**Philip G. Cerny, "Terrorism and the New Security Dilemma," *Naval War College Review* 58:1 (2005), 11–33.** *[T2]*
The freely-available companion to Cerny's paywalled 1998 *Civil Wars* article, and the book's working text for the neomedieval **diagnostic checklist**. Contributes the "durable disorder" concept — a heterarchical order that is a mid-point, not a terminus — and the enumerated characteristics of a neomedieval world (competing institutions with overlapping jurisdictions; fluid boundaries; uneven consolidation; fragmented identities; expanding legal "grey zones"). Most valuable to the mechanism argument: Cerny names *exogenous competitive pressure* as the consolidation driver now absent, the very variable the book re-introduces (weapon capital-intensity) to predict re-consolidation. *Caveat:* the 2005 piece enumerates **five** characteristics; the outline's **six**-fold checklist derives from the 1998 original (retrieval list). — Engaged in **Ch1** (checklist), **Ch3** (property-rights & grey-zones), **Ch5** (hollow sovereignty), **Ch8** (grey zones), Introduction, Ch12 & Appendix C.

**Stephen J. Kobrin, "Back to the Future: Neomedievalism and the Postmodern Digital World Economy," *Journal of International Affairs* 51:2 (1998), 361–386** (bundled as the 1999 Routledge author manuscript). *[T2]*
The origin of the book's **platforms line**. Kobrin argues a digital, networked world economy dissolves the territorial basis of jurisdiction: in cyberspace one cannot say where a transaction occurs, whose law applies, or who may tax it — so singular territorial authority gives way to overlapping, heteronomous authorities. Treats NGOs, the WTO, and standard-setting bodies as genuine political authorities, not mere market actors — the seed of the "platforms as institutional universalisms" move the book completes. Notably, Kobrin himself anticipates *re-centralization* (a supranational authority with taxation and enforcement powers), which the book redirects from the supranational to the intra-bloc terminus. *Caveat:* cite published *JIA*/Routledge pagination, not the manuscript's. — Engaged in **Ch2** (central), **Ch4**, Ch11, Ch12, Appendix D.

## III. Security, Private Force, and the Military Dimension

**Sean McFate, *Durable Disorder: The Return of Private Armies and the Emergence of Neomedievalism*, PhD thesis, LSE, 2011** (the apparatus behind *The Modern Mercenary*, OUP 2014). *[T2]*
The book's evidence for the **return of the market for force**. McFate's puzzle — that the chief customer for private armies is the strongest state, not the weakest — grounds his claim that the eroding mercenary taboo restores "the final piece of Bull's neomedievalism," private international violence. Supplies the condottieri lineage (*condottieri* = "contractors") and, crucially, the **disanalogy the book's Part III turns on**: the modern market is a *monopsony* (the US as consumer-in-chief), and modern PMCs depend on state-provided stability — banking, contract enforcement — where the medieval condottiere built independent fiscal bases. He does not use "bastard feudalism"; that framing is the book's. — Engaged in **Ch3** (central), **Ch4** (condottieri; Prigozhin), Ch11, Ch12.

**Phil Williams, *From the New Middle Ages to a New Dark Age: The Decline of the State and U.S. Strategy* (SSI / U.S. Army War College, 2008).** *[T3]*
The **pessimist counter-read**. Williams accepts the neomedieval diagnosis but rejects its complacency: "durable disorder" is not a stable equilibrium but a way-station that can tip, abruptly and non-linearly, into a "New Dark Age." Contributes the key qualifier for the interstitial spaces — that "ungoverned" zones (favelas, Sadr City, Somalia) are *alternatively* governed by militias and traffickers acting as surrogate authorities — and the "stateocentrism" blind spot. *Caveat:* as government/think-tank analysis its structural claims want corroboration (T3); and chronologically Williams (2008) *precedes* McFate (2014), so the book must stage the McFate–Williams contrast itself rather than present Williams as replying. — Engaged in **Ch3** (counter-read), Ch5, Ch11–Ch12.

## IV. European / Regional Application

**Ole Wæver, "European Security Identities," *Journal of Common Market Studies* 34:1 (1996), 103–132.** *[T2]*
The accessible, page-verifiable statement of the Copenhagen School reading of European order, standing in for the two paywalled target Wæver chapters (retrieval list). Contributes the securitization lens — security as a speech act with distinct referent objects (state = sovereignty, nation = identity, Europe = integration) — and, directly useful to the EU-as-neomedieval argument, Wæver's own invocation of a "new middle ages" of contested sovereignty organised as **concentric circles** around a "westernistic" centre, with Europe "systemically post-sovereign while the units retain their sovereignty." Grounds the book's treatment of the EU as the most advanced neomedieval polity in a securitization-theorist's own terms, not the book's imposition. — Engaged in **Ch11** (EU regulatory church), Introduction, Ch1, Ch12, Appendix D.

## V. Popular / Strategic Synthesis

**Anne-Marie Slaughter, *A New World Order* (Princeton UP, 2004), ch. 5, "An Effective World Order," and ch. 6, "A Just World Order."** *[T2]*
The **optimist's read of the dispersal phase**, engaged as "important and wrong." Slaughter's disaggregated-sovereignty thesis holds that the state is not disappearing but disaggregating into transnational networks of courts, regulators, and legislators; ch. 5 argues these networks can govern durably through soft power and "regulation by information," and ch. 6 supplies five norms to make such governance just. The book treats this as the connective tissue of an interregnum mistaken for load-bearing architecture: where Slaughter sees networks *dispersing* power, the artillery-state mechanism predicts the underlying stacks *concentrating* it and driving re-consolidation. Her conceded "ineradicability of power" is the pivotal wrong turn. — Engaged in **Ch1** & **Ch2** (the alternative reading rejected), Ch12, Introduction/Positioning.

**Robert D. Kaplan, "The Coming Anarchy," *The Atlantic Monthly* 273:2 (Feb. 1994), 44–76.** *[T3]*
The **atmospheric predecessor**, engaged critically. Kaplan reads West Africa as a bellwether of a "coming anarchy" — states hollowed out by scarcity, disease, tribalism, crime — and gestures at a pre-Westphalian "Last Map" of moving power-centres "as in the Middle Ages." Contributes the register the later theorists systematise, and a clean point of divergence: Kaplan's causal engine is Malthusian scarcity, not the book's force capital-intensity. Positioned as an instance of the recurring **declinism-cycle** (every generation announcing the end of the state) that the introduction inoculates against. *Caveat:* Kaplan uses no rigorous neomedieval framework — influence and atmosphere only, never analytical scaffolding. — Engaged in **Introduction** (declinism-cycle), **Ch1**, Appendix D.

**Parag Khanna, "The Next Big Thing: Neomedievalism," *Foreign Policy* no. 172 (May/June 2009).** *[T3]* ⚠️ **retrieval list**
The **popular-synthesis marker** of the neomedieval diagnosis — a mass-audience packaging of the Bull/Kobrin/Friedrichs thesis (overlapping authorities: corporations, city-states, NGOs, PMCs, would-be empires), in an admonitory "new Dark Age" register. Framed as *resemblance*, so any use flags **[ANALOGY-ONLY]** per doctrine §2. **The bundled PDF is a defective web capture (article body absent); it cannot carry a page-level citation and is on the retrieval list for a clean full text.** The annotation above is provisional pending that copy. — Engaged in Introduction, Ch1, Appendix D.

### New section proposed: I-bis. The vocabulary the literature borrowed (lineage before Bull's readers)

**Charles Tilly, "Western State-Making and Theories of Political Transformation," in Tilly (ed.), *The Formation of National States in Western Europe* (Princeton UP, 1975), ch. 9, pp. 601–638.** *[T2 — IN REPO (corpus holds ch. 9 only; OCR sidecar `Tilly-1975-Western-State-Making-ocr.txt`, printed p. N = PDF p. N−600)]*
The founder of the bellicist school as a neomedievalist two years before Bull, and with technology as the disperser. The chapter's close: "At the same time as the state system absorbs the entire world, the individual state may be losing part of its significance… Perhaps control of a contiguous territory was peculiarly advantageous to the land- and water-bound technologies of the European state-making eras, but an obstacle to full exploitation of technologies of flight, electric power and electronic information-handling… Perhaps, unknowing, we are writing obituaries for the state" (p. 638 / PDF 38; confirmed in sidecar), and, reporting Johnson 1972, the suggestion "that the multinational corporation is superseding the national state as a repository of power" (p. 630 / PDF 30; confirmed). What it contributes to this appendix is a dated statement, from the school the book's mechanism extends, that the dispersal was expected and that information technology was expected to cause it — the very variable the book now runs in reverse (spine §7: information technology re-concentrating). Concedes to Bull, in advance, the diagnosis; denies nothing, because the passage is a "perhaps." The book's difference (c): the same technological family, priced at the decisive layer, re-concentrates rather than disperses. *Caveat:* the volume's slogan ("war made the state, and the state made war") is in ch. 1, p. 42, not in the retrieved chapter; the 1975 chapter is not to be cited for it. Chronology: 1975, before Bull 1977; superseded on state formation by Tilly 1990 (ch10's text), not on this point. — Engaged in **Ch1** (by name, beside Tilly 1990 pp. 3–4, 225), Introduction (method: the "relationships among variables" sentence, p. 601 / PDF 1, confirmed), Ch10, Ch12.

**John Gerard Ruggie, "Territoriality and Beyond: Problematizing Modernity in International Relations," *International Organization* 47:1 (Winter 1993), 139–174.** *[T2 — IN REPO (JSTOR scan; sidecar `Ruggie.txt`, printed p. N = PDF p. N−137; the OCR runs words together and scrambles the two-column page at column breaks, so wording is confirmed but spacing restored, and every verbatim use must be re-read against the page image before it enters a chapter)]*
The literature's lexicographer, not one of its members. Ruggie supplies the vocabulary of medieval plurality that Kobrin 1998 imported into the neomedieval line — medieval Europe as "the archetype of nonexclusive territorial rule" (p. 149 / PDF 12; confirmed), a system "in which authority was both personalized and parcelized within and across territorial formations and for which inclusive bases of legitimation prevailed" (p. 150 / PDF 13; confirmed, across a column break), against the modern form that "has differentiated its subject collectivity into territorially defined, fixed, and mutually exclusive enclaves of legitimate dominion" (p. 151 / PDF 14) — and the "unbundling" of territoriality "(of which the doctrine of extraterritoriality was the first and most enduring instantiation)" as the state's own "generic contrivance" for living with its exclusivity (p. 165 / PDF 28; confirmed). His diagnosis of the present is the European Community as "the first 'multiperspectival polity' to emerge since the advent of the modern era" (p. 172 / PDF 35; confirmed), "nothing less than the emergence of the first truly postmodern international political form" (p. 140 / PDF 3; confirmed), closing: "Unbundled territoriality is not located some place else; but it is becoming another place" (p. 174 / PDF 37; confirmed). What he concedes to Bull: nothing by name — Bull and the "new medievalism" are absent from the article (sidecar searched: no "Bull," no "medievalism"); his one present-to-medieval analogy is hedged, "somewhat in the manner of medieval rulers vis-à-vis cosmopolitan bodies of religion and law" (p. 173 / PDF 36; confirmed). What he denies is the book's kind of explanation: the weapons-driven account "has at least two serious shortcomings" — "its retrodictive value is severely limited" because the Italian city-states and the Hanse were viable "for the better part of two centuries," and the leap from functional pressure to the specific modern form "entails a claim of staggering historical and intellectual proportions" (p. 156 / PDF 19; confirmed) — and, of the medieval-to-modern transformation, "while there may be law-like generalizations in the … transformation, there are none of it" (p. 169 / PDF 32; confirmed). This appendix records him as the strongest theoretical statement of the anti-determinism objection, and records what he gives the book in the same breath: the decades, from a writer with no fiscal-military thesis — "The turn came suddenly. By the end of the century, strong centralized administration had 'almost completely transformed the political life of western and west-central Europe'" (p. 161 / PDF 24; confirmed); the state "invented by them twice, once in the leading cities of the Italian Renaissance and once again in the kingdoms north of the Alps" (p. 166 / PDF 29; confirmed); and the invitation the book answers, that no epochal thought on the territorial state had appeared "since 1957, when John Herz published his essay, 'Rise and Demise of the Territorial State'" (p. 143 / PDF 6; confirmed). The book's answer (c), per the assessment: Ruggie attacks a claim the book no longer makes — that weapons determined the *form* — since spine §8(g) rules the form open; and his two centuries of viable alternatives are the two centuries *before* the 1440–1500 ratchet, whose end is the book's evidence. Contested interpretation (b): Ruggie's social-epistemology account of the transition (single-point perspective; sovereignty as "merely the doctrinal counterpart," p. 159 / PDF 22) against the bellicist school. — Engaged in **Ch1** §II (the attribution repair below) and §VI (by name, the IR canon's statement of the decades), **Ch12** §§I, II, IV (Kapstein's satellite; the fairs; the Pentagon's "reconstitution" baffled, n. 143), **Ch2** and **Ch4** (footnotes: "little islands of alien sovereignty," p. 165 / PDF 28, confirmed), **Ch10** §I, Coda (p. 169 answered), Appendix C (the EU sub-wager's null).

### II. Theoretical reconstruction — additions (the second-generation refutation of the borderless thesis)

**Jack Goldsmith and Tim Wu, *Who Controls the Internet? Illusions of a Borderless World* (Oxford UP, 2006).** *[T2 for the argument and the ICANN/root narrative; T3 for the China reportage (Zittrain–Edelman and Reporters Without Borders studies, to be cited direct if load-bearing) — IN REPO (sidecar `Goldsmith-Wu.txt`, printed p. N = PDF p. N+13; front matter p. vii = PDF 8)]*
The earliest sustained refutation, from the legal side, of the 1990s claim that a borderless medium would dissolve territorial rule — and so the earliest canonical statement of the book's Part I correction that the dispersal was transitional. Their story is "the birth and early years of a new kind of Internet—a bordered network where territorial law, government power, and international relations matter as much as technological invention" (p. vii / PDF 8; confirmed); "the Internet is splitting apart and becoming bordered" (p. viii / PDF 9; confirmed); "Behind the mists and magic of the Internet lies an older and stronger order whose relevance remains inescapable" (p. ix / PDF 10; confirmed). The mechanism is coercion of local intermediaries: "the Internet has made the network itself the intermediary" (p. 70 / PDF 83; confirmed); the intermediaries "are invisible, but they are there. And they can be controlled" (p. 70 / PDF 83; confirmed). Their capacity test on the internationalists is Ch1 §V's test on Slaughter, run in 2006: "not a single treaty has been drafted and ratified related to issues like defamation, gambling, speech, privacy, and the like" (p. 167 / PDF 180; confirmed); the root fight "is indisputably between national governments" (p. 171 / PDF 184; confirmed); and the forecast is the bloc casting avant la lettre — "a technological version of the cold war" among the United States, China and Europe (p. 184 / PDF 197; confirmed), "what we once called a global network is becoming a collection of nation-state networks" (p. 149 / PDF 162; confirmed). What they concede to Bull: nothing by name (Bull and "medieval" absent from the sidecar); they stand in the Kobrin line as its refutation, and their intermediary is always pliant — the 2006 blind spot, in which no intermediary holds decisive capability, is the void Ch2 charges Kobrin with, reproduced eight years later. What they deny the book (b, the rival mechanism at full strength): the legibility layer was re-territorialised by cheap legal coercion of local chokepoints, with no artillery-scale investment and no fiscal-administrative construction. The book's answer (c): their chokepoints are the canon-and-register layer, which Ch11's fifth move already holds cheap; what coercion cannot do — build the fab, the model, the constellation — is the mechanism's subject; and the concession must be made in text as a layered tense (accomplished for canon and register, compelled and unaccomplished for the decisive stack), a register amendment to spine §7 for Roderick's ruling. Their gift is ICANN as the Sforza form: the United States "never actually ceded control over either ICANN or the root" (p. 169 / PDF 182; confirmed) — function delegated, title and revocation kept for a generation. — Engaged in **Ch1** §V (beside Slaughter), **Ch2** §§II, III, V (the root contest as the first completed investiture case; "the effective sovereign of global privacy law," p. 174 / PDF 187, confirmed), Ch10 §VIII, Ch11 Move five, **Ch12** §§II–IV, Appendix C, Coda §VI.

**Henry Farrell and Abraham L. Newman, "Weaponized Interdependence: How Global Economic Networks Shape State Coercion," *International Security* 44:1 (Summer 2019), 42–79.** *[T2 — IN REPO (sidecar `FN-article.txt`, printed p. N−41; ligatures garbled in the sidecar, restored here)]* With the narrative extension, **Farrell and Newman, *Underground Empire: How America Weaponized the World Economy* (Henry Holt, 2023)** *[T3 — IN REPO (EPUB sidecar, no pagination; pins by chapter and file marker); its assessment places it in Ch12 and Ch1, not here — it is noted inside this entry only for the lineage fact it carries]*.
Adjacent-downstream of the neomedieval dossier: the structural corrective the Slaughter line required, stated in the field's citation of record. Against the liberal reading of globalisation as flat and pacifying, "Asymmetric network structures create the potential for 'weaponized interdependence,' in which some states are able to leverage interdependent relations to coerce others" (p. 45 / PDF 4; confirmed); "Focal points of cooperation have become sites of control" (p. 47 / PDF 6; confirmed). Two effects are named and are recommended for adoption by name in Ch12: the "panopticon effect" and the "chokepoint effect" (pp. 55–56 / PDF 14–15; confirmed) — the book's legibility stack and off-switch respectively. What they concede to Bull: nothing by name (Bull and "medieval" absent from the article); their lineage runs through Keohane and Nye, whose predictions they reverse — "key global economic networks have converged toward 'hub and spoke' systems" (p. 49 / PDF 8; confirmed). Their scope condition is the book's mechanism in modern IR: "States that have jurisdictional control over network hubs and enjoy sufficient institutional capacity will be able to deploy both panopticon and chokepoint effects" (p. 58 / PDF 17; confirmed); and their cast is the book's — "only the United States and a couple of other key states and statelike entities (most notably, the European Union [EU] and, increasingly, China)" (p. 57 / PDF 16; confirmed). What they deny the book (b, at full strength): the networks "were not constructed as tools of statecraft" (p. 54 / PDF 13; confirmed) — the state inherited this power by jurisdictional accident, without new taxation; and their coercion is horizontal (state against state through a compliant hub), never vertical (the contest for the switch's ownership), which is the book's question. The book's answer (c): the levers were free but the hand was not (OFAC, the built apparatus); and the fiscal threshold is defended on the military stack, not on the coercion channels — a concession to be made openly. The 2023 book adds the lineage fact this appendix wants: its arc opens from Walter Wriston's *The Twilight of Sovereignty* (date to verify on retrieval — Farrell and Newman say only that he wrote it "just a few years earlier" than 1996), "nearly forgotten today," in which "The sovereign power of states, which had grown since the waning of the Middle Ages, was now in decline" (ch. 1, file `chapter1.xhtml`, "waning of the Middle Ages"; confirmed) — the business-side twin of the neomedieval diagnosis, to be named in Ch1 §V beside Slaughter; Wriston himself is not in corpus. — Engaged in **Ch12** §IV (by name; the vertical/horizontal disanalogy stated), §§I, III (footnotes), **Ch10** §VIII (paired with Bratton, attributing "chokepoint"), **Ch1** §V (footnote: the Slaughter verdict corroborated).

### VI. The critics — addition

**Stephen D. Krasner, *Sovereignty: Organized Hypocrisy* (Princeton UP, 1999).** *[T2 — IN REPO (sidecar `Krasner.txt`, printed p. N = PDF p. N+10)]*
The realist answer the retrieval ledger anticipated, entered with the qualification the ledger lacked: **Krasner never addresses the neomedieval hypothesis.** The sidecar was searched — no "medievalism," "new medieval" or "neomedieval"; "medieval" occurs five times, none of them about Bull's hypothesis; Bull appears as the English school's exponent of sovereignty as constitutive norm — "Hedley Bull, the best-known exponent of the English school, writes: 'From the perspective of any particular state what it chiefly hopes to gain from participation in the society of states is recognition of its independence of outside authority…'" (p. 47 / PDF 57; confirmed; continued p. 48 / PDF 58 and p. 51 / PDF 61, with a bare citation of Bull 1977 at p. 3 n. / PDF 13, which the assessment did not list). His four sovereignties — "domestic sovereignty, referring to the organization of public authority within a state and to the level of effective control exercised by those holding authority; interdependence sovereignty, referring to the ability of public authorities to control transborder movements; international legal sovereignty, referring to the mutual recognition of states or other entities; and Westphalian sovereignty, referring to the exclusion of external actors from domestic authority configurations" (p. 9 / PDF 19; confirmed) — and his thesis: the principles of the two authority-sovereignties "have always been violated … Westphalian and international legal sovereignty are best understood as examples of organized hypocrisy" (p. 24 / PDF 34; confirmed); "There has never been some ideal time during which all, or even most, political entities conformed with all of the characteristics that have been associated with sovereignty" (p. 238 / PDF 248; confirmed). What he concedes to Bull: that an international society exists and that its norms matter as scripts — "a well-understood cognitive script, one that is sometimes honored and sometimes not" (p. 69 / PDF 79; confirmed). What he denies: that the norms constitute the system, and, against the globalisation literature, that anything is new — "these challenges are not new. Rulers have always operated in a transnational environment" (p. 223 / PDF 233; confirmed). Placed under "the critics" because his position, if read straight, dissolves the neomedievalists' diagnosis into a change in what analysts notice; the book's answer (c) is that his violated sovereignty (external actors excluded) and the book's locked one (internal intermediaries subordinated under a fiscal monopoly of decisive force) occupy different cells of his own grid — he brackets the internal consolidation ("I offer no explanation for the displacement of other institutional forms, such as the Holy Roman Empire…", p. 5 / PDF 15; confirmed) and then concedes it with a functional cause ("functional advantages enjoyed by states over other structures such as feudalism, the Catholic Church, or city-states," p. 68 / PDF 78; confirmed). Two things he gives the book: the authority test for Ch2 — private actors like Amnesty and the Catholic Church "are not violating Westphalian autonomy; they make no claim to authoritative decision making" (p. 119 / PDF 129; confirmed), so the platforms breach only if they claim authority; and "invitation" as the name for spine §8(g)'s ornamental route — "more powerful states are unlikely to lose their Westphalian sovereignty unless they invite external authority through conventions or contractual arrangements" (p. 29 / PDF 39; confirmed). — Engaged in **Ch1** §VI (by name, beside Osiander and Teschke; the "exclusive" sentences re-cut) and §III (footnote), **Ch2** (the p. 119 test), Ch4 §V, Ch5 §I, Ch10, **Ch12** §§II, IV (invitation v. intervention, with §8(g)), Coda §VI, Appendix C (the American sub-wager: absorption by revocation against absorption by contract).

### VII. The historiographical anchor the literature lacked — addition

**William H. McNeill, *The Pursuit of Power: Technology, Armed Force, and Society since A.D. 1000* (University of Chicago Press, 1982).** *[T2 — IN REPO (sidecar `McNeill.txt`, printed p. N = PDF p. N+9)]*
Entered beside Roberts and Parker as the world-historical statement of the affordability effect and of the return from market to command that Part III predicts — an anchor the neomedieval literature did not use and which is not itself neomedievalist. His hypothesis is Chinese in origin — market-regulated behaviour around the year 1000 "tipped a critical balance in world history" (p. 25 / PDF 34; confirmed) — and Europe's market in violence is, on his reading, "an eccentric departure from the human norm of command behavior—the sort of behavior that dominated ancient times and has reasserted itself with remarkable power since the 1880s" (p. 116 / PDF 125; confirmed); his coinage is the "military-commercial complex" (p. 117 / PDF 126; confirmed); his retrospect from centuries hence: "Political management, having monopolized the overt organization of armed force, resumed its primacy over human behavior" (p. 386 / PDF 395; confirmed). The affordability claim in his words: "Wherever the new artillery appeared, existing fortifications became useless. The power of any ruler who was able to afford the high cost of the new weapons was therefore enhanced at the expense of neighbors and subjects … the major effect of the new weaponry was to dwarf the Italian city-states and to reduce other small sovereignties to triviality" (p. 89 / PDF 98; confirmed — the sidecar reads "high cos't," an OCR slip). And, for the bloc geometry, the *trace italienne* as the reason consolidation ran inside polities and froze between them: "Only the wealthiest states and cities could afford the scores of cannon and the enormous labor of construction required by the trace italienne" (p. 90 / PDF 99; confirmed); "Imperial consolidation halted halfway" (p. 91 / PDF 100; confirmed).
