## I.

On 25 March 1454, a deputation of English lords rode to Windsor to
speak with their king, and afterwards did something without precedent:
they put his silence on the record.

The Parliament Roll preserves their report. They had pressed Henry VI
"by all the waies and meanes that they cowede thynke" to obtain some
instruction, some preference, some sign — "but they cowede gete noo
answere ne signe" — and this fact, the fact of nothing, was formally
entered "of record" (Rot. Parl. v). Constitutions do not usually
document their own voids; the entry exists because the void had to be
proved before anything could be built over it. Two days later the
lords elected Richard of York "Protectorem & Defensorem… quamdiu Regi
placeret" — for as long as it should please the king — an office whose
very name had been chosen, as this chapter will show, for what it did
*not* claim. The reader knows the political story from Chapter 5: the
protectorate improvised, resented, dissolved at the king's recovery;
St Albans five months later. This chapter is about the constitutional
theory that made the improvisation so difficult — the most
sophisticated legal fiction of the age, and the load under which it
broke. Its subject, stated at the outset because the rest of Part II
has been preparing it, is the distance that opened in these years
between what the constitution appeared to be and what it could
effectively do — between a crown whose authority the lords would not
touch and a government that had to be carried on around it — and the
two occasions on which England measured that distance within a single
reign: a minority, which was designed and which held, if not without a
crisis that nearly broke it, for fifteen years, and an incapacity, for
which no office could be designed at all.

Chapter 8 ended by observing that the machinery of the coming
judicial monopoly was operated in the name of a person — anointed,
mortal, occasionally a child, occasionally mad. The age's own lawyers
had an answer to that mortality, and it is one of the most remarkable
constructions in the history of political thought. The king, they
held, has two bodies. Plowden's report of the Tudor cases states the
doctrine in its maturity: the body natural, subject to "the
Imbecility of Infancy or old Age and other natural Defects"; and "his
Body politic is a Body that cannot be seen or handled… utterly void
of Infancy and old Age and other natural Defects" — so that "what the
King does… cannot be invalidated or frustrated by any Disability in
his natural Body" (Plowden, *Commentaries*, 212a–213; the marginal
gloss: "The King is always adjudged in Law of full Age"). Blackstone
would carry the maxim into the classical constitution: the king never
dies. It is elegant, it is functional, and it contains a single
unexamined premise on which everything in this chapter turns: the
doctrine presumes that the natural body *executes*. (The modern
vocabulary for the same split is Krasner's, and it is worth having in
view from the start: "Domestic sovereignty involves both authority and
control, both the specification of legitimate authority within a polity
and the extent to which that authority can be effectively exercised"
(Krasner 1999: 4), a distinction he states again as two questions —
"what authority structures are recognized within a state, and how
effective is their level of control?" (Krasner 1999: 10). The
fifteenth-century problem is what becomes of a constitution in which
both are vested in one body.) The body politic is void of defects precisely because the body natural does the work
and the fiction absorbs the flaws. Where the work outgrows the
worker — where the demands on the natural body exceed what any
natural body can supply — the fiction faces a choice it was not
designed to survive: break, or be re-founded on something other than
a person. The fifteenth century broke it. The sixteenth re-founded
it. And the twenty-first, this chapter will argue in its second
half, is running the same experiment on new continental
institutions whose constitutions are, by charter, personal. A
constitution that lives in a body can die by one.

## II.

The doctrine's genealogy matters because it shows the fiction was
built for exactly the problem that defeated it.

Kantorowicz traced the arc — from the Norman Anonymous's *gemina
persona*, the twinned person of the anointed king, around 1100;
through the thirteenth-century juristic turn, when the theologians'
*corpus mysticum* migrated from the sacramental church to the body
politic; through Bracton's lawyer-king; to the Tudor maturity
Plowden reports [GAP: Kantorowicz's chapter-level argument awaits
the book, on the acquisition list; the arc is carried from the open
scholarly apparatus]. The construction's purpose was continuity:
the medieval polity needed the crown's authority to survive
minorities, dotages and the grave, and the two-bodies fiction — 
like the parallel maxim that dignity does not die — supplied a
corporate immortality centuries before the business corporation
generalised the trick. Maitland, the doctrine's great sceptic, is
kept in view throughout this chapter as the useful acid: the two
bodies were "metaphysiological nonsense," he wrote; it was "an
idle jest to say that the king never dies" when the demise of the
crown in fact stopped "all the wheels of state" — parliament
dissolved, offices vacated, the peace itself in abeyance until the
successor's accession (Maitland 1901, open). Maitland's point is
not that the fiction was silly but that it was *strained* — a
patch over a personal constitution's structural exposure — and
strained fictions are diagnostic instruments: where they tear
shows where the load ran.

## III.

The tear of 1453–54 is documented at a level of constitutional
self-consciousness that repays close reading, because the lords of
England, forced to govern around an insensible king, reasoned
their way along the two-bodies doctrine's exact edge — and their
drafting shows it.

The first thing the record shows is how long they refused to
reason at all. The king collapsed in the summer of 1453; "even in
these tragic circumstances there was a delay of eight months before
York was made protector. The court refused to admit that the king
was ill" (Lander 1976: 75). Parliament's November session was
explained away by the plague at Reading and prorogued; by January
the queen had a bill of five articles drawn, the first of which was
"that she desireth to have the hole reule of this land" (Griffiths
1981: 722); in February parliament fined absent peers "for the
first and only time in medieval England" in order to get a quorum
of the realm into one room (Griffiths 1981: 724); and the five
physicians attending the king were given formal authority for their
"experiments" under the signatures of twenty-eight lords, so that
even the medicine had to be constitutionally warranted (Griffiths
1981: 717). None of this is delay for want of candidates. York was
willing; the queen was willing; the delay is the measure of an
office that could not be filled, because the office was the person,
and the person was present, and alive, and — in the doctrine's own
terms — of full age and void of defect.

When they did act, they could not simply replace the king's will,
because the doctrine located authority in him and the fiction made
his authority perpetual; any instrument that *transferred* the
exercise of kingship would concede that kingship was transferable
— a concession fatal to the constitution and dangerous to every
magnate who might later face a recovered king. So they built,
with visible care, a non-substitute. York's own protestation
located the emergency's authority in the peerage only "by
th'occasion of th'enfirmite of oure said Souveraine Lord," in
whom, he affirmed, "resteth th'exercice of his auctoritee" — the
exercise itself never moving. And the office they gave him was
defined by negation, in the roll's extraordinary sentence: the
lords devised "nought the name of Tutour, Lieutenaunt, Governour,
nor of Regent, nor noo name that shall emporte auctorite of
governaunce of the lande; but the seid name of Protectour and
Defensour, the whiche emporteth a personell duete of entendaunce
to the actuell defence of this land… duryng the Kynges pleaser"
(Rot. Parl. v. 242; printed in full at Lander 1976: 76). Not
tutor, not lieutenant, not governor, not regent — four rejected
titles, each rejected because it implied governance; and the
accepted title imported only a personal duty of attendance to
defence, revocable at the pleasure of a king who could not speak.
The protectorate did not fail as an institution. It was *designed*
not to be one — a scaffolding built around the throne on the
strict condition that it touch nothing — and the design was the
two-bodies doctrine operating as a constraint on constitutional
invention.

It was, accordingly, improvised and dissolved. The king recovered
at Christmas 1454 (Griffiths 1981: 717) and the office lapsed with
the pleasure that had sustained it; the second protectorate, of
October 1455 to February 1456, was granted on the same limited
terms, with dismissal now by the king in parliament and the infant
Prince of Wales's rights expressly safeguarded (Lander 1960) — and
here the chapter must name a disagreement rather than smooth it.
Lander's reading is that the second protectorate answered no second
illness: the king "suffered no second breakdown during the later
part of 1455 comparable to his complete mental and physical
collapse of 1453–5" (Lander 1976: 90), and the episode was "an
attempted palace revolution which failed miserably for lack of
support" (Lander 1976: 99) — a magnate working the device the
incapacity had created. If he is right, the incapacity produced not
only an office that could not be filled but a form that could be
borrowed; and the chapter's claim survives either reading, since a
device that can be worked by a faction when the king is well is the
same device that could not govern when he was not. Watts's verified
formulations name what the same political nation had already been
doing for a generation: the minority council's "artificial king,"
the conciliar manufacture of an "artificial royal will in periods
when the king could exercise no will in person" (Watts, ch. 5,
p. 127). England could simulate the royal will when custom licensed
it — for a child — and could not lawfully institutionalise the
simulation for a broken adult. The fiction permitted a fiction, and
forbade a solution.

The distinction the lords drew without naming it has a name in the
English constitutional tradition, and the name arrives four centuries
late, from an author who took care to say that he was not describing
this period at all. Walter Bagehot divided the constitution into two
sets of parts: "those which excite and preserve the reverence of the
population—the DIGNIFIED parts, if I may so call them; and next, the
EFFICIENT parts—those by which it, in fact, works and rules," because
"every constitution must first GAIN authority, and then USE authority"
(Bagehot 1867, "The Cabinet," PDF p. 21). His caveat travels with every
use made of him here — "I am not, of course, now speaking of the middle
ages: I am not dealing with the embryo or the infant form of our
Constitution; I am only speaking of its adult form" (Bagehot 1867, "The
House of Lords," PDF p. 48) — and the claim entered on the Roll's
evidence is correspondingly modest: not that his adult form existed in
1454, but that the anatomy he described in its maturity can be seen, in
its involuntary first version, in the drafting. At Windsor sat a part
that could gain authority and could not use it, whose "auctoritee"
York's protestation was careful to leave where it lay; at Westminster
the lords assembled a part that could use nothing except by borrowing
an authority it dared not claim, and borrowed it therefore under a
title chosen to deny the borrowing — so that what Watts calls the
manufacture of an artificial royal will is the efficient part being
assembled while the dignified part sat insensible at Windsor. The
genealogy is his own, and he draws it from
this chapter's case: reverence and capacity are "transmitted according
to" different laws, and "very soon that line comes to a child or an
idiot, or one by some defect or other incapable. Then … the grave
council begins not so much to suggest as to inculcate, not so much to
advise as to enjoin" (Bagehot 1867, "Conclusion," PDF p. 103). A child,
an idiot, one by some defect incapable: 1422, 1453, 1392 — his three
terms are this chapter's three cases, the word for the second of them
quoted as his and not adopted, and he knew where in English history
they lay, observing of the ante-Tudor period that "some monarchs were
imbecile" and that the mediaeval Parliament was not, like his own, "a
ruling body" but "an EXPRESSIVE body" (Bagehot 1867, "Conclusion," PDF
p. 104). What must travel with the borrowing is a disanalogy he himself
supplies, because the error it guards against is an easy one: the
dignified parts were never decoration. He calls them decoration in one
place — "retained, not for intrinsic use, but from their imaginative
attraction upon an uncultured and rude population," like "the
additional and solely-ornamental wheels introduced into the clocks of
the Middle Ages," so that "all such ornamental work is a source of
friction and error" (Bagehot 1867, "On Changes of Ministry," PDF p.
83) — and the condition of everything in another: they "are the
preliminaries, the needful prerequisites of ALL work. They raise the
army, though they do not win the battle" (PDF p. 21); and of the
monarchy of his own day, "the use of the Queen, in a dignified
capacity, is incalculable. Without her in England, the present English
Government would fail and pass away" (Bagehot 1867, "The Monarchy I,"
PDF p. 30). The two passages are one doctrine and not a contradiction —
ornamental as administration, indispensable as authority — and the
fifteenth century had found it out the hard way, because the lords
could not do without the king they could not use: the insensible body
at Windsor was the only thing in the kingdom that could raise the army
York was appointed to lead. In the personal monarchy proper the two
parts had been one body; 1422 to 1455 is the involuntary separation
from which the designed one descends; and the negative drafting of
March 1454 refused to make that separation lawful. What this book takes
from Bagehot is the distinction. What it declines is his valuation, and
the refusal has to be visible, because for him the separation of
reverence from rule is not a condition to be measured but the merit of
every working constitution and the glory of England's: supreme power
resides "not necessarily or commonly in the whole people … but in a
CHOSEN people, a picked and selected people" (Bagehot 1867, "The
Cabinet," PDF p. 28), and "the apparent rulers of the English nation
are like the most imposing personages of a splendid procession … The
real rulers are secreted in second-rate carriages" (Bagehot 1867,
"Prerequisites," PDF p. 101). This book treats the distance between the
two sets of parts as a quantity rather than a virtue — it can widen or
close, it can be hidden or shown, and it can be measured, which is the
use Part III makes of it — and it notes here, without pursuing it, that
the same distinction has since been turned on the elected institutions
themselves, by Colin Crouch, writing against Bagehot's complacency
rather than from it: "it is difficult to dignify it as democracy
itself" (Crouch 2000, PDF p. 8). That belongs to the settlement, and the
settlement is Part III's subject. What this chapter has to establish is
narrower: that a constitution can carry, for a term, a dignified part
with all the authority and an efficient part with all the work, that
the fifteenth century did so twice, and that only the occasion on which
the separation had a known end proved survivable — which is the
business of the section that follows.

## IV.

The minority is the control, and the chapter must set it out with
the same care, because the distinction on which the argument now
rests — between an office that was designed and an office that
could not be filled — is only as good as the evidence that the
first of them worked.

The design is on the record from the first weeks. Henry V died at
the end of August 1422; in the parliament that followed, after "a
long discussion among the lords and careful investigation of law
and precedent," Gloucester's demand for the powers of a regent "was
rejected on 5 December: claims based on his birth, the will, and on
precedents quoted by him were found wanting." He was given instead
the title of "Defensor of this Reme and chief counseiller of the
kyng," which — as the lords explained when the point was reopened
in 1428 — implied no "name of Tutour, Lieutinent, Governour, nor of
Regent, nor no name that shuld emporte auctorite of governaunce of
the lond," and which was to last not until the king came of age but
only "as long as it liked the Kyng" (Griffiths 1981: 22). The
reader will recognise the sentence: the negative formula of March
1454 was not improvised at Windsor. It was the minority's formula,
thirty years old, taken down and re-used, word for word in its
rejected titles. What differed was everything around it. In 1422
the words defined an office whose term was known and whose
authority the design could actually place elsewhere, in a council
"never less than twelve in number" and rising on occasion to
eighteen, of which the protector was chief councillor and probably
president, and whose three principal officers of state carried the
administration (Griffiths 1981: 32); the child had no will to be
displaced, and would acquire one on a date the realm could foresee.
In 1454 the same words were wrapped around a king who had a will in
law, none in fact, and might recover either at any hour. That the
one formula produced two such different offices is this book's
observation on the record, not Griffiths's; it is marked as such.

And the designed office worked, in the qualified sense the record will
bear — with the hedge stated here rather than left for a reader to
supply, and stated against the strongest statement of the contrary
view. Tilly, whose
account of state-making this book has treated as its ancestor from
Chapter 3 onward, holds as a rule that armed lords were at once the
king's necessary auxiliaries and his rivals, and "for that reason,
before the seventeenth century, regencies for child sovereigns
reliably produced civil wars" (Tilly 1985, "Violence and
Government," para. 4). The minority of 1422–37 is the
counter-instance, and the chapter can only claim it by saying so.
It was not serene. Beaufort's appointment as chancellor in July
1424, in Griffiths's account, "seriously endangered the stability of the
council and led to a major political explosion when Gloucester
returned from the Low Countries in April 1425" — "the most severe
domestic crisis of the protectorate," and "the most serious domestic
threat to the stability of Henry VI's government" in the whole
minority (Griffiths 1981: 36, 73). But it was contained: the
collision was resolved by "drastic changes within the council" at
the Leicester parliament of 1426, not by battle (Griffiths 1981:
36); the councillors who had served Henry V supplied "that crucial
element of continuity and stability which made the transition to a
minority régime a peaceful one" (Griffiths 1981: 34); the
protectoral title lapsed of itself "once Henry VI had taken his
coronation oath on 6 November" 1429, and the regulations of
February 1430 were "primarily a reiteration of the principles
underpinning the government since 1422" (Griffiths 1981: 38). The
minority ended as it had been designed to end, by the king's
majority — the great council summoned to Sheen for 21 October 1437
(Griffiths 1981: 238), from which the young king emerged, in
Griffiths's careful phrase, "vulnerable to the exhortations and
solicitations of those about him" (Griffiths 1981: 240) — a
vulnerability that belongs to the next crisis, not to this one.
Fifteen years, one explosion contained by parliament, no civil war:
against Tilly's rule, that is the exception, and it is claimed as one
and claimed narrowly — a single run, in one kingdom, which complicates
a generalisation drawn from the whole of Europe before the seventeenth
century without overturning it. Why England escaped the rule is a further question, and the
chapter's answer is an inference marked as its own: the minority
had what the incapacity lacked, a settled succession and a certain
horizon, and it had what Tilly's regencies lacked, a war abroad
that absorbed the armed lords under Bedford's regency of France
rather than leaving them at home to contest the council.

The distinction can now be stated flatly. The minority was an
office that could be designed because the person whose authority it
exercised had, as yet, no will to be usurped and a known date on
which he would acquire one; authority and its exercise could be
separated by agreement, for a term, and reunited on schedule. The
incapacity was an office that could not be filled because the will
existed in law, was absent in fact, and might return without
notice; authority and its exercise had been separated by nature,
without a term, and the constitution had no instrument for saying
so — which is why the lords proved the void "of record" before they
dared to build over it, and why what they built was designed to
touch nothing. The same constitutional words, applied to the same
king, produced a government that ran for fifteen years and a
government that lasted nine months and was, on one serious reading,
borrowed by a faction the moment the king was well. Tilly's rule is
met on its own terms: the minority did not produce the civil war;
the incapacity did — and even that sentence, Lander's dissent
reminds us, must be stated with the hedge that the incapacity
produced the occasion and the magnates supplied the war.

## V.

France, characteristically, tried to legislate the solution in
advance — and the deeper case shows the deeper failure. Charles
VI's government, after the first attacks, wrote the substitute
into ordinance: January 1393, the queen named co-guardian of the
dauphin hedged with a council of twelve; April 1403, government
during the king's "absences" — the era's exquisite word for his
mind's departures — vested in majority rule in council (the
ordinances verified via the open scholarly apparatus, footnoted
to the *Ordonnances des rois de France*) [GAP: Famiglietti and
Autrand for the court detail]. On paper, France possessed what
England refused to draft: a standing constitutional provision
for an executive without the king. In practice, intermittency
defeated law. Every lucid interval re-personalised the
constitution — the returning king could undo, re-grant,
re-favour — and every relapse re-opened the contest among the
princes whom the ordinances had made colleagues; the council
built to absorb the king's absences became the arena in which
Orléans and Burgundy fought for his person, and Chapter 5's
civil war followed. The two designs — England's deliberate
non-institution, France's legislated institution — failed in
opposite ways, and the pairing is the chapter's evidence that
the problem was structural: under a personal constitution,
neither refusing nor drafting the substitute could stabilise
it, because the constitution's own premise — authority lives in
the person — re-asserted itself at every recovery, every
relapse, every death.

## VI.

The century's full experimental record confirms the pattern in
its form and, read with the previous sections in mind, denies it
in its results. In 1422, the negative title, the capped
patronage, the quorate council, commissions running "as long as
it liked the Kyng." In 1454, the negative definition. In 1455–56,
the same limited terms, dismissal now by the king in parliament,
the infant Prince of Wales's rights expressly safeguarded. Across
the Channel, the Treaty of Troyes vested the regency of France in
Henry V while elaborately preserving each kingdom's separate laws
— the dual monarchy as a personal union precisely *not* an
institutional merger. Limited, revocable, council-bound, and
protective of the personal constitution each was built to
relieve: the runs of the experiment the record preserves — and
there were several in each kingdom — returned the same *form*.
They did not return the same result, and the difference between
the results is not a matter of how many runs there were. A
minority was an office temporarily filled by a council; an
incapacity was an office that could not be filled at all; and the
two failed differently rather than identically. That distinction,
and not a tally, is what the argument rests on, because Tilly's
rule is stated for the whole of Europe before the seventeenth
century and no count taken in two kingdoms could meet it (Tilly
1985, "Violence and Government," para. 4). The minority's
run held for fifteen years and ended on schedule; the incapacity's
runs lasted months and ended at the king's pleasure or, on
Lander's reading, at the failure of a manoeuvre; the French runs
were overturned at every lucid interval. The form was the
constant. The variable was whether the person at the centre of
the form had a will, and when it would return — and it is that
variable, not the form, that the rest of this chapter follows.
(The framing of that record — one form, repeatedly adopted,
succeeding only where the personal constitution's premise was
suspended by nature for a known term — is this book's reading of
an open fact-pattern, and is marked as the book's own.)

## VII.

What finally relieved the broken fiction — and made the separation
of 1454 lawful, then ordinary, then invisible — was not a better
regency ordinance, and the chapter must now engage the debate
about what it was.

Elton's thesis, in its strong form, gave the answer a decade and
an author: in the 1530s, under Thomas Cromwell, England underwent
a revolution in government — household administration giving way
to bureaucratic departments, a privy council, a "chief national
executive"; the state, in the phrase Collinson's memoir
preserves, "took leave of the Middle Ages," and "Cromwell, not
Henry… really the government." The strong thesis was attacked at
birth (Wernham's review, 1956), besieged across the great *Past
& Present* exchange of 1963–65 (Harriss and Williams pressing
medieval continuities, Cooper the seventeenth century, Elton
replying twice), and revised from inside by Elton's own pupils —
Starkey's privy chamber restoring the personal court to the
centre, the *Revolution Reassessed* essays distributing the
change across two centuries — until Elton himself conceded, in
1991, that he may have "overstated [Cromwell's] systematic
approach" [GAP: the debate's texts are gated; the citation
chain is verified, the wording carried from the open memoir].
This book requires none of the strong version. It requires what
no party to the debate contests — call it the weak thesis, a
label that is the book's own: across the later fifteenth and
sixteenth centuries, in England as on the continent, the
personal constitution gave way under fiscal-military pressure
to an institutional one — offices that did not die, records
that did not forget, salaried officials whose authority ran
from function rather than proximity. Whether the transition
was Cromwellian and sudden or distributed and slow is a
quarrel over tempo and agency inside an agreed direction —
the same shape, the reader will notice, as the
military-revolution debate Chapter 6 staged, and it is
resolved here the same way: the book takes the direction,
which is conceded, and leaves the ranking to the specialists.
The two-bodies fiction was not repealed; it was relieved. The
political body acquired, at last, an executive that was not a
body at all — and with it the beginning of the arrangement
Bagehot would describe in its adult form, in which the part that
gains authority and the part that uses it are separated not by
the accident of a king's health but by design.

## VIII.

Five centuries after the relief, the personal constitution has
returned — not in the residual monarchies, which are not the weak
case but the demonstration, the one surviving form in which a
separated dignified part can be watched doing its work, since on
Bagehot's account the half that does not command still raises the
army the half that commands employs; it has returned in the
charters of the new continental institutions, where the two
bodies are one again, and load-bearing, and growing.

The documentation is the institutions' own. Meta's proxy statement
discloses that its founder holds 99.8 per cent of the Class B
shares and 60.8 per cent of the company's total voting power, and
that Meta is, in the exchange's formal category, a "controlled
company" (Meta DEF 14A, 2026). Alphabet's founders jointly hold
some 52.7 per cent of its votes through the same dual-class
mechanism (Alphabet DEF 14A, 2026). Nor are these legacy
anomalies: by Ritter's count, 48.4 per cent of American
technology flotations in 2025 carried dual-class structures,
against essentially none in 1980 — the personal constitution is
the *default charter* of the new institutional generation. What
the fifteenth century achieved by anointing, the twenty-first
achieves by share class: authority vested in a named natural
body, formally insulated from the institution's other members,
and — the two-bodies problem returning on schedule — subject to
every natural defect the body politic was invented to transcend.
The extreme case concentrates the point. One individual
simultaneously heads six ventures spanning launch, orbital
communications, vehicles, tunnelling, neural interfaces and
frontier AI (Tesla beneficial ownership 19.8 per cent per the
2025 proxy; his reported control of SpaceX appears in no public
primary document and is carried here as reported only). The
episode that displays the constitutional stakes is told in its
corrected form, fully attributed, because the popular version is
false in a direction that matters: in 2022, asked by Ukraine to
enable Starlink coverage for a drone attack on the Crimean
anchorage, Musk *declined to enable* it — the coverage was never
on, and no switch was thrown — citing escalation risk (Isaacson's
revised account and Musk's own statement, in agreement on this).
Told correctly, the episode is still remarkable: a war-relevant
decision of state consequence, taken by a natural body
constrained by no office, under no command authority, on his own
strategic judgement. And the state's response arrived on
schedule and is the more instructive half: dedicated defence
contracting for the constellation, and Starshield — "owned and
controlled by the US government," in the operative description —
the improvised institutional substitute, built because the
personal constitution above a critical capability had become
intolerable to the political body that depended on it.

The pattern is not confined to the platforms, and the chapter
states its public-sector face with the same primary discipline.
The presidential systems are personalising: the American
Presidency Project's tables show the current administration
issuing executive orders at 179 a year, the highest rate since
Franklin Roosevelt. The party-state has personalised furthest:
the 2018 removal of China's presidential term limits (2,958
votes for, two against), and the concentration of policy
coordination in leading small groups chaired by the leader —
six of eleven at the count in the open literature —
"informal, off-the-books mechanisms" of personal rule, several
of which were then upgraded into formal Commissions: the
routinisation datum, personal authority being re-clothed in
institutional dress even as it concentrates (Miller, Hoover).
(Cheung's account of the same apparatus states the two-body
exposure in terms this chapter recognises: the national security
commission is "a personalistic symbol of Xi's command of the NSS"
(Cheung 2022: 67); authority is "hyperconcentrated under Xi's
authority," held simultaneously in the chairmanships of three
commissions (Cheung 2022: 284); and the deputies "lack the
political qualifications to take over in anything more than a
short-term acting capacity," an overconcentration he calls "the
gravest risk" to the system's prospects (Cheung 2022: 285–86).
The judgement is his, and it is a judgement about an office.)
And the American experiment in personalised authority *inside*
the state ran its full arc within a single year, and is
recorded here as the dated episode it is: a department of
government efficiency created by executive order in January
2025, animated by the same extreme case above, exited by its
animating person that May, and disbanded by November (OPM) —
created by order, dissolved by interview, its claimed savings
contested. It had a programme as well as an animator, which is
worth recording because it makes the episode a design rather
than a whim: on Jill Lepore's account the department "drew its
inspiration from RAGE—Retire All Government Employees—by the
neo-monarchist Curtis Yarvin," whose "aim was to replace humans
with bots" (Lepore, printed p. 112 / PDF p. 126; the pin is
provisional and the characterisation of Yarvin is hers). Across systems — proxy statement, executive order,
party constitution — the signature is identical: authority
migrating from office to person, then, under load, being
hastily re-clothed in improvised institutional dress. The
fifteenth century would recognise the wardrobe.

## IX.

Whether the improvisations mature is the pending question, and
the honest answer is that the record cuts both ways — which is
what a transition looks like from inside.

The substitutes are institutionalising, unevenly, and the
unevenness is itself instructive: where a legislature or a
regulator has done the institutionalising, the instrument exists
and binds; where the institution was announced by the body it was
to constrain, some of it was never built at all. Trust-and-safety
has professionalised into a discipline with a standards body and now
an ISO standard; the European legislator has written the
counterweight into law — the Digital Services Act requires the
largest platforms to establish "a compliance function,
independent from their operational functions," whose head "shall
not be removed without prior approval of the management body"
(DSA, Art. 41): statute compelling an internal check on founder
discretion, the conciliar ordinance of 1403 re-enacted as
European regulation. The AI governance layer is acquiring
offices — the UK's institute, its American counterpart, the EU
AI Office with its growing staff — and one instrument among the
frontier labs is a matter of corporate law rather than
announcement: Anthropic's Long-Term Benefit Trust, a class of
stock held by trustees who will elect a board majority, a device
for placing the institution's long-run interest beyond any single
natural body (primary announcement). The constitutional
announcements made alongside such instruments are a different
matter, and nothing here rests on them. Anthropic's own proposal
that a constitution for powerful AI systems "could be a democratic
process wherein diverse stakeholders provide input" produced, on
Jill Lepore's account, neither of the bodies it named — "No
promised world governance board was ever assembled … no
constitutional convention was ever held" — and the constitution
that was published "was written chiefly by a philosopher working
for Anthropic" (Lepore, printed p. 106 / PDF p. 120; pins
provisional). She is a pointer rather than the authority: the
negative belongs at the labs' own documents, and this chapter
counts an instrument whose constitutional character was announced
and not built as no evidence of institutionalisation at all
[PRIMARY OWED: the labs' published governance documents against
the announcements]. Even the securities regulator has formalised
the two-body problem: since 2009, CEO
succession is a governance matter shareholders may raise, not
"ordinary business" the board may keep to itself (SEC Staff
Legal Bulletin 14E).

And the substitutes still fail against the person. The test
case ran in November 2023, and this book reads it evenly
because it cuts both ways. OpenAI's nonprofit board — the
regency instrument, formally empowered — dismissed the founder
for being "not consistently candid in his communications." Five
days later he was back, and the board was gone, dissolved by
the coalition his person could still command: employees,
investors, the essential commercial partner. The substitute
existed, and acted, and could not hold — Charles VI's lucid
interval in corporate form, the returning person
re-personalising the constitution that had briefly operated
without him. (Bagehot's own prototype of the returning natural
body was George III, whose reign he treated as the standing
warning of constitutional royalty — "We have had the case of a
meddling maniac," and, of such a king's rule, "a secret power
which is always eager, which is generally obstinate, which is
often wrong … which is irresponsible because it is inscrutable,
which cannot be prevented because it cannot be seen" (Bagehot
1867, "The Monarchy II," PDF p. 46) — the same king of whom he
wrote that in the 1770s he "was the Government" (Bagehot 1867,
"The Monarchy II," PDF p. 37). The epithets are his, and they are
quoted as a Victorian judgement on an office, never as this
book's description of any person.) Institutionalisation of the new
political bodies is pending, not achieved; the two-bodies problem of the
platform age is live, and Chapter 12 weighs it where it bears
hardest — in the Chinese candidacy, whose settlement is being
executed as personal rule, and in the succession clauses of every
charter Part III reads.

There is, however, a second question that the fifteenth century
obliges this chapter to leave open about its own evidence, and
it is the one Bagehot's anatomy was brought in to sharpen. Each
of the substitutes just listed — the independent compliance
function, the trust, the succession rule, the board that acted —
is described here as an efficient part in the making: an
instrument that will, if it matures, use authority. But the
minority council of 1422 and the protectorate of 1454 were built
from the same materials, and only one of them ever used any. The
other gained authority for a body that sat elsewhere, and was
designed to. Which of the two the new instruments become — parts
that rule, or parts that are retained, in Bagehot's phrase, for
their "imaginative attraction" while the work is done in another
room — cannot be read from their charters, any more than the
Roll's negative sentence could be read for what the protectorate
would in fact be permitted to do; it can only be read from what
they are later found to have done, and this chapter has no such
record yet. It has instead the shape of a question that Part III
must state in full and Appendix C must undertake to score: what
happens to a constitution when its efficient part migrates — out
of the office, out of the assembly, into the stack and the
persons who hold it — and its dignified part stays, gaining an
authority it no longer uses, raising an army it no longer
commands. Two of Bagehot's own observations mark the ground on which
that question will be fought. The first is a concession his
theory makes and his confidence does not: a polity resting on
deference is in "unstable equilibrium," and once the equilibrium
is disturbed "there is no tendency to return to it" (Bagehot
1867, "Prerequisites," PDF p. 102), so that a separation which
has been demonstrated is, on his own account, a separation that
has begun to fail. The second is a condition the fifteenth
century did not meet and the present may: the ante-Tudor kings
could not have manufactured a compliant assembly even had they
wished to, because "the instrument in that behalf is the
centralised executive, and there was then no 'prefet' by whom the
opinion of a rural locality could be made to order" (Bagehot
1867, "Conclusion," PDF p. 104). The lords of 1454 were an
unsymmetrical reality because nobody yet possessed the means to
make them a symmetrical sham; whether the present possesses those
means is a question this chapter does not answer. Bagehot's own
answer for 1867 was the disguise — constitutional royalty "acts
as a DISGUISE. It enables our real rulers to change without
heedless people knowing it" (Bagehot 1867, "The Monarchy I," PDF
p. 36), and "it is needful to keep the ancient show while we
secretly interpolate the new reality" (Bagehot 1867,
"Conclusion," PDF p. 106) — kept deliberately and, he thought, to
England's credit. Whether the democratic bloc's answer is the same one, in whose favour the
disguise is kept, and whether the separation can be demonstrated
rather than merely suspected — which on Bagehot's own theory
would be the beginning of its end — are the questions Chapter 12
takes up under a name this chapter has deliberately not used.

The fifteenth century's resolution, when it came, did not come
from constitutional theory at all. No regency ordinance, no
protectoral drafting, no conciliar formula solved the broken
fiction — the solution arrived from outside the problem, in the
form of offices, records and salaried officials: the
bureaucratic state, which relieved the natural body by making
most of government proceed without reference to any body at
all. And what forced that invention was not philosophy but
pressure — the fiscal-military pressure of the gun and the
payroll, which demanded an executive that could not pause for
a king's health, his minority, or his grief, and which put, in
Tilly's words, "disarming the great" high "on the agenda of every
would-be state maker" (Tilly 1985, "Violence and Government,"
para. 4). The reader has already seen the machine at work; the
next chapter opens its account books.
