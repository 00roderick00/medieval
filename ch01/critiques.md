# Critiques — Ch. 1: The Return of the Middle Ages

Objection 1 is the outline's named stress test; Objections 2–5 were surfaced
during research. Each is steelmanned before the chapter answers it (§6). The
Perplexity second-opinion run on the draft is logged at the foot.

---

## Objection 1 — The medievalist's flattening complaint (the named objection)

**Steelman.** "Overlapping sovereignty c. 1200" is a slogan that flattens a
period of enormous variation. There was no single "medieval order": the
England of Henry II, with its precocious common law, itinerant justices and
Exchequer, was already a proto-state with a reach no thirteenth-century German
princeling approached; Capetian France was centralising; Norman Sicily was a
bureaucratic monarchy. To lump these with the fragmented Rhineland and
communal Italy under one "overlapping authority" heading is to select the most
fragmented cases and call them the norm — the very cherry-picking the book
accuses the neomedievalists of. Worse, the modern "overlapping jurisdiction"
being compared (platforms vs states) is a matter of *regulatory* competition
among functioning bureaucracies, not the *personal, parcelised* authority of
the medieval world; the analogy equivocates on "overlapping."

**The chapter's answer.** Concede the variation openly and narrow the claim, as
the outline directs: the baseline is not "the Middle Ages" but the specific,
unusually plural mid-thirteenth-century Rhineland and northern Italy —
precisely because those are the times and places the modern comparison
illuminates, and precisely *not* the centralising England or France that the
book's Part II will need as the *consolidators*. This is not cherry-picking but
case selection with its reasons stated: the chapter wants the dispersed pole of
a spectrum it will later show consolidating.

**Residual exposure — flagged, not hidden.** The northern-Italian half of this
defence is well sourced (communes; Guelph/Ghibelline; the 1250–73 Great
Interregnum). **The Rhineland half is currently under-sourced:** the claim that
the Rhineland was *exceptionally* fragmented specifically c. 1250 was not found
in open sources, which document the *early-modern* Kleinstaaterei instead
(sources.md RETRIEVAL #7). Until a regional monograph is retrieved, the draft
should either lean the defence on northern Italy or state the Rhineland claim
with an explicit citation once obtained — it must not assert "exceptionally
fragmented c. 1250" for the Rhineland on present evidence. On the "overlapping"
equivocation: the book answers it not here but by its whole method — it does
not rest on the *word* "overlapping" but on the *mechanism*; the resemblance is
scaffolding, the mechanism is the argument (CLAUDE.md §2). Any drafted passage
that leans on the resemblance rather than the mechanism is flagged
[ANALOGY-ONLY].

## Objection 2 — Cerny's checklist is a sieve that catches everything

**Steelman.** A six-point checklist loose enough to be "satisfied" by the
2020s was loose enough to be satisfied by the 1990s, the 1930s, or any period
of visible disorder. Multiple competing institutions, fragmented identities,
grey-zone economies, entrenched property — name a century without them. A
diagnostic that cannot fail is not a diagnostic; running it against the present
and finding it "confirmed" proves nothing.

**The chapter's answer.** The checklist is not offered as proof; it is the
*diagnostic* stage the book will supersede. Part I deliberately accepts the
neomedievalists' own instrument and shows the present satisfies it — because
the book's disagreement with them is not about the diagnosis (which it grants)
but about the *prognosis* (dispersal as terminal vs transitional). The
falsifiable content is not in the checklist; it is in the *mechanism* (Part II)
and the *indicators* (Appendix C), which can fail. The chapter should say this
plainly: the checklist earns its place by being the literature's, not the
book's, and by setting up the question the literature never answered.

## Objection 3 — Characteristic 2 refutes the whole frame

**Steelman.** Cerny's second characteristic — the *absence of exogenous
territorialising pressure* — is contradicted by the present. Since roughly
2017 the world has seen exactly such pressure: renewed great-power
competition, NATO's post-2022 consolidation, all members driving defence
spending up. If the defining neomedieval condition (no external threat forcing
consolidation) has lapsed, then by the literature's own criterion we are not in
a neomedieval world at all, and the chapter's "diagnosis confirmed" collapses.

**The chapter's answer.** This is the chapter's best turn, and it should lead
with it rather than defend against it. Cerny's own logic is that *without*
exogenous pressure there is no consolidation — the pressure is the trigger, not
a symptom. Its partial return is therefore not evidence against the book; it is
the leading edge of the book's *predicted re-consolidation*, the
re-territorialising force Part III argues is driving the Tudor turn. The
weakest-fitting neomedieval characteristic is weak in exactly the place, and at
exactly the time, the pendulum begins its return swing. The objection, pressed
hard, hands the book its Part III thesis a hundred pages early — so the draft
should let it, with a forward-pointer, not smother it. (Calibration: state this
as *consistent with* the thesis, not as proof of it; the indicators decide.)

## Objection 4 — Bull and Friedrichs are being quoted at second hand

**Steelman.** The chapter's two foundational authorities are not in the bundle,
and the memo concedes their primaries were unreachable; the formulations come
from a single secondary article (Baaz). To build the book's opening on
second-hand quotation of its two most important predecessors is exactly the
provenance failure the doctrine forbids — and if Baaz has paraphrased or
mis-paginated, the error propagates into the book's foundation.

**The chapter's answer.** The doctrine's rule is "never cite a gated work from
memory as if consulted" (§4) — and the chapter does not. It cites a specific,
open, peer-reviewed source (Baaz) that reproduces the passages *verbatim with
page numbers*, corroborated by a second open source (Gibbins) and by
independent search. That is legitimate scholarly practice (citing a reliable
intermediary), not fabrication. The exposure is real but bounded: the exact
heading order and Bull's verbatim caution wording are marked [UNVERIFIED —
RETRIEVAL] and will not be quoted until the primary is in hand. The draft must
not upgrade "quoted in Baaz" to a bare "Bull writes" until then. This is the
ch06 discipline (cite the open intermediary; retrieve the primary for the
load-bearing quote) applied to the humanities.

## Objection 5 — "The state as anomaly" overstates a contested case

**Steelman.** The chapter's closing move — that the sovereign state is a mere
contingent settlement that can dissolve — rests on the revisionist Westphalian-
myth literature (Osiander, Teschke), which is itself contested. Andrew Latham
and others argue medieval and modern sovereignty differ in degree, not kind;
if so, the state is not an "anomaly" poised to vanish but a durable
institutional form, and the book's whole "return swing" loses its pivot.

**The chapter's answer.** The book does not need the maximal claim (the state
as freak accident) and should not make it. It needs only the minimal,
uncontested one: the sovereign state is a *settlement produced by specific
pressures*, so that *if those pressures change, the settlement can change* —
which even Latham's "variation on a theme" concedes. The draft states both
sides (Osiander/Teschke vs Latham) and rests the argument on the weaker
version, exactly as the chapter's treatment of Cerny rests on the diagnosis it
can grant and the ch06 answer to Rogers rests on his own fiscal concession.
The book's strength throughout is that it wins on the weaker, harder-to-deny
version of each claim.

## Objection 6 — Krasner: sovereignty was organized hypocrisy from the start, so there was no lock to spring

**Steelman.** The chapter's arc is a two-state model: a plural order
"locked" (ruling (c)) into "the exclusive sovereignties of 1700," now
returning to plurality, with the lock kept as a real condition from which
the pendulum swings back. Krasner denies the second state ever existed as
practice. "Violations of Westphalian sovereignty have been almost routine
in international politics even though observers have been blinded to
their frequency by the assumption that the Westphalian model has been
operative" (Krasner 1999: 28). "The right, or ability, of the state
apparatus to exercise full authority within its own territorial borders
has never been consistently established in practice" (p. 51). "Perhaps
half of the countries of Europe have never enjoyed Westphalian sovereignty
for a single moment of their existence as international legal sovereigns"
(p. 29). If sovereignty was hypocritical from the start, what the
neomedievalists "diagnosed" is a change in what analysts notice, not in
the world — "these challenges are not new. Rulers have always operated in
a transnational environment" (p. 223) — and he extends the hypocrisy back
through the book's own period: rulers in "the European feudal system …
would like those in the sovereign state system, expound principles that
they would also violate" (pp. 227–28). On this reading there was no lock
to spring and nothing for a pendulum to leave. The objection reaches §VI's
ruling, spine §3, CLAUDE.md §1 claim 1 (dispersal as transitional), and
spine §7's Bull test, which Krasner holds malformed — "The international
system is not like the game of chess. It does not have constitutive rules"
(p. 229).

**The chapter's answer.** Krasner's own grid dissolves the objection,
because the sovereignty he shows to be hypocritical and the sovereignty
the chapter says was locked occupy different cells. His violated
Westphalian sovereignty is the exclusion of *external* actors — other
rulers, lenders, courts, institutions — from a polity's authority
structures. The book's lock is the subordination of *internal*
intermediate authorities — lords, cities, orders, the Church's writ inside
the realm — and the crown's monopoly of decisive force: domestic
sovereignty in Krasner's control sense, which he says "is not necessarily
related to international legal or Westphalian sovereignty" (p. 12). He
never claims the internal consolidation was hypocritical; he brackets it
— "I offer no explanation for the displacement of other institutional
forms, such as the Holy Roman Empire, the Chinese tributary system, or the
Hanseatic League" (p. 5) — and then concedes it as fact with a functional
cause: the state's dominance "was not a random event but reflected
functional advantages enjoyed by states over other structures such as
feudalism, the Catholic Church, or city-states" (p. 68). His own
regularity — strong states lose autonomy only by invitation (p. 29) —
means the consolidators were in practice what the book says they became.
And his globalization scepticism is dated: written before the platforms,
it treats transnational actors as flows or as advocates that "make no
claim to authoritative decision making" (p. 119); ch02's point is that the
new universalisms claim exactly that.

**Grade and residue.** Good against the mechanism and the "locked" ruling
— *provided the lock is restated as internal subordination and the fiscal
monopoly of decisive force, never as exclusivity*. Weak wherever the draft
says "exclusive" (§I once, §VI twice), because there Krasner is simply
right, and the register decision is proposed in the memo. A residue to
carry rather than answer: the consolidated state was, from 1648 onward,
routinely penetrated by invitation (pp. 20, 29), so Part III's absorption
of the stack may itself run by invitation — spine §8(g)'s ornamental
route, reached from the other side; that is ch12's and Appendix C's. The
pendulum survives as a claim about who holds decisive force inside the
polity; it does not survive as a claim about the purity of borders, and
the chapter should stop making one.

## Objection 7 — Ruggie: the weapons thesis cannot explain the form, and "there are none of it"

**Steelman.** Ruggie names the book's mechanism and rejects it, in the
strongest theoretical form of the anti-determinism objection the outline
assigns to the Swiss pikes. Of the claim that a shift in weapons
technology "drove the desire for larger and fiscally more capable
political formations" (Ruggie 1993: 156, citing McNeill): "The theory that
the modern state was functionally determined in this manner has at least
two serious shortcomings. First, its retrodictive value is severely
limited … the Italian city-states and the Hanse, in fact were viable
political alternatives to the territorial state, fully able to levy taxes
and raise armies, for the better part of two centuries. In social life,
two centuries is no mere time lag. Second, there is a substantial logical
and empirical gap between the existence of some functional pressure for
political units to grow, and their blossoming specifically into a system
of territorially defined, territorially fixed, and mutually exclusive
state formations. To assert that the specificities of the modern state
system also were functionally determined entails a claim of staggering
historical and intellectual proportions" (p. 156). And against any
repeatable mechanism at all: "while there may be law-like generalizations
in the medieval-to-modern transformation, there are none of it.
Accordingly, understanding that transformation — and presumably any
analogous shift that may be taking place today — requires an
epistemological posture that is quite different from the imperious claims
of most current bodies of international relations theory" (p. 169). This
reaches the DEFEND-tier mechanism, spine §8(a), and CLAUDE.md §1 claim 2
("a MECHANISM, not an analogy"; "a repeatable historical arc").

**The chapter's answer.** Three limbs. First, Ruggie attacks a claim the
book does not make — that weapons determined the *form*. The chain ends at
the subordination of intermediaries under a permanent fisc, and spine
§8(g) has already ruled the constitutional form open; Ruggie's second
shortcoming is now the spine's own position and should be conceded under
his name — the form objection is not answered but adopted. Second, his two
centuries are the two centuries *before* the ratchet: his counter-examples
run 1300–1500, the mechanism 1440–1500, and the book's evidence is the end
of exactly those alternatives — the Hansa's disintegration (ch04), Venice
unable in the long run to compete (ch10), Charles VIII's train (chs. 6,
10). Spruyt's selection event has no date in Ruggie; the book supplies it,
and Ruggie's own p. 161 concedes the decades — "The turn came suddenly."
Third, his positive account — "legitimate power became fused with the
provision of public order, steadily discrediting its deployment for
primitive extraction and accumulation" (p. 161) — is a legitimation layer
the book can carry (chs. 7, 9), not a rival cause.

**Grade.** Good on the first two limbs. Weaker on the third and honestly
so: the book has no account of why the *idea* of exclusive sovereignty
formed (Ruggie's social-epistemology domain — single-point perspective,
p. 159) and should not pretend to one; "there are none of it" is answered
only by claiming one link, not the transformation, which the Coda's method
note must say. The objection is carried here as well as at ch10 because it
is the strongest theoretical statement of the anti-determinism case the
chapter's method must survive.

## Objection 8 — Ruggie: Bull's test is the wrong test

**Steelman.** Spine §7 has Bull supplying the test — "does the trend
replace sovereignty, or reproduce it in new units?" — and the §III [DELTA]
asks the chapter to restate its "crossed on his own measures" claim
against Bull's reproduce-or-replace tests. Ruggie denies that
substitutability is the question at all: "There is an extraordinarily
impoverished mind-set at work here, one that is able to visualize
long-term challenges to the system of states only in terms of entities
that are institutionally substitutable for the state … The theoretical or
historical warrant for that premise has never been mooted, let alone
defended" (p. 143); and "In no sense could the medieval trade fairs have
become substitutes for the institutions of feudal rule. Yet, the fairs
contributed significantly to the demise of feudal authority relations"
(p. 155). If transformation ran through institutions that substituted for
nothing, then a chapter that crosses Bull's threshold by showing
non-substitutable entities acquiring authority has proved something Bull's
test cannot register, and a chapter that fails to cross it has proved
nothing about transformation either way.

**The chapter's answer.** The test the book applies is subordination
(spine §8(h)(b)), not substitution: the platforms are not substitutes for
the state — the universalism correction of §III says so — and a
non-substitutable entity can still be subordinated, or fail to be. Bull's
per-trend tests remain useful as the founder's, and the [DELTA] keeps
them; but the "new units" limb of spine §7's formulation is the book's,
not Bull's, and §7 should say so. Ruggie's fairs cut both ways — the lord
could close the fair but another lord would host it (p. 155) — which is
why the mechanism predicts consolidation inside blocs, where the
alternative sites run out.

**Grade.** Plausible-unproven until the [DELTA] is resolved: the answer
gives the [DELTA] its resolving principle but does not itself rewrite the
§III paragraph, which is Roderick's. Recorded in the memo (Revisions
1(iv)) as an addition to the [DELTA], not a closure.

## Objection 9 — Goldsmith and Wu, with Farrell and Newman: the state reasserted over the network for free, without artillery or a fisc

**Steelman.** Goldsmith and Wu re-territorialised the network's legibility
layer — content, commerce, payments, identity — between 2000 and 2006 with
no artillery-scale investment and no fiscal-administrative construction.
The instrument was law on concentrated local chokepoints, and it was
cheap: "Government regulation works by cost and bother, not by hermetic
seal" (2006: 68); the ATF "simply ordered Visa, MasterCard, and AmEx to
stop taking online cigarette orders … And that is how, without ever laying
a finger on online sellers, the government can impose its power, often
without even needing to go to court" (pp. 76–77); "physical coercion by
government—the hallmark of a traditional legal system—remains far more
important than anyone expected" (p. 180). Farrell and Newman add that
the levers were not even built by the state: the asymmetric networks "were
not constructed as tools of statecraft … By building centralized networks,
market actors inadvertently provide states … with the necessary levers to
extend their influence across borders" (2019: 54). Read straight, the
identity-payments-legibility stack of CLAUDE.md §1 claim 3 was taken back
by ordinary coercion twenty years before the book's mechanism is said to
run, and the mechanism is superfluous to that half of the wager. This
reaches spine §6's DEFEND-tier sentence as applied to the legibility limb,
and spine §7's tense rule — the reassertion these authors describe ended
"by the mid-2000s," accomplished.

**The chapter's answer.** (i) Category: their chokepoints are the
canon-and-register layer, which the manuscript already holds cheap — ch11's
fifth move ("the harness was the canon and the register, not the plant";
the state "could mandate the canon") worked where it rode on the fourth
and failed wherever it depended on holding the instrument. Access-control
lists on a carrier's routers are the Index and the licensing proclamation
on wire; Goldsmith and Wu are not a rival to the mechanism but the fullest
documentation of Moves four and five on the network. (ii) What cheap
coercion cannot do is the book's subject: it can order a carrier's routers
to drop packets; it cannot build the router, the fab, the model or the
constellation — "built with American bricks" (p. 93) is dependence stated
and not theorised. The mechanism explains the compulsion to move from
mandating the canon to holding the plant, where the fiscal cost begins.
Farrell and Newman's own scope condition does half the work on their side
too: the levers are free, but the hand is not — "states' variable ability
to employ these forms of coercion will depend on the combination of the
structure of the underlying network and the domestic institutions of the
states attempting to use them" (2019: 58), and position without apparatus
is impotent. (iii) Their scale limit — "The techniques of intermediary
control are generally less effective in small nations" (2006: 81–82) — is
the mechanism's precondition from the legal side: intermediary control
works for polities whose markets hold the intermediaries' assets, the
blocs, and fails for the interstices.

**Grade.** (i) and (ii) good; (iii) adequate. But the concession must be
made in text, not buried: the legibility stack was re-territorialised by
law before the artillery mechanism ran, and the mechanism's claim over it
is confined to scale and to its fusion with the chokepoint layer (map,
then cut), not to its existence. That requires a layered tense —
accomplished for canon and register, compelled and unaccomplished for the
decisive layer — a register amendment to spine §7 proposed in the memo,
not a reversal. The fiscal threshold is defended on the military stack,
not on the coercion channels.

## Objection 10 — Schmitt: the two universalisms were not competitors but orders of one *respublica*

**Steelman.** §III, following Friedrichs, renders the medieval hinge as
"the standing competition of two universalisms — Empire and Church, each
claiming the whole world in a different register, neither able to absorb
the other." Schmitt insists the competition sat inside one order: the
medieval unity "rested on the distinction between potestas [power] and
auctoritas [authority] as two distinct lines of order of the same
encompassing unity. Thus, the antitheses of emperor and pope were not
absolute, but rather diversi ordines" (Schmitt 1950: 61), and the real
division came only with the thirteenth-century *societas perfecta*
doctrine ("Misfortune did not arise until the 13th century," same page).
If the two universalisms were not trying to absorb each other, the
"duality" that Friedrichs makes the engine of the plural order is
misdescribed, and the modern translation (states v. market) inherits the
error.

**The chapter's answer.** Competition-within-unity is still competition,
and Friedrichs's "held together by" already concedes the unity; Schmitt's
own account has the order surviving the emperor's impotence "as long as
the auctoritas of the pope sufficed" (p. 66), which is authority real and
nowhere exclusive — the chapter's substrate stated by the century's most
influential jurist of spatial order. The precision is worth a footnote at
"neither able to absorb the other": they were not trying to, being orders
of the same *respublica*; and the dating to the thirteenth century is the
chapter's own baseline decade. Wherever Schmitt is cited, even in a note,
the 1939 provenance of the *Großraum* doctrine travels with the citation.

**Grade.** Adequate; footnote-grade, not argument-level for this chapter.
His rival cause for the state's formation — "solely" a new spatial order,
p. 140 — is ch10's critiques, and is not carried here.
