# What you need to decide — a plain briefing

*2 September 2026*

This briefing explains, in ordinary language, the decisions that only you can make before the book can move forward. It defines every term as it goes. There are three real decisions, followed by a reading job, a shopping job, and one piece of writing. Nothing here is urgent in the sense of a deadline, but the machine cannot do its next round of work until the three decisions are made, because that work has to be written against your rulings.

## First, some words that come up throughout

**The spine.** A file called spine.md at the top of the project. It is your private judgement document: what you actually believe, in your own words, and how confident you are in each part. The drafting machine reads it so that the chapters carry your convictions rather than guessing at them. Nobody but you is supposed to change it. It currently has eight sections; section 7 is your statement that the book's claim is a prediction, not a report; section 8, added today, is the set of rulings you gave on 9 August.

**The council.** In August you asked several different artificial intelligence models to review the book independently, and their answers were combined into two documents. The first, "Model Council Synthesis: The Artillery State," assessed the whole book. The second, "Why English Normandy Collapsed So Fast," answered your objection that the speed of the French reconquest in 1449 to 1453 must have come from artillery. Both are now filed in the project's research folder. Their arguments are useful; their facts are unverified until checked against real sources.

**A probe.** A research file that sits inside a chapter's folder and gathers evidence on one specific question, with every claim traced to a source. Today's Claude Code session wrote two: one on how the French crown's permanent tax system came about, and one on what actually happened when Standard Oil was broken up in 1911.

**"Pending human review."** The label the machine puts on anything it has written that you have not yet read and approved. Nothing so labelled is treated as settled. When you have read something and agree with it, you tell the machine and it changes the label to "reviewed."

**A Revisions entry.** The machine never silently rewrites a file you have already approved. Instead it adds a dated note under a "Revisions" heading saying what it found and what it proposes. Today's session added about a dozen such notes across the chapters. They are the things waiting for you to read.

**The taille.** The permanent direct tax that the French crown began collecting in the 1440s to pay for a standing army. It matters to the book because the book argues that paying for artillery forced kings to build permanent taxation and administration, and that this is what consolidated their power.

**The Estates.** The representative assembly of the French kingdom, which in principle had to consent to taxation. The council argued that the Estates meeting at Orléans in 1439 voted the taille into permanence.

**The ordonnance of 1439.** A royal decree issued at Orléans on 2 November 1439. The one thing everybody agrees it did was to give the king the exclusive right to raise troops and to license captains.

**Contamine.** Philippe Contamine, the historian whose two-volume study of war, state and society in late medieval France is the book's most important scholarly source. Both volumes are in the project and have been read in full by the machine.

**The ratchet.** Your own image from the 9 August rulings. A ratchet is a mechanism that lets a wheel turn one way and stops it turning back. You said artillery was not the motor of the French state's consolidation and not a mere consequence of it, but the ratchet: the thing that made the new tax system impossible to reverse, because the guns it paid for delivered proof no assembly could argue with.

**Appendix C.** The part of the book that turns its prediction into a scorecard: a list of measurable indicators, each with a starting value and a stated direction, so that a reader in fifteen years can tell whether the book was right. The council's strongest criticism was that this scorecard needs firm dates and firm conditions for being wrong, not just directions.

**A sub-wager.** A piece of the book's overall prediction that can be scored on its own. The council suggested splitting the single big wager into several of these. Today an "American sub-wager" was drafted from your playbook.

## Decision one: what to say about 1439

**What you ruled on 9 August.** That finance and firepower built each other over two decades: the taille of 1439 and the standing companies of 1445 came before the decisive use of the guns in 1449 to 1453, but the artillery revolution of the 1420s and 1430s came before the taille, so the Estates voted for permanent taxation in a world where royal guns were already visibly paying off. Artillery was the ratchet.

**What the probe found.** Three things. First, Contamine's text of the 1439 ordonnance contains no grant of a tax at all. It is the troop-monopoly decree. Permanent taxation, as far as the sources in the project show, was a practice that hardened over the following years through annual decrees, a formula used in 1446 that granted money "until the conclusion of peace," and a consolidation in 1450, rather than a single vote in 1439. So the phrase "the taille of 1439," which the council used and which your ruling repeats, is currently a piece of period shorthand that the record in the project does not carry. The full text of the 1439 decree exists in a printed collection called the *Ordonnances des Roys de France*, volume thirteen, which is available free online and is now on the list of things to fetch. Second, Contamine dates the point at which the king's councillors regarded the new arrangements as effectively permanent to 1445 and 1446, which is before the guns broke the walls of Normandy in 1449. So if the ratchet is the guns, what the guns locked in was not the crown's intention, which was already fixed, but everyone else's acquiescence: no Estates General was summoned again until 1484, and when it finally met, the system survived it. Third, the co-evolution itself is well supported. The Bureau brothers had taken over the artillery by 1437, and in 1442 the crown was spending twice as much on the new artillery as on the old, all under an improvised financial regime with, in Contamine's words, "neither budget nor forecasts." The guns achieved superiority before predictable finance existed, and predictable finance then made that superiority permanent.

**What you have to decide.** Whether to keep the words "the taille of 1439" in your spine as accepted shorthand until the decree's text has been fetched and read, or to change the ruling now to something like "the settlement of 1439 to 1446," and to add one sentence saying whose ability to reverse the settlement the ratchet removed: the assemblies' and the magnates', not the king's. My own view is that the second option is stronger and costs nothing, because the refined version fits the evidence better and still supports everything you want to say. But the spine is your document, and this is a sentence only you should write.

## Decision two: how to state the Standard Oil proof case

**What you ruled on 9 August.** That the United States will consolidate control over its technology stack in its own traditional way: not by nationalising the platforms but by licensing, legal process and, above all, the ability to switch their systems off. You gave Standard Oil as the proof: when the Supreme Court broke it up in 1911, John D. Rockefeller became richer and less powerful, because the state did not take the oil, it destroyed the vertical integration that made oil sovereign. And you asked a question: after the breakup, who owned the power to turn off the energy?

**What the probe found.** It fetched and read the Supreme Court's 1911 opinion and decree. Your reading holds on the power side: the decree handed every share of the thirty-seven successor companies back to the existing shareholders, Rockefeller first among them, and forbade the seven named individuals from ever recombining them. Wealth was kept; unified command was extinguished. But the answer to your question is a surprise. In 1911 the power to turn off the energy passed to nobody. The decree removed the private switch and did not create a public one. The public switch was then built over the following three decades by a different set of laws: the Hepburn Act of 1906, which made pipelines common carriers under federal supervision and which came five years before the breakup; a Texas law of 1917 doing the same at state level; the wartime Trading with the Enemy Act of 1917, whose licensing power over transactions is the ancestor of every later economic control; the Texas Railroad Commission's orders from 1930 onward, under which a state agency set the rate at which every oil well in Texas could produce; and federal emergency and energy-policy legislation through the 1970s. So the proof case has a lag: the decoupling of wealth from power happened in one act in 1911, but the state acquired the off-switch over a generation, using regulation rather than antitrust. The probe notes that this lag mirrors what the first probe found about France: a settlement that hardened over years, not a single stroke. One further caution: the figure for Rockefeller's fortune tripling after the breakup is currently supported only by an article without footnotes, so it cannot be used until a proper biography, Chernow's *Titan* or Yergin's *The Prize*, is in the project.

**A sentence in the chapter that now conflicts with your ruling.** Chapter 12, which you have already reviewed, currently says the platforms are "not the American state's Standard Oil but its monasteries," and treats the antitrust instinct as nineteenth-century nostalgia. Your ruling says Standard Oil is the proof case. The two can be reconciled, because the chapter is attacking the nostalgic idea of trust-busting while your ruling reads 1911 as a consolidation of control, but the sentence as written contradicts the spine, and the machine will not touch a reviewed draft without your instruction.

**What you have to decide.** Two things. Whether the proof case, when it is written into chapter 12, is stated with the lag, which I recommend, since it is truer and it rhymes with the French story, or in the compressed form. And how the monasteries sentence is to be reconciled: dropped, qualified, or kept alongside a new passage explaining that the American method was regulation of the switch rather than seizure of the property.

## Decision three: the six proposed changes to chapter 6

**Background.** Chapter 6, "1450: The Year the Walls Fell," is the book's most complete chapter and the one that carries its central evidence. The Normandy council answered your objection that the speed of the reconquest must have come from artillery by agreeing with you at one level and not another. Its formulation, which five of eight models reached in nearly the same words, was that artillery set the clock speed of the collapse, while finance and politics determined that a collapse would happen at all. Most of the roughly one hundred strongholds never saw a gun; they surrendered under negotiated agreements that fixed a date on which the gates would open unless a royal relief army appeared. The French call this a *délai de secours*, a relief deadline. Artillery made those deadlines short, because everyone knew what the guns could do; then the destruction of the only English field army at Formigny in April 1450 meant no relief could ever come, and every deadline expired.

**What the council recommended and what the machine found.** Six changes, each assessed today against what the chapter already says. First, to separate two different kinds of speed, the speed of the campaign, which was a chain of surrenders with political and financial causes, from the speed of the individual sieges, which was genuinely the guns' doing and roughly ten times faster than before. Second, to make the relief deadline the chapter's central device. Third, to quote two English documents as the English state's own post-mortem: the Duke of Somerset's report to Parliament in 1449 that Normandy had no place supplied with ordnance or artillery of any kind, and William Worcester's *Boke of Noblesse*, which says in plain terms that lack of money to pay the soldiers was a cause of the loss, and to quote the French chronicler Blondel's line that it is not walls that protect cities but soldiers trained for war. Worcester's text has now been fetched and the key passages verified. Fourth, to present the Battle of Castillon, the book's opening scene, with two dissenting voices acknowledged: the historian Michael Howard's flat statement that it was in no sense a victory for field artillery, and the contemporary chronicler Thomas Basin's verdict that Talbot died of his own rashness. Fifth, to add the Gascony sequence of 1451 to 1453 as a controlled experiment: the same artillery train won Gascony by negotiation in 1451, lost it in a week in October 1452 when Bordeaux invited the English back, and had to win it again by fire in 1453, which shows that guns produce speed but not loyalty. The machine notes that Vale's 1969 study disputes the council's claim that a broken tax promise was the trigger for Bordeaux's revolt, so that detail needs care. Sixth, to add a caution that several of the French sources praising the artillery are one interdependent tradition rather than independent witnesses.

**What you have to decide.** A yes or a no on each of the six, recorded in the assessment file in the chapter 6 folder. The machine has stated for each what the chapter already contains and which supporting sources are in the project at page level and which are not.
