# The September intake, read against the book — what to add, what to change, what to decide

## 1. What was read

Twenty-seven works: Andrade, Basin (Samaran, tome I), Bobbitt, Brands and Gavin (eds), Bratton (the 2015 edition, 727-page file), Chernow, Elliott, Fortescue (Plummer's edition, whole), Freedman, Hall, Hoffman, Howard, İnalcık, Kafadar, Miller, Parrott, Rogers (ed.), Scheidel, Sharman, Spruyt, Strayer, Tilly, Vale (*Charles VII*), Varoufakis, Vaughan (*John the Fearless*), Yergin, Zielonka. Four were scans without text (Howard, Spruyt, Tilly, İnalcık) and were OCR'd; Andrade was an EPUB and carries chapter pins rather than page pins. Two were not on any list — Brands and Varoufakis — and turn out to matter. Everything below is drawn from the assessment files, which carry the page pins; this note is the synthesis, and it is organised by what the books do to the argument rather than book by book.

The second batch, read the same evening, adds twenty-two works: Griffiths (*The Reign of King Henry VI*), Lander (*Crown and Nobility*), Depreter (*De Gavre à Nancy*, the monograph), Barker (*Conquest*), DeVries (*Guns and Men in Medieval Europe*), Lane (*Venice: A Maritime Republic*), Lane and Mueller (*Money and Banking in Medieval and Renaissance Venice*, vol. I), Morris (*The Papal Monarchy*), Dean (*Crime and Justice in Late Medieval Italy*), Scott and Scribner (*The German Peasants' War: A History in Documents*), Eisenstein (*The Printing Press as an Agent of Change*), Johns (*The Nature of the Book*), six articles (Ágoston 2014, İnalcık's 1960 *Speculum* piece, Payling 1998, Grummitt 1999, Elliott's "A Europe of Composite Monarchies" 1992, Baker 1979), Belloc's *The Modern Traveller*, the Paston Letters in Gairdner's 1872 edition (vol. I), a Nicholson volume, and the appendices of Cosneau's *Le Connétable de Richemont* (pièces LXXII–LXXXVII). Two files are not what the ledger wanted: the Nicholson is *Love, War and the Grail* (Brill, 2001), a study of the military orders in romance, not the history of the orders the ch01 gap needs; and the Eisenstein file is the Chinese translation, so its pins are usable but every quotation must be re-quoted from the English edition before it is cited. Barker, Cosneau, Belloc, Paston, Baker and the Nicholson were scans and were OCR'd.

## 2. The headline

The direction holds. Not one of the twenty-seven contradicts the mechanism at the level the spine defends it — the price of decisive force compelling permanent finance, permanent finance compelling administrative capacity, and the guns of 1449–53 making the settlement of 1439–46 irreversible for everyone who might have reversed it. Several of the rival-mechanism books turn out to concede the parts the book needs: Parrott concedes the rated warship as the one technology that forced ownership "akin to the role of siege artillery"; Hoffman concedes the entry price and the institutional character of the train; Tilly concedes state-made cannon and Tudor subordination; Spruyt concedes that war drove "an exponential increase in state demands" after 1450 and quarrels only with the starting gun; Andrade's own cited evidence has the guns doing exactly what the book says they do in the one part of China that still had intermediate authorities to suppress.

What changes is the register, in about nine places, and a handful of facts. Three of the register changes are already implied by rulings you made on 2 September and simply need to be carried through the text; the rest are for you to decide. There are also five findings you should know about before anything else is written, because they alter what the chapters can claim.

The second batch does not alter that verdict, but it bears harder on the *English* limb and on the medieval set-pieces than the first did, because it contains the standard monographs — Griffiths, Lander, Barker, Depreter, Lane, Morris, Dean — against which a specialist reviewer will read chapters 4 through 11. Read together they say one thing the book must now say plainly: the mechanism ran cleanly in France, and everywhere else it ran partially, late, or in a different order. Venice built the fisc and the arsenal and refused the bureaucracy; Burgundy built the apparatus on a fisc that was not permanent and lost the guns with the duke; the Ottomans had foundry, payroll and register before any Latin monarchy and then devolved with the guns in hand; England had a monopoly of force without a fisc until the 1530s and consolidated by avoiding war; Castile ran the chain and the rest of Spain never completed it. None of these is a counter-example to the mechanism as the spine defends it, but each is a counter-example to any sentence that treats the French sequence as the general case, and there are several such sentences in the drafts. Five further findings from this batch belong beside the first five.

## 3. Five things to know first

**The Howard sentence is not in Howard.** Ch06 §VI now stages "Michael Howard's dissent" that Castillon "was in no sense a victory for field artillery." The words Castillon, Talbot and 1453 do not occur anywhere in *War in European History*, nor in its index. The one page that treats 1449–53 (printed p. 14) says nearly the opposite — "a few artillery discharges at the beginning shattered the ranks of the archers" — and a block of lines there is lost to the scan. The council's attribution was wrong, or it belongs to another author (the assessor's guess is a misremembered Bert Hall). The dissent must be re-sourced or recast before it goes near print; and Howard, correctly read, is a better witness than the one the draft invented: gunners decisive in siege (p. 13), field artillery marginal in general until Gustavus (p. 30).

**Basin's Castillon is in a volume we do not have.** The Samaran edition in corpus is tome I (to 1444). The Castillon verdict, the portrait of Jean Bureau and the "fifteen companies" sentence the draft quotes from Quicherat are all in tome II (1944), printed from the Göttingen manuscript Quicherat never saw; Samaran calls Quicherat's text "une copie postérieure de près d'un siècle, copie incomplète et parfois assez corrompue." Every Castillon pin currently carried under Quicherat must be re-read in tome II. What tome I gives instead is worth having: Basin's "temerity" is a stock verdict he applies to every commander who attacks an entrenched camp — Clarence at Baugé, the Scots at Verneuil, René's Germans at Bulgnéville — which lowers the weight of his Castillon verdict against your conjecture; and the Caux rising of 1435–36, a whole province rising for the French crown with no royal instrument behind it, cut to pieces by three hundred horse, is the third controlled case for the morale argument, cleaner than the two the chapter now uses.

**Vale hands the Talbot conjecture its missing document.** In June 1450 Somerset offered Scots of the bodyguard 4,000 écus to seize one of four men — Dunois, Villequier, Jacques Coeur or "Jean Bureau, treasurer of France" — while a thousand troops went "among the king's artillery, putting torches into the powder kegs and spiking the bombards" (Vale p. 138; MS Dupuy 38; Charles VII to James II, Stevenson i. 346–51, which is in corpus). Three years before Castillon, the English side understood the train and its accountant as the target. It does not prove Talbot's mind; it removes the objection that nobody at the time thought that way.

**Chernow changes the Rockefeller sentence.** On Chernow's account Rockefeller had retired from power in 1897–99; the presidency was "entirely nominal… Archbold… ran the organization" (p. 485). The decree of 1911 severed Archbold's command, not his — and even that survived informally until about 1921 (daily 10.30 meetings at 26 Broadway, eleven marketing territories, the Burton cracking patent; pp. 623–24). The fortune figures ($300m in 1911, $900m in 1913) are in Chernow but unfootnoted there too; Yergin's $900m traces to Nevins. The successor count is thirty-four companies (thirty-three spun off plus the parent), against thirty-seven affiliates named in the decree. So "Rockefeller got richer and less powerful" must become a sentence about the office, which ch12 already half-writes; and the spine's open question — who held the switch after 1911 — has an answer: the same men, informally and eroding, for a decade, before the public switch existed.

**Fortescue saw the fiscal machine and rejected it.** The most systematic English post-mortem of the crown's weakness, written twenty years after Castillon, budgets every royal charge and contains no line for guns; the words "gonnes," "ordenaunce," "artillery" do not occur in his text. But he describes the French taille and the standing army precisely — and calls them the *destruction* of French might ("the myght of his reaume is nerehande distroyed therby," p. 117), because England's strength "stondith most vppon archers, wich be no ryche men." Your ruling §8(e)(6) says the defeated did not see the fiscal apparatus. Fortescue shows the defeated saw it, called it tyranny, and failed to see what it had just bought them in Normandy. That is a sharper sentence, and it makes Fortescue the longbow theorist in person.

## 3b. Five more things to know first (second batch)

**The Cade footnote cites Griffiths for the opposite of what he says.** Ch07 §I attributes the "gentry skeleton" of the rising — the pardon roll read as a roster of propertied rebels — to Griffiths pp. 619–20. Griffiths argues that the pardon list cannot be read that way: Cheyne was the only knight pardoned on 6–7 July and is offered as an *unlikely* rebel; officials, abbots and priors took pardons for protection; the list "represents, in brief, a cross-section of society in the south-east of England, but little more" (p. 621). The "eighteen squires, two MPs" tally is not in Griffiths at all. The chapter's propertied-revolt claim survives — on the programme, the county's structure and Harvey and Bohna — but the footnote must be re-sourced and Griffiths recorded as the dissent. Griffiths also separates the three Cade manifestos that the draft runs together as Stow's *Complaint*, dates the Blackheath encampment to 11 June rather than the first week of the month, and has Moleyns killed by one of his own captains, not lynched by unpaid soldiers.

**Henry VII cannot be the book's English consolidator.** Lander's tallies are the ones ch11 was waiting for, and his verdict is that England 1461–1509 consolidated by avoiding war and taxation, kept no standing army because it could not pay for one, and disciplined its nobles by a legal terror of recognizances that Henry VIII dismantled within a year of his accession (at least 45 cancelled in the first year, 130 more over the next five; "possibly nine peers at the most remained bound" by mid-1510, pp. 298–9). On Lander's account Henry VII is the pre-settlement case — monopoly of force without a fisc, governing by bond — and Grummitt's rediscovered clerks' books say the same from the other side: the chamber was a real reserve, spent on the Cornish rebels of 1497 before the tax arrived, but it was an informal, decentralised system of deposit treasuries, "not simply a continuation" of the Yorkist chamber and not the personal weekly ledger the draft imagines either. The English Tudor turn must therefore be dated to the 1530s, with the dissolution named as its fisc, or spine §3's "standing taxation established" must be rewritten for England. DeVries independently makes England the mechanism's weakest leg: his "dark ages" of English artillery run to the mid-sixteenth century.

**"The Depreter refinement" is not Depreter's.** The monograph never mentions a dynastic centre. It confirms the apparatus — recette, controller, ordinary gunners, a ducal monopoly of the guns enforced against towns and nobles — and denies the manuscript's premise that the apparatus was complete and fiscally sound. The Nancy failure sits in a contested *charroi* levy, an embezzling receiver (Menostey, gaoled 15 January 1477 and dismissed with a fine) and materiel losses at Grandson and Morat that Depreter calls "sans doute catastrophiques," with the ducal arsenals "quasi vidés" (pp. 183–84). What passed to Mary and Maximilian was the office structure, not a park of guns, so "transferred the whole apparatus intact — lands, revenues, recette and guns" cannot stand. Depreter's own evidence supports a two-variable reading — offices without fiscal permanence, plus one mortal node — which fits ruling §8(a) better than the present one-variable sentence, and the refinement should be relabelled as the book's own inference from Depreter, not his thesis. Lane's Venice completes the triad by variation from the opposite side: a centre nothing could kill (Foscari deposed by vote, the system carried on), capital, credit and an arsenal, and by Lane's flat verdict no bureaucracy and no executive — "those most nearly successful were, except for Venice, monarchies" (p. 251). Burgundy had the apparatus and one mortal node; Venice had a survivable centre and no apparatus; neither consolidated.

**Two of chapter 6's set-piece sieges are, on Barker's account, a sale and a starvation.** Cherbourg's "longest resistance" and Verneuil's "impregnable" Grey Tower are the chapter's exhibits for the same-wall proof; Barker has Cherbourg's surrender bought and the tower starved out, and she supplies the deflationary reading — provisioning, leadership, purchase — that a specialist will bring to every compression in the Normandy list. The chapter must now distinguish the sieges where guns are documented on the wall (Argentan's cart-sized hole, Caen's mined tower) from those where hunger and money did the work. Her compensating gift is the English side of the ledger: two hundred guns at Dieppe, a train abandoned at Avranches, Kyriell landing 2,500 men "and a great artillery train" — which proves the chapter's real claim, that the decisive object was an institution and not a park of cannon, better than anything the draft now cites. She also corrects Rouen's ransom to 50,000 *saluts*, replaces Honfleur with Harfleur in the Talbot forfeiture, and confirms with Griffiths that Fougères was planned in London fifteen months ahead by a Garter knight on a royal pension, not "seized by an English freebooter."

**Louppy-le-Château is now in corpus, and it is not what the chapter says it presupposes.** Cosneau's pièce LXXXIV is a royal commission of 26 May 1445 to five named commissioners for Poitou, assigning 190 lances (100 under the seneschal, 60 under Loheac, 30 of Floquet's) and taxing the province — "exemps et non exemps, privilégiez et non privilégiez" — in kind and money to feed them, quarterly, with the sum left blank in the original. It carries no total establishment, no fifteen captains, no money wage, no reference to the taille and no word "permanent," and its opening sentence shows the founding ordonnances already in existence and being applied. The lost general act is confirmed as earlier than 20 April (pièce LXXXIII, Richemont's safe-conduct citing it), but the 1,500-lance figure and crown pay rest on Basin, Escouchy and Contamine's other documents, and ch10 §I must say so. The compensating documents are better than the one the chapter wanted: pièce LXXXVI, the "trois voyes," shows the payroll's cashification — the choice offered each *pays* between provisioning in kind and a money rate of 31 l.t. per lance of six, which matches Contamine's tariff exactly (15 for the man-at-arms plus two archers at 7 l. 10 s.); pièce LXXXII, the 1444 export of the écorcheurs under the Dauphin "en maniére que la force et auctorité nous demeure," is a T1 sentence on the monopoly of force; and the spelling in Cosneau's heading is "Luppé-le-Chastel," with "Louppy" as his modern form and neither "-Château" nor "-Châtel" anywhere in the range.

## 4. Corrections of fact (to be made at the next intake run; pins in the assessments)

The Praguerie paragraph in ch07 (echoed in ch10) is wrong on Vale's account: the settlement was negotiated at Montferrand in May 1440, not "Cusset in July"; the rebels' pretext was the *aides*, not the taille; pardons were not universal (La Trémoille, Chaumont and Prie were banished); Dunois was among the rebels; and the ordonnance of 1439 was "a dead letter" until 1445, not "thereafter enforced." Basin, the nearest court insider, gives the rising no fiscal motive at all. The "27 December 1444" Gaspard Bureau date has no carrier in Vale either and should be dropped. The 1.8m/4.6m revenue arc should be re-pinned to Commynes via Vale (1.8m under Charles VII; 4.7m at Louis XI's death; total levy, not taille alone). Jean Bureau was mayor of Bordeaux from 1451, not "after 1453" (Hall). Cadillac: Vale and Hall both carry capitulation after bombardment, not a storm through a breach — resolve at the sources before "the only storming in the record" stands. The trebuchet had a high trajectory, not a flat one; the flat trajectory is the gun's (Hall p. 21). Corned powder of the 1440s "kept and burned better"; uniform burn is sixteenth-century (Hall). Bureau's new powder and iron shot date to 1453, not the whole decade (Hall p. 119). Ch01's Great Interregnum ran 1254–73, not from 1250 (Scheidel). The pre-1439 constitutional theory of French taxation was not simple consent but consent with a defence-of-the-realm override recognised by 1300 (Strayer p. 54) — which helps the ruling. Rogers does not rank the infantry revolution above the artillery revolution; he refuses a single hinge — the contest ch06 §VII stages with him is partly manufactured (Rogers ed., p. 56). The Çandarlı were ulema-origin, not aristocratic; Mehmed II completed the kul system rather than creating it; 1444 was a faction's coup, not a weary abdication (Kafadar, confirmed by İnalcık). Aragon in 1450–79 was "a society in retreat," not "expanding" (Elliott). The Habsburgs inherited the Spanish composite from Ferdinand; they did not build it (Elliott, Scheidel). The American denial regime dates from 2016–20 (ZTE, Jinhua, Huawei, the FDPR of May 2020), not from the October 2022 rule (Miller; Brands). "One EUV supplier" should read "one integrator, with American-held nodes inside it" (Miller p. 228–30). Proration in Texas was attempted from 1930, lawful from November 1932, federally policed from 1933–35 (Yergin). The Belloc couplet is not in Freedman; Freedman carries the knock-out-blow genealogy (Holman) and the Belloc text is a separate T1 retrieval.

From the second batch. The Arsenal was about sixty acres (some twenty-four hectares), employing about 2,000 men on average and 3,000 in emergencies — not forty-five hectares and sixteen thousand (Lane p. 362); replace the galley-a-day parenthesis with the dated surge of a hundred galleys in two months in 1570. The 1262 debt paid five per cent a year, half-yearly, and the forced loans fell on "the propertied," not "the wealthy" (p. 150); Lane gives no date for the Ten becoming permanent, nothing on the *bocche di leone*, no secret trial for Carmagnola (a formal hearing and a public execution, p. 232) and no captaincy-for-life for Colleoni. Colleoni "the loyal one" is wrong: in 1467 he was carving a Romagna principality with Florentine exiles until bought off, and the republic seized much of his estate the moment he was dead (pp. 233–36). The Çandarlı execution date — "1 June 1453, three days after the fall" — is the date of the Pera *amān-nāme* that Zaganos signed; İnalcık's own list closes Çandarlı's term on 30 May, and on the Ottoman sources the execution was in August or September (pp. 412–15). Janissary strength under Mehmed II grew "from four or five thousand to ten or twelve thousand" (İnalcık p. 426; Ágoston's first register gives 7,841 in 1484) — write the growth, not the band — and Ottoman gunners were paid by timar from the 1390s and salaried only a generation later, so the artillery itself began on the in-kind base. The Bergavenny fine was about two-thirds of a year's revenue, not a year, was probably paid only once, and was prosecuted under the rank rule of 1390 rather than the statute of 1504 (Lander pp. 33–4, 289); "private armies" is a term Lander calls "completely misleading" (p. 31), Buckingham's whole retinue costing him just over £900. The *Dictatus Papae* is undated, probably spring 1076, of uncertain authorship, and Morris reads it as a definition of emergency powers, not a monarchic blueprint (p. 112); Worms partitioned the symbols only in Germany; Canossa was Henry's coup, not a measure of the claim's reach; Innocent III's temporal intervention was *casualiter*, by reason of sin, and the apex of the claim is Innocent IV. The Teutonic Order was militarised only in 1198 and had no Prussian state in the making in 1200 (Nicholson p. 26) — re-date the ch01 clause or drop the Knights from the c. 1200 list, as the same paragraph does for the Hansa. The Bundschuh programme was "no lord but God and the Emperor" — the Pope or God is dropped in the draft, and it is the ecclesiastical half that makes the demand restorationist (Scott and Scribner pp. 10, 45). The Rouen ransom was 50,000 *saluts*; Talbot was held because Harfleur, not Honfleur, refused to submit; Kyriell landed 2,500 men, not four thousand; Rouen 1418–19 has no figure of twelve thousand expelled in Barker; Harcourt and Avranches carry two durations each (Barker, passim). DeVries is quoted at p. 138, not 137, for Commynes's "so much shooting"; his Porter and Rogers passages at p. 128 are quoted in order to oppose them, in support of Hale, and the note must say so; and "No baron had this. No city had this" is false for guns (Ghent's two hundred carts in 1382; Nuremberg's 2,230 pieces in 1462) and true only for the standing, salaried train. The Depreter pin for the Revolution-cartridges anecdote is p. 2 of the monograph, not p. 83, and "all but one destroyed" overstates. Ch08's four Paston quotations check against Gairdner 1872 vol. I — three verbatim, the Osbern letter smoothed ("Also, as he seyth, now late the Lord Molyns hath sent hym a letter") — but every citation given as "vol. II" or "no. 193" must be re-pinned, because the file is the 1872 edition and its numbering is not the 1904 library edition's. The Belloc couplet is section VI, printed p. 41, co-credited to B. T. B., and it is about a private company's Maxim gun holding a mutiny of its own porters, not a prophecy of the knock-out blow. The Cade chronology, Formigny figures and Fougères details are given above.

## 5. Register decisions — for your ruling

**(a) "Price" and "above what any subject could pay."** Three books converge on this. Hoffman's tables show the unit price of artillery *falling* through the very decades the book calls the repricing (English artillery relative to factor costs down 1.4 per cent a year 1382–1439; handgun productivity up 3 per cent a year). Hall shows powder at a fifth of its 1380s price by 1480 and private siege trains — Burgundy's, the Liège princes', the German cities' — persisting in France and Spain. Thompson (in Rogers ed.) shows the Spanish artillery account never exceeding 4–5 per cent of the military budget after 1490, and a marquis donating fifty tons of guns in lieu of taxes. None of this touches the 1442 document, which measures the right thing: an *establishment* absorbing the budget. The fix is vocabulary and one aphorism: say establishment cost, entry cost, standing cost — never unit price — and re-grade "no subject could pay" to "no subject could sustain it as a standing, provincial-scale instrument at campaign tempo." Vaughan's *John the Fearless* supplies the "before" picture: in 1409 the Dijon accounts report "gunpowder is not to be had and, in any case, there is no money to buy it"; the train at Rougemont in 1411 was "a rather motley collection" including pieces lent by nobles. Guns were everywhere in the 1410s and decided nothing; supply at tempo — powder, carriage, cash — was what the 1440s changed.

**(b) "Suppressing" → "subordinating" the intermediaries.** Tilly's flattest claim: "no European state (except, perhaps, Sweden) made a serious attempt to institute direct rule from top to bottom until the era of the French Revolution" (p. 25). Chapters 10–11 document subordination of the intermediaries' autonomous fiscal-military capacity — bonds, absorbed orders, licensed retaining, ranked courts — not their elimination. "Suppressing every authority between the crown and the taxable subject" (ch10 §I) and the chain's last link in CLAUDE.md §2 should say subordinating. This is a doctrine sentence, so it is yours.

**(c) "Ended" → "locked."** Strayer's state of 1300 was real — permanent institutions, professional administrators, "loyalty to the state… stronger than any other loyalty" — and, as he says himself, "still not very strong" and reversible. His own account of the late-fifteenth-century turn is a confessed blank ("It is difficult to decide what factors changed the behavior of the possessing classes," p. 91). Scheidel's fifteen centuries say the same from the macro side: consolidation within, plurality between, and the state system as polycentrism's mature form rather than a break in it. Both books are answered by ruling §8(a) as it stands — the ratchet's object is everyone else's ability to reverse — provided the Introduction, ch01 §VI and the Coda stop saying the plural order was *ended* in 1440–1500 and say the overlapping order was *locked* into units while inter-unit plurality hardened. The trellis is Strayer's; the ratchet is the book's.

**(d) §8(e)(6), reworded per Fortescue** (see §3 above): the defeated saw the fiscal apparatus, called it tyranny, and did not see what it bought.

**(e) §8(c), the oil case: "severs" → "acquires and keeps."** Yergin's public switch was not taken from the barons; it was begged for by a bankrupt industry ("crawling to Washington on their hands and knees") and built as a state-run price cartel with federal police, kept deliberately at state level "where it would be… much less visible," and voluntary in form — and in 1973 Washington discovered the companies still held the international switch. Chernow's 1911 was a market already dissolving the monopoly, with the state supplying the form. The lag and the hybrid form are confirmed exactly; the verb "severs" is not. Wherever Standard Oil is the exhibit: the republic acquired the switch when the barons could no longer hold it, and kept it. (The twelve-case pattern as a whole is untouched; the AT&T and Standard Oil decrees still preserve wealth by design.)

**(f) The Depreter limb of the wager.** Zielonka's whole book is that canon alone *did* consolidate a bloc — a jurisdiction of twenty-five states built by conditionality and no fisc ("This was power politics at its best, even though the term 'power' was never mentioned"). As the Introduction's condition 4 is drafted, he has already collected. The narrowing is honest and costs nothing: the limb should read "consolidate a bloc's decisive capability," not "consolidate a bloc." His concession that extraction is "the crucial pillar of any stateness" and that the stable plural Europe rests on the American guarantee are the two sentences to answer him with, by name.

**(g) The Ottoman case.** Kafadar and İnalcık together: the classical Ottoman state was made without artillery in the causal chain — unigeniture, the kul army of the 1370s financed by the *pençik* on the gazis' booty, the reduction of frontier begs to removable appointees — and 1453 sealed it by prestige as much as by wall-breaking. İnalcık's decline chapter then runs the mechanism with the sign reversed: firearm infantry demanding coin that an in-kind fiscal base could not supply, re-dispersing power to a provincial stratum; Sharman's figures (treasury share of revenue from about 60 per cent in the 1500s to under 20 by 1700) are the same story. So ch11 §V's closing line — "none was executable without it" — is refuted for the Ottoman member of the four and must exempt it; and the Ottoman ratchet slipping from inside is a fifth failure mode the book has not imagined, and the one the platform chapters should fear: absorption undone by the absorbed capability becoming an estate.

**(h) Spain, per Elliott.** Castile 1474–1504 executes the mechanism cleanly (revenue up nearly thirtyfold outside Cortes control; the magnates' political power broken; the Orders annexed and then pledged to bankers; the corregidor in every town) — "in Castile at least," which is Elliott's own boundary. Beyond Castile the greatest military power of the sixteenth century was a plurality that never completed the settlement and fielded decisive force for a century and a half on Castile, credit and silver. That narrows the DEFEND-tier claim from precondition of decisive force to condition of *sustaining* it, and the chapter should say so.

**(i) The longbow metaphor.** Hall: "None but the king of England could seriously hope to employ masses of archers"; the longbow "depended on an entire peasant culture to sustain it." The democratised, learn-in-weeks weapon was the crossbow and then the arquebus. Spine §4's "the people's weapon: democratised, cheap, effective en masse" is true of the pike and the hand-gun, and of the drone; it is not true of the longbow. The metaphor is about decisiveness, and the Agincourt caveat already refuses the longbow-alone story — but you may want to choose the weapon the metaphor rides on with this in view.

**(j) The 1360s.** Hoffman, via Henneman, dates France's first permanent taxation to Charles V in the 1360s, made permanent and then abolished on his deathbed in 1380. The settlement of 1439–46 is therefore the *second* making of permanence, the first having been reversed — which is the strongest evidence the book has that irreversibility, not permanence, is the thing to explain, and the thing Hoffman's model assumes and his own French case disproves. Ch10 §III should carry it.

**(k) England's date.** Lander and Grummitt together (see §3b): Henry VII is monopoly-without-fisc, governing by recognizance; his son relaxed both the terror and the fisc within six years. Either the English Tudor turn is dated to the 1530s with the dissolution as its fisc, or spine §3 is rewritten for England. This touches a ruling and is yours; the drafts' Henry VII passages in ch11 §§I–II are wrong as written whichever way you rule.

**(l) The Depreter refinement, relabelled.** The two-variable reading (offices without fiscal permanence plus one mortal node) is the book's, drawn from Depreter's evidence; it should be attributed that way, and "intact" and "guns" removed from the transfer sentence. Venice enters ch10 §VI as the fourth counter-case beside Burgundy, the Swiss and China, with two disanalogies stated: the Venetian fisc was driven by thirty years of Lombard war fought by hired companies, not by cannon, and Venetian decisive force was limited by men, not capital — the Arsenal "produced more galleys than Venice could find crews for" (Lane p. 364). "Venice became the proto-state itself" (ch04 §VII) must go.

**(m) "None was executable without it," and devolution as the fifth failure mode.** Ágoston dates and mechanises what Kafadar, İnalcık and Sharman implied in the first batch: the Ottomans had a central foundry, a salaried firearms corps and a register before any Latin monarchy, and the outcome was "military devolution" (p. 123) — prebends converted to pay a standing army in deficit from the 1590s, governors' private armies rising from a third to well over half of the timariot muster, janissary certificates sold. Ch11 §V's closing line must exempt the Ottomans; §IV must end in devolution; and Appendix B's Ottoman row now has a pinned "conversion failed" cell. This confirms and sharpens (g) above rather than adding to it.

**(n) The composite monarchy, from its source.** Elliott's 1992 essay runs the book's mechanism — war, mobilisation, union — and reports its failure: the unions attempted under fiscal pressure in the 1620s produced 1640, not 1485; there were only three successful secessions from a composite monarchy between 1523 and 1707; the eighteenth-century fiscal-military state "remained essentially composite." All four of ch11's consolidators were composites (France's *pays d'élections* and *pays d'états* included), and Wales is an accessory union executed in the decade of the print statutes. The best answer is §8(b) as you ruled it — the mechanism operates on the core and the composite is what the core rules through — but it narrows the DEFEND-tier claim: the settlement subordinates the intermediaries of the core polity, and rules the rest by contract, *aeque principaliter*. Ch12 §IV can now state the American and Chinese readings in Solórzano's two legal forms.

**(o) "Outspent" is the wrong verb for England's law.** Payling relocates the failure point of fifteenth-century justice from the sheriff to the king's pardon — "royal justice was evenhanded in its negligence" (p. 10) — and Baker shows the merchant courts absorbed by procedure (assumpsit, writ of error, the 1477 statute) inside the chapter's 1470s–1540s window but with nothing fiscal or military in sight, the incorporation unfinished in 1700. Dean, for Italy, dates the criminalisation of vendetta to the communes of c. 1300, a century before the fisc. Ch08 §VII should claim sequence, not payment: the settlement bought enforcement, not law, and for the English merchant courts the driver Baker finds is professional. Payling's Crown is not weak but unwilling — "a tolerance of violence on the part of a martial aristocracy" — and the honest line is that toleration was the price of a crown that could not pay for the alternative, which stopped tolerating when it could (Stourton, 1557).

**(p) Print was not the second capital-intensive medium.** Eisenstein and Johns agree on this against the draft from opposite sides: the hand press was cheap and everywhere, the state's harness was the canon and the register rather than the plant, the most consolidated states throttled their own print while the least consolidated polity in Europe ran the medium, and when the English crown tried to take the medium for itself by *quo warranto* in 1684 it won the case and lost the medium four years later to a change of dynasty. Johns's licensing state could not hold the number of printing houses to twenty (fifty-three in 1661–62, sixty-two by 1705), and capital intensity in print arrives with steam in the nineteenth century. The parallel for the platforms is to steam, not to Gutenberg; ch11 §V must state the disanalogy, and ch12 gains the 1670–88 *quo warranto* as the worked example of the state-absorbs-stack route attempted and reversed. The 1557 charter "chartered a guild that took enforcement, and much else, into its own hands" — a "Petit State," in Atkyns's word — not "delegated enforcement."

**(q) The papal template, per Morris.** The medieval universalism ch02 uses as its template was a jurisdiction, not a sovereignty; the Ullmann reading the chapter names as its spine is the one the field has set aside. Restated in Morris's terms — emergency powers, *casualiter*, jurisdiction over sin — the template fits the platforms better than hierocracy did. The papal fisc (1,214 gold ounces against Richard I's England; a Papal State "created on the battlefields of Sicily and Germany") is the T2 statement of the disanalogy ch02 owes: a universalism that could not pay for force. Morris's "it was easier for the papacy to assume authority than to restore it once regional powers had lost the directing initiative" (p. 113) is the ratchet stated for the other universalism, and it cuts both ways.

**(r) The Belloc syndrome, correctly framed.** The couplet is a joke about private, asymmetric possession — a chartered-company expedition's Maxim gun — and the poem's arc is the thesis's negative case: the gun holds a mutiny for a page and the expedition collapses for want of porters, ammunition, legitimacy and a fisc. Drop "prophets of the knock-out blow" from the couplet, cite Belloc for the syndrome (faith in the instrument) paired with Talbot per §8(f), and Freedman and Holman for the genealogy. One sentence's cost.

**(s) DeVries's "ratchet."** He uses the word approvingly for slow cumulative technical evolution — a terminological collision with the spine's key term that needs one defusing sentence where he is first cited. And every DeVries pin should be cited as a witness testifying against his own frame, with the frame (Hale's "centralisation can be explained without reference to gunpowder weapons") named.

**(t) 1525 was annihilation, not absorption.** Scott and Scribner's documents give the Peasants' War as a revolt against the incipient territorial state — excises, bailiffs, Roman law — defeated "with bloodthirsty ruthlessness," and the Diet of Speyer's 1526 recess as an inter-princely security compact (mutual aid "with horse and foot" for a month at the neighbours' cost), which is ch07 §VII's "shaped, not prevented" in one document; but the editors also say recent scholarship has shown the "unrestrained control" reading of the aftermath to be wrong, and the forty-seven cannon at Königshofen belong to the princes' side. The chapter should carry the compact and drop any suggestion that the commons were absorbed.

## 6. The rivals — where each must be met by name
