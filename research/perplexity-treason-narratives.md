When Defeat Is Blamed on Treason Instead of the
Enemy's Military System
Comparative cases for the collapse of English Normandy, 1449–1453
Research report — every factual claim below is sourced to a page fetched in this session and
linked to the exact URL. Unverified fields are marked n.a.

0. The baseline case (Normandy 1449–53), stated for calibration
The French "stack." From 1444 Charles VII "reorganized France," including financially, "with the
creation of a permanent tax," and created the compagnies d'ordonnance, "the first form of a
standing army in French history," so that by 1449 he could field "15,000 to 20,000 well-trained
and well-paid men" (Wikipedia, Normandy campaign of 1449–1450). The fiscal step is explicit: the
king "gradually managed to convince the États généraux to make the land tax permanent," and
"with this new source of funding" promulgated the Grande Ordonnance de Louppy-le-Château
of 26 May 1445, establishing "a permanent army made of 15 companies at the exclusive service
of the King" — "a permanent army of 9,000 men who were paid in both times of war and peace"
— plus garrison mortes-payes and, in 1448, the tax-exempt franc-archers as "the reserve of the
permanent army" (Musée de l'Armée). Artillery was the second layer: after 1420 "crumbed"
gunpowder spread and did not separate in transport; breech chambers gave a "remarkably
rapid (if brief) rate of fire"; and Jean and Gaspard Bureau "are generally credited with making
Charles VII's artillery such a formidable force," notably by regularizing its "seemingly chaotic
variety" (Osprey, The Fall of English France 1449–53, sample text). At Castillon on 17 July 1453 "the
French camp was defended by a ditch in which the artillery was positioned, hidden from the
sight of the English" (Musée de l'Armée). Operationally the effect was tempo, not spectacle: two
converging armies (French from the east, Breton from the west), Pont-Audemer on 12 August
1449, Lisieux on 16 August, then Dieppe, Fécamp, Arques, Pont-de-l'Arche, Conches, Verneuil,
Gerberoy, Coutances, Saint-Lô, Carentan (evacuated the day before the Bretons arrived),
Valognes — then Formigny on 15 April 1450, where Richemont "deployed methodically behind
the English" and Kyriell's army was "annihilated" (Wikipedia, Normandy campaign).
The English attribution. English contemporaries read this as domestic betrayal. The Kentish
rebels' Proclamation of Grievances (4 June 1450) states: "his false council has lost his law, his
merchandise is lost, his common people is destroyed, the sea is lost, France is lost, the king
himself is so set that he may not pay for his meat nor drink, and he owes more than ever any
King of England ought, for daily his traitors about him…" and demands removal of "all the false
progeny and affinity of the Duke of Suffolk… the false traitor, the Duke of Suffolk" (Fordham
Medieval Sourcebook). Suffolk's 1444 marriage negotiation contained "a secret clause [that]
returned Maine and Anjou to France," which "was to contribute to his downfall"; he was
"accused of maladministration by many," suspected of "being a traitor," arrested 28 January
1450, banished, intercepted at sea and beheaded (Wikipedia, William de la Pole). The fiscal reality

behind "unpaid garrisons" is documented: English "direct taxation had been bringing in everless cash since the mid-14th century; it had slumped since 1430 and had reached a particularly
low level in the 1450s," while Parliament had planned "to use the conquered territories in
northern France as a source of revenue, but as these slipped from English control so their
revenues declined" — and Adam Moleyns was "killed by mutinous troops in Southampton" in
1450 (Osprey sample).
The comparative question is therefore: where else did a defeated elite/public attribute a
systemic, institutionally driven collapse to treason, betrayal or moral failure?

Spanish
conquest of
Mexico/Peru,
1519–1533

The "stack" is real but not the one contemporaries named: standard colonial procedure ("acquiring a native
ally… a native interpreter… display violence… publicly seizing a native ruler"), overwhelming indigenous
manpower (final assault on Tenochtitlán "with 200,000 native allies"), disease, disunity, and the steel sword;
muskets, cannon, armour, horses and dogs were "not of great consequence… because they were in short
supply" (Wikipedia, Seven Myths of the Spanish Conquest). The omen/supernatural explanation is
post-conquest construction, not contemporary: Sahagún's students, "beginning in the 1560s and
'70s, wrote down what no indigenous person had ever said before — namely, that their forefathers had been
paralysed even before 1519 by the appearance of terrifying omens," and Fernández-Armesto "traces all the
omens to precedents in three Greek and Latin books, by Plutarch, Josephus and Lucan, as well as the Bible";
the codex itself dates the same portent to four, eight and ten years before contact in different books
(Mexicolore, "The myth of the omens"). An indigenous account written in 1570 does record technological
awe — harquebuses called "yllapas" because they "emit[ted] bolts of thunder like the sky itself," horses as
"very large animals with silver feet," Spaniards resembling viracochas (Titu Cusi Yupanqui, trans. Julien) —
but this is wonder, not a treason narrative. Use only with explicit dating; do not cite "Moctezuma
thought Cortés was a god."

Pakistan, 1971

Yahya Khan's address of 16 Dec 1971 attributed collapse to being "significantly outnumbered cut off
supplies and by land sea, and air due to the enemy's blockade," facing "a treacherous and merciless
adversary, heavily armed and supported by a superpower," and to India's "imperialist ambitions" (NYT text).
The official inquiry went the other way — toward institutional and moral self-criticism, not treason: defeat
was "not merely the result of military factors alone, but… the cumulative result of political, international,
moral and military factors," rooted in "the process of moral degeneration among the senior ranks of the
Armed Forces… set in motion by their involvement in Martial Law duties in 1958," and "The foundation of
this defeat was laid way back in 1958 when the Armed Forces took over the country" (Hamoodur Rahman
Commission Report, Dawn text). The Indian side genuinely was an integrated politico-military system — an
apex core group that "functioned very efficiently," a Secretaries Committee for war preparations, calibrated
support to the Mukti Bahini, the 9 Aug 1971 Indo-Soviet treaty, and timing chosen for closed Himalayan
passes (IDSA, Journal of Defence Studies) — but the loser partly did see it. Qualified analogue only.

C. Synthesis: when does "treason" get invoked?
1. The trigger is invisibility of cause, not scale of loss. In every high-ranking case the defeat
was produced by things with no battlefield silhouette: a permanent tax and paid garrisons
(1445–53), staff-computed march rates and a cavalry report acted on within hours (1870), a
rolling barrage plus counter-battery plus American replacements plus blockade attrition
(1918), encirclement tempo of 50 miles a day (1941), and a first-night collapse of an "integrated
whole" air-defence network (1991). Sources: Musée de l'Armée; Wikipedia, Bazaine; Saber & Scroll;
DTIC ADA312212; Journal of Strategic Studies. Where destruction was visible and attributable —
Argentine commanders watching British helicopters and carrier aviation — the inquiry
produced a materialist verdict and no treason claim (Informe Rattenbach).
2. The specific cognitive trigger is an arithmetic impossibility. Nasser's inference is the clearest
statement of the mechanism anywhere in this dossier: because the enemy struck "from the
west" and hit "all military and civilian airports" at once, it followed "that he was given facilities
that go beyond his capabilities, exceeding his calculated strength" (American Rhetoric). When the
observed output exceeds the loser's model of enemy capacity, an unseen agent — a foreign fleet,
a domestic traitor — is postulated to balance the equation. The Kentish commons perform the
same inference in 1450: the simultaneous loss of law, trade, sea and France must have a single
hidden agent, "his traitors about him" (Fordham).
3. Surrender and capitulation, not battlefield death, are what get criminalised. Bazaine (Metz),
Stoessel (Port Arthur), Kachalov/Ponedelin/Kirillov (encirclements), Suffolk (ceded Maine): all
are prosecutions of bargaining decisions taken under systemic pressure. Order No. 270 is the
limit case, converting the act of being encircled into "betrayal of their homeland" with family
arrest attached (Wikisource); the Bazaine defence names the sociology out loud — weak peoples
"cherchent une victime; ils l'immolent" (Le Droit Criminel).
4. Four distinct causal types, which the chapter should keep separate.
Genuine misunderstanding within an available worldview: Byzantine providentialism — sin,
oath-breaking, Trojan vengeance (scispace PDF).
Elite self-exculpation: Hindenburg/Ludendorff, who knew the truth on 1 Oct 1918 and
arranged for others to sign (GHDI Thaer; GHDI testimony).
Fabrication and censorship: the 1967 collusion claim, denied even by Soviet intelligence yet
carried into textbooks (zionism-israel; MEQ); Iraqi after-action reporting "in such a way as to
deflect blame" (JSS).
Later myth: the Kerkoporta/Marble Emperor cycle (Wikipedia) and the Mexica omens
invented in the 1560s–70s (Mexicolore).
5. Institutional asymmetry is the recurring blind spot, and money is its usual form. The
English case is fiscal at root — taxation "slumped since 1430" while conquered-territory revenue
evaporated (Osprey sample) — against a French crown that had converted the taille into
permanent revenue and paid troops "in both times of war and peace" (Musée de l'Armée). Its
twentieth-century analogues are Germany with "little to no reserves left" facing continuous
American reinforcement (Saber & Scroll) and Pakistan's inquiry tracing 1971 to 1958 (HRC report).

Notably, one of these — the HRC — is the counter-example that shows a defeated elite can reach
the institutional diagnosis, when the inquiry is empowered to look past the last campaign.
6. Caution the chapter must state explicitly. The "stack" story is itself vulnerable to overreading. Frieser dissolves 1940 into "the accidental coincidence of the most varied factors" (JMH
review); the leading specialist volume on 1453 concludes the fall "had little to do with
gunpowder" (BMCR); Restall dismantles the technological reading of the Spanish conquest (
Wikipedia); and Diu shows the "European gunnery revolution" reading failing outright (IESHR).
The argument should be that treason narratives and technological-determinist narratives are
both substitutes for institutional analysis.

D. Recommendation: the three best comparators for a chapter on the Artillery State
1. France 1870 — the juridical twin. It gives the full sequence in eighteen months: a fiscalindustrial-staff system converting information into movement (5 vs 15 miles a day; the 15
August patrol order), a wartime accusation of treason by the head of government ("Le
maréchal Bazaine a trahi !"), and a formal treason trial ending in a death sentence — exactly
the Suffolk/Somerset arc, with far richer documentation (Wikipedia, Bazaine; Dalloz, Gambetta;
Le Droit Criminel). It also supplies the historiographical epigram the chapter needs: strong
nations do not accuse their unlucky generals of treason; weak ones "cherchent une
victime."
2. Germany 1918 — the attritional-institutional twin, with proof of bad faith. The pairing of
the private 1 October 1918 admission with the public 18 November 1919 testimony is the
single best documented instance of an elite knowingly substituting betrayal for system
failure, and the underlying causes (manpower exhaustion, blockade-era industry, allied
collapse, combined-arms tempo at Amiens) map onto the English 1449–53 problem of
unpayable garrisons and no relief (GHDI Thaer; GHDI testimony; Saber & Scroll).
3. Byzantium 1453 — the period-adjacent siege-state twin. It is the only case within a
generation of Normandy where an artillery-and-fiscal siege system met a polity that could
not pay, and where the defeated wrote their own explanation: divine wrath for oathbreaking, internal treachery, Trojan vengeance — with the outright betrayal legends
demonstrably later (Kritovoulos; scispace PDF; BMCR; Wikipedia). Using it also disciplines the
argument, since the specialists deny gunpowder primacy.
Runner-up, if a fourth is wanted: Egypt 1967, purely as the cleanest statement of the inferential
mechanism ("facilities that go beyond his capabilities") and of how such a claim survives into
official memory (American Rhetoric; zionism-israel).
