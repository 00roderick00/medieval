# Rulings Sheet III — text (29 September 2026)

## Where the project stands

The book exists in full draft. All fourteen units — the Introduction, twelve chapters and the Coda — are written, every research memo has been reviewed by you, and the assembled manuscript stands at 97,443 words against a target of about 145,000. Two rounds of renovation have been applied since the start of September: the first carried your rulings of 2 September into the drafts, the second carried your rulings of 14 and 16 September, including the decision that consolidation is likely but its constitutional form is open. That second round is what took the manuscript from 67,000 words to 97,000. The chapters that grew most were chapter 6 on Normandy, chapter 9 on incapacity, chapter 10 on the fiscal machine, chapter 11 on the four consolidators and chapter 12 on the present.

Since 13 September, more than eighty new works have been read against the drafts: the September monographs; the contemporary rivals and primaries; a 16 September intake that brought in the load-bearing retrievals the earlier reading had asked for — Wolin, Crouch's 2004 book, Bean, Major, Youings, Holman, Leseur, Perroy and the full Tilly 1975 volume; the Burgundian and French chronicles; and most recently Jill Lepore's book on the artificial state. Every one has been assessed, entered in the relevant chapter's sources and memo, and its objections logged in the chapter's critique file. None of that reading has been allowed to touch a draft, because the rule is that nothing changes in the text until you have ruled. That is why this document exists.

The decisions that follow are the residue of that reading. In their raw form there were well over a hundred of them — seventy-four on the spreadsheet in specialist shorthand, and some forty more lettered at the foot of chapter memos by the 16 September intake and never brought onto the sheet at all. Here they have been consolidated into thirty-two, grouped by subject, and written out in ordinary language with the background, the stakes and the consequences of each direction. Some of the thirty-two are large — three would change what the book argues; several would change how it argues. Many are small. Each says which it is.

Once you have ruled, Claude Code applies the rulings in a third renovation pass, and the manuscript will then be ready for the expansion pass that takes it from 97,000 words to target. That expansion is a separate decision and is not on this sheet.

## How to read this document

Each decision has the same shape. The background sets out what the book says now, quoting the actual sentence, and what was found, naming the author and the page. Why it matters says which part of the argument is touched and what an expert reviewer would do with it. Your choices are lettered, and under each letter is what happens if you take it — what changes on the page, what it costs, what it buys. My view is a single sentence at the end, and it is a view, not a decision.

Write your answer in the yellow box under each decision — a letter, a word, or your own wording. The row numbers in italics under each heading are for bookkeeping only; they let the ruling be mapped back to the files. You do not need to understand them.

There is a summary table at the very end where you can write all the answers in one place if you prefer.

## Decision 1 — The name "ornamental democracy": whose idea it is, and how to introduce it
*Covers rows: (mm), (oooo), (llll), Check 6, and ch12 memo Revisions 29–30 (the Wolin and Crouch 2004 intake of 16 September)*

**The background.** Two weeks ago you ruled that the democratic bloc may end up "ornamentally democratic", and the phrase "ornamental democracy" is proposed as chapter 12's one coined term, with the Coda using it again. Your own voice rule, added on 1 August, says that before the chapter's footer (the short ledger at the end of each draft recording what the chapter coined) claims a term as a coinage, someone must check whether an existing scholar already owns it. That check has now been run against four books, and it is complete. The earlier version of this decision said that Sheldon Wolin's *Democracy Incorporated* had not been read and that the check against him was still open. That was wrong: he was read in full on 16 September, and the check was made at the page. What follows corrects the record.

The first predecessor is Colin Crouch, a British political sociologist. The corpus now holds two statements of his argument: the Fabian Society pamphlet *Coping with Post-Democracy* (2000), read in full, and the book *Post-Democracy* (Polity, 2004), which arrived on 16 September as a partial photocopy of about fifty-five of its hundred and twenty-seven pages (chapter 1 complete; most of chapter 2, "The Global Firm", and all of chapters 5 and 6 missing). His condition is yours in outline, twenty-five years early: "while the forms of democracy remain fully in place — and today in some respects are actually strengthened — politics and government are increasingly slipping back into the control of privileged elites" (Crouch 2004, p. 6), "Virtually all the formal components of democracy survive within post-democracy" (p. 22), and yet "it is difficult to dignify it as democracy itself" (p. 21). Every quotation chapter 12 took from the pamphlet is confirmed at a printed page of the book except two, which survive only in the pamphlet and must be labelled as such. His word sweep is clean in both texts: no "ornament", "ornamental", "façade", "hollow", "husk", "veneer", "shell" or "decorative"; his words are "spectacle", "forms", "formal components", "entropy" and "parabola". The adjective itself comes from Walter Bagehot's *The English Constitution* (1867), from which you already borrow the split between the "dignified" parts of a constitution (the ones people revere) and the "efficient" parts (the ones that govern). Bagehot uses "ornamental" exactly twice, in one passage, of "the additional and solely-ornamental wheels introduced into the clocks of the Middle Ages" (Bagehot, PDF p. 83), never of a constitution or of democracy, and he thought the separation of reverence from rule a good thing, which you do not.

The second predecessor is Wolin, the Princeton political theorist, in *Democracy Incorporated: Managed Democracy and the Specter of Inverted Totalitarianism* (2008). A search of all 375 pages found "ornament", "ornamental", "façade", "veneer", "husk" and "decorative" nowhere. For honesty the near-misses are recorded: "charade" and "shell game" of one 2006 legislative episode (pp. 77–78); "hollowed out" of the English monarchy, not of democracy (p. 96); "ceremonial figurehead" of kingship (p. 97); and one genuine near-hit, "Democratic legitimation might be defined as the ceremonial and symbolic action whereby citizens invest power with authority" (p. 147). His own phrases are "managed democracy", "a political form in which governments are legitimated by elections that they have learned to control" (p. 47), "dissociated democracy", in which "the people reigned but did not rule" (pp. 228–29), "a rhetorical democracy" (p. 218) and "a bogus political democracy" (p. 273). More important than the words is the object. His is a regime type, "a new type of political system" (p. 44), covering the economy, culture, religion and the press, corporate and cultural in its mechanism, and offered by him as "tentative, hypothetical" (p. xvi) with no test by which it could be shown false. Yours is a measurable relation, confined to the state's strategic commitments and the decisive stack, with four published falsifiers in Appendix C, arising from a fiscal-military mechanism. A bloc could score ornamental on your test while his other indices improved, and democratic on yours while remaining, on his account, inverted-totalitarian. He is a rival diagnosis of the same symptoms, not the owner of the term. He also supplies, in his own vocabulary, two things the chapter can use: the restriction to strategic commitments, "the most substantial element of state power is removed from public debate" (p. 199), and a word for absorption by alliance, "predomination — that is, rule by diverse powers which have found it in their interests to combine while retaining their separate identities" (p. 185).

The third predecessor is Jill Lepore, the Harvard historian, in *The Rise and Fall of the Artificial State* (a phone-scanned copy with no title or copyright page, so no publisher or year may be cited and every page number is provisional). A search of all 306 pages found "ornament", "ornamental", "façade", "hollow", "husk", "decorative", "charade" and "sham" nowhere; the nearest hits are "trappings" (of religion), "veneer" (of Nick Bostrom's prose) and one "figurehead", her summary of Douglas Adams's Zaphod Beeblebrox (printed p. 210 / PDF p. 224). Her concept is "government without consent" (printed p. 5 / PDF p. 15), which asks whether the governed chose; yours asks whether elected institutions control the state's strategic commitments, a different test and compatible with genuine elections. Her "artificial state" runs the opposite way to yours: "Democracy and the liberal nation-state had to be abolished to free corporations from the restraining force of any government" (printed p. 205 / PDF p. 219), corporations dissolving the state where your state absorbs the corporations. The Lepore check of 28 September was the third run of the ledger and it re-confirmed what the 16 September run found.

**Why it matters.** The question has changed. Two weeks ago it was whether the coinage could be claimed at all; with all three predecessors read, the verdict is that it can, and the question is now on what conditions. It remains a large point because it sits at the book's rhetorical climax. A hostile reviewer who knows the post-democracy literature will treat an unattributed "ornamental democracy" as Crouch rebranded, and a reviewer who has read Wolin will say that the American separation of strategic control from electoral control was diagnosed in 2008, under a different mechanism, and the book has renamed it. That second objection is the serious one, and it is not about the word. Wolin dates the condition from the Cold War: "A 'defense establishment,' comprising the economy, the military, and the state, came into being" (p. 28); "The Cold War consolidated the power of capital … What was abandoned was all talk of participatory democracy" (p. 26). If the separation was achieved by 1950 without a compute stack, the mechanism explains nothing new and the 2020s indicators measure a sixty-year-old condition. The book's best answer is that Wolin's state sheds capability, "Corporate expansion extends to military functions, a province once jealously guarded as a state prerogative" (p. 136), and that the mechanism predicts the reversal of that trajectory when a supplier can no longer be treated as a contractor. But the honest part of the answer is a concession: the ornamental route is not an emerging condition but a deepening one, and what the mechanism predicts is not its arrival but its irreversibility, the point at which the state's dependence on the decisive layer can no longer be escaped by re-tendering. Narrower than the draft now claims, and more defensible.

**Your choices.** Choice A keeps the coinage on conditions: at first use in chapter 12 you introduce the term in the same breath as its pedigree, and the footer ledger claims it, no longer provisionally, at a count of one. The conditions are the sub-choices below. This costs perhaps 300 to 400 words at the ending's most loaded point and a narrowing of the claim. It buys a reviewer who sees command of the field, and it turns Wolin from a threat into the book's own unfavourable prior, the hypothesis the falsifiers are built to beat.

Choice B is the bare coinage: you introduce "ornamental democracy" as your own term with no genealogy and no concession. It reads cleaner and costs nothing in words. It buys a reviewer who says, correctly, that you renamed post-democracy, and who then produces Wolin's page 28 and asks why a book about the state absorbing the stack did not notice that the separation it predicts was diagnosed a generation before the stack existed.

Choice C drops the term: the ending says "post-democracy, restricted to strategic commitments", the Wolin concession is still made once, and the chapter's one coinage is spent elsewhere. This costs the sharpest label in Part III and some of the close's force; it buys complete safety on originality. A reviewer would call the book honest and slightly flat.

Inside Choice A there are five conditions, each a sub-choice, recorded in ch12 memo Revisions 29–30. The first is that the term be introduced as a special case of post-democracy, not as Crouch's concept renamed, with the four Crouch quotations re-pinned to the 2004 book (pp. 5, 6, 21, 22) and the two pamphlet-only sentences kept at the pamphlet and labelled as the Fabian typescript; the alternative is to keep citing the pamphlet throughout, which is a lower tier of source when the book is to hand. The second is the adjective clause: one sentence saying the adjective occurs in neither Crouch nor Wolin, and in Bagehot only of clock-wheels, and refusing Bagehot's valuation; this is now checkable against three texts and costs a sentence. The third is that the draft's present sentence, which names Wolin "here and not cited, because it is not in the corpus", together with its [GAP] bracket, be replaced by a rival-diagnosis sentence carrying three things: that he is a rival and not a parent, that his object is the regime where yours is the commitment, and that his diagnosis is offered as hypothesis where yours publishes its falsifiers. The draft currently names Crouch and Wolin "in one breath" as a pair of allies; they are not a pair, and the text must say which is which. The fourth is the dating concession, in one clause and not a paragraph: that in the American case the separation of strategic commitments from electoral control predates the stack, and that what the mechanism predicts is irreversibility, not arrival. You can decline this and keep the stronger claim; if you do, Wolin's page 28 is the first thing a reviewer will quote. The fifth is whether to credit "predomination" (Wolin, p. 185) where the present indicator is stated, in §IV's fusion paragraph or in §VII, as his word and not adopted as the book's, and whether it should replace "fusion" as the name of spine §8(g)(3)'s indicator, since it describes exactly the American case the indicator watches: parties that combine and keep their identities. Crouch's 2004 book offers a competing figure for the same thing, the party leadership stretched into "an ellipse stretching way out beyond the party's ranks" (p. 73), and his own verdict on the fusion, "This is the central crisis of early twenty-first-century democracy" (p. 52). Either can stand beside the other; the choice is whether the indicator keeps the book's word or takes a predecessor's.

There is one further sub-choice, carried over. The Lepore intake wants the whole predecessor clause in chapter 12 §VII; Decision 2 may move the constitutional close to a different section. Wherever the close lands, the predecessor clause, the Wolin concession and the Lepore collision sentence go with it. And one caution for the record: the Crouch pins now rest on the pamphlet plus a partial book, and the sharpest Crouch passages the critiques file relies on ("institutional idiot"; "impossible to see any major reversal") lie in the missing chapters, so they stay cited to the pamphlet until the rest of the 2004 book is retrieved.

**My view.** A, with all five conditions, and this is a view: the ledger check is closed in the coinage's favour on three separate runs, so the word is yours, but the claim it names is only as strong as the dating concession makes it, and I would rather the book said "irreversibility, not arrival" than had a reviewer say it first.

## Decision 2 — Where the book's real ending lives in chapter 12
*Covers rows: (nn)*

**The background.** Chapter 12 as drafted runs: §VI states the book's claims; §VII is the asset-class ledger; §VIII returns to the battle of Formigny and gives the book its last words. §VII opens with its own disclaimer, "*(The CLAUDE.md §9 boundary governs this section: comparative historical analysis and asset-class direction; no actionable guidance.)*", and continues, "What did the last Tudor settlement do to wealth? The ledger is stark and it is the only forecast instrument this book will hand its professional readers." It closes: "Nothing more specific is offered, because nothing more specific would be honest."

Your ruling two weeks ago split the book's third claim into two: a defended part (the barons will be subordinated) and an open, scored part (what constitutional form the consolidated state takes, which may be ornamental democracy). That split needs a home in chapter 12, and every recent assessment nominates §VII for it, because that is where the chapter's closing argument sits. But §VII cannot take it. The project's own rules confine that section to comparative history and asset-class direction, and a section fenced for a wealth ledger is the wrong register for an argument about legitimacy and control. The Bagehot assessment says so plainly: "§VII as drafted is the asset-class ledger and cannot carry it; a new section or a renumbering is Roderick's call." Decision 1's predecessor clause and title-collision sentence must go wherever the constitutional close goes.

**Why it matters.** This decides what readers remember the book as having argued. As things stand, the last substantive thing said before the Formigny return is a wealth ledger for professional readers. A book that ends on asset-class direction invites the reading that its historical apparatus was built to service a market view. Putting the constitutional claim last inverts that impression at no cost to the ledger's content. It is a structural point rather than an evidentiary one, but it is large.

**Your choices.** Choice A inserts a new section between §VI and §VII: the constitutional close becomes the new §VII, carrying Crouch, Bagehot, Wolin, Lepore, Stasavage's Cortes of Castile and the falsifiers; the ledger slides to §VIII and the Formigny return to §IX. This costs one renumbering across the chapter and every cross-reference to "ch12 §VII" elsewhere, including Appendix C, and it lengthens the run to the final image by 700 to 900 words. It buys a clean order: claims, then the form question, then the ledger as a consequence, then the return.

Choice B is the same insertion but with the ledger moved up to sit directly after §VI, so the constitutional close is the last analytical section before Formigny. The renumbering is the same. The cost is that the ledger, with its disclaimer, interrupts the line between the claims and the close. It buys the strongest possible final emphasis.

Choice C moves the ledger out of the chapter into a short Appendix E, or folds it into Appendix B, and the constitutional close takes the §VII slot outright. This costs the ledger its prominence, and it was written for a readership whose attention you presumably want; it risks reading as embarrassment about the book's commercial audience. It buys the tightest chapter and the least friction with the rules, since an appendix carrying asset-class direction is unambiguously outside the argumentative spine.

**My view.** B, as a view: the ledger was written honestly for a real audience and should not be hidden, but the constitutional close, not the wealth ledger, should be the last analytical thing the reader meets.

## Decision 3 — The Coda's fifth disclaimer
*Covers rows: (cccc)*

**The background.** Coda §V reads: "Four disclaimers, stated flatly because each has been earned by a chapter." Your ruling that the consolidated state's constitutional form is open, and may be ornamental, has not yet reached the Coda. The row proposes a fifth disclaimer: the settlement is not claimed to be democratic. Crouch supplies the condition without the slur (the forms remain "fully in place" while control slips elsewhere, Crouch PDF p. 2); Bagehot supplies the instrument while refusing your valuation of it, since for him the dignified parts "raise the army, though they do not win the battle" (Bagehot PDF p. 21); David Stasavage, an American political scientist writing on representative assemblies, supplies the worked case, the Cortes of Castile keeping its forms while losing control and being abolished in 1665.

**Why it matters.** Small in words, large in effect. A book that predicts consolidation and declines to condemn it reads as commending it, and the Coda is the last place a reader looks for the distinction. Without the disclaimer, a reviewer can say the book's ending is complacent about democracy; with it, the two things you ruled apart are visibly kept apart at the end as well as the beginning.

**Your choices.** Choice A adds the fifth disclaimer, naming Crouch, Bagehot and Stasavage in one sentence each, with "ornamental democracy" appearing only as a back-reference to chapter 12, so that the coinage is introduced once and only once. This costs perhaps eighty words and depends on Decision 1 going A or C. Choice B leaves four disclaimers and lets chapter 12 carry the point alone. This costs nothing on the page and leaves the Coda's own summary of the wager silent on its most contestable implication.

**My view.** A, as a view: it is cheap, and the Coda is precisely where the sceptic checks what the author does and does not claim.

## Decision 4 — Conceding when the state does NOT acquire the stack: "decisive AND inescapable"
*Covers rows: (pppp)*

**The background.** The book's fiscal limb says that once a capability becomes decisive and costs more than any subject can pay, the state with a permanent fisc acquires it. Chapter 12 §IV applies that to the present and rules, for the United States, that "the state will absorb the stack". Your spine §8(g) already lists this as item 4, a limb that may need narrowing.

Lepore's historical chapters, which rest on her own archival work and carry more weight than her contemporary ones, hold three dated cases in which a state wanted a data-processing stack, had the money, and did not get it. The American National Data Center, proposed in the 1960s, was "abandoned due to privacy concerns" and dubbed "the Snooping Machine". The Soviet OGAS, a planned nationwide computer network for running the economy, failed because "the process of developing the network was left to unregulated, independent contractors" (both at printed p. 59 / PDF p. 70). Chile's Cybersyn, Salvador Allende's real-time economic control room, was "abandoned after Pinochet took power in a coup in 1973" (printed p. 62 / PDF p. 73). In each case the state had the fiscal capacity; in each case private firms went on to build what the state could not. The assessment grades the book's present answer "good on the sequence, weak on the three failures", and recommends a concession in one clause: the mechanism predicts acquisition only where the capability is decisive AND the state's dependence on it inescapable. In 1960s data-processing neither held; the state could govern without it, and nothing decisive turned on it.

**Why it matters.** This touches the predictive claim directly, so it is large. Left alone, a reviewer with Lepore's chapter 3 in hand has three counter-examples to a limb the book states as a rule, and can argue the American state has a documented habit of abandoning exactly this kind of stack. Conceded, the same cases become the boundary of the claim, and the book's own falsifier sheet inherits a new obligation: Appendix C's indicator DC-4, which asks whether a public institution exists that could replace an essential supplier, must now show that present dependence is inescapable, not merely assert it. Lepore also names no such public substitute anywhere in her book, and that zero is worth recording in the DC-4 design note.

**Your choices.** Choice A adopts the concession: §IV gains one clause at the point where the fiscal half is stated ("decisive and inescapable"), the three failures are named in a note rather than the text, and DC-4's design note records that the indicator must demonstrate inescapability and that Lepore supplies no public substitute. This costs a clause, a note and a paragraph in the appendix. It buys a narrower claim that survives its own counter-examples, and it turns Lepore's three failures into the book's own evidence for why the present case is different. A reviewer would then have to argue that the present dependence is escapable, which is an argument on your ground.

Choice B keeps the fiscal limb as stated and treats the three failures as answered by sequence alone, the point your spine §8(c)(i) already makes, that abdication and absorption are consecutive rather than alternative. This costs nothing on the page. It buys a cleaner rule and leaves three named historical cases contradicting it, discoverable by anyone who reads a Pulitzer-winning historian's latest book.

**My view.** A, as a view: the concession is already true on the book's own account, it costs one clause, and the obligation it places on DC-4 is one the indicator should have carried anyway.

## Decision 5 — Where the modern analogy stops: administration yes, representation no
*Covers rows: (mmmm)*

**The background.** Chapter 12 §III compares the identity-payments-legibility stack to two medieval and early-modern instruments of making a population visible to the state: the Spanish Inquisition's records and the Ottoman devşirme (the levy of Christian boys for imperial service, which required registers of who lived where). The draft's sentence reads: "the comparison licenses nothing beyond the observation that uniform legibility is a governing *capacity* — the capacity the Inquisition and the devşirme built by force and the API builds by default [ANALOGY-ONLY beyond that point]". The flag is a project marker meaning that a passage rests on resemblance rather than mechanism and needs your decision; the draft currently leaves it open with a bare "beyond that point", which states no limit.

Lepore supplies the limit. She draws a line three times between technologies that assist government and systems that replace it. The load-bearing sentence is on Cold War computing, which served "government administration of everything from welfare provision to national security. Only later would these tools be applied, by private companies, as substitutes for the democratic functions of representation, deliberation, and participation" (printed p. 38 / PDF p. 49). Her two-sided formula follows: "The ability to count gave the state power; the ability to be counted gave the people power" (printed p. 94 / PDF p. 107). The line is administration against representation: machines may administer; they may not represent.

The same distinction answers a question the rest of chapter 12 leaves hanging. §IV rules that the American state will absorb the stack, and your ruling made the constitutional form of what results an open question (claim 3(ii)). Lepore's line gives that question its criterion: not whether the state acquires the stack, but whether what it acquires is administrative capacity or the representative function itself. The identity-payments-legibility stack straddles the line, which is why the question is live. The same criterion would enter chapter 9 §IX, which asks "what happens to a constitution when its efficient part migrates".

**Why it matters.** It is a medium-sized point on the page and a large one in the argument, because the flag marks the one place in Part III where the book itself admits it may be arguing from resemblance. Left as "beyond that point", a reviewer says the author knew the analogy was thin and did not say where it stopped. Narrowed, the flag becomes a stated limit with two pins, and the limit does double duty as the criterion for the open half of the third claim. Note the narrowing is not a closure: Lepore gives a principled stopping line but no mechanism for how episodic legibility (a census) became continuous legibility (a platform), so the flag stays, narrowed.

**Your choices.** Choice A narrows the flag: "beyond that point" is replaced by a stated limit (the comparison carries the administrative capacity and its two-sidedness and stops at the representative function), with Lepore's two sentences in the note; §IV gains one sentence making the same distinction the criterion for claim 3(ii); chapter 9 §IX's close gains the same criterion for what the efficient part's migration would consist in. This costs three sentences across two chapters. It buys a stated boundary, a criterion for your own open question, and the removal of the draft's most visible hedge. Lepore carries only the formula; the evidential weight stays on the primary documents already in the sources (the tax-information exchange regimes, Aadhaar, the GENIUS Act, the e-CNY).

Choice B leaves the flag as it is, for you to resolve at the bridge rewrite. This costs nothing now and keeps the analogy joints yours, as the doctrine says they are. It leaves the book's own admission of thinness standing unresolved through another pass, and forgoes a criterion that §IV and chapter 9 both need.

**My view.** A, as a view: the limit is principled, the two pins are clean, and the criterion is one the book needs regardless of where the analogy stops.

## Decision 6 — The trust-busting sentence and the deregulation record
*Covers rows: (nnnn)*

**The background.** Chapter 12 §IV describes the American state's instinct towards the platforms: "and its present instinct, when it reaches for them, is usually described — sometimes by its own officials — in the vocabulary of the trust-busting age, as though the platforms were its Rockefeller moment and the remedy a dramatic dissolution". Lepore's record of 2023 to 2026 runs the other way: the industry used the race with China "to defeat, among other initiatives, antitrust [reform] … Protection law, and an AI Accountability law" (printed p. 107 / PDF p. 121), and the 2025 AI Action Plan relieved "all AI infrastructure projects of the burdens of environmental review" (printed p. 222 / PDF p. 238). A warning: the page 107 sentence comes from a sheared phone-scan page and has been reconstructed; it is marked pending verification and may not be quoted until the page image is read. The page 222 pin is clean.

**Why it matters.** A small point, but a factual one on a contemporary claim, and the project's rules require contemporary claims to rest on primary documents. As drafted, the sentence describes one strand of the American response as if it were the strand. A reviewer who has read Lepore, or the newspapers, will note that the same years saw antitrust reform defeated and infrastructure deregulated, and will ask why the book noticed only the trust-busters.

**Your choices.** Choice A keeps the sentence and adds the counter-instinct: the deregulatory record of 2023 to 2026 is stated as the other strand, sourced to the Action Plan itself rather than to Lepore, so the trust-busting sentence reads as one strand and not the strand. This costs a clause and a primary citation. It buys a sentence that survives the record. Choice B leaves the sentence as it stands. This costs nothing and leaves an easy correction available to any reviewer.

**My view.** A, as a view: the correction is cheap and the page 222 primary is already identified; only the page 107 quotation should wait for verification.

## Decision 7 — Which half of the reassertion has already happened: the "layered tense"
*Covers rows: (ll), (eeee) and (vvv)*

**The background.** The book's contemporary wager is written in a single tense: the state's reassertion over the tech barons lies ahead. Chapter 12 §III says of the modern state, "Its legibility is infrastructural and persistent"; §IV says "the state will absorb the stack"; chapter 1 §V says "The disaggregated state was not the destination"; chapter 8 §VII's modern bridge calls the present reassertion "early in its arc"; and Coda §I says the mechanism "operated once, in the documented case Part II reconstructs", and then states the present-day wager in the future tense. Your spine §7 rules that the reassertion is compelled but not accomplished.

Two works now in the corpus show that half of it was accomplished twenty years ago, and cheaply. Jack Goldsmith and Tim Wu, two American law professors, in *Who Controls the Internet?* (Oxford, 2006), documented how states took back the internet's legibility layer between 2000 and 2006. Legibility, in the book's sense, means the state's ability to see who is who and what is where; on the internet it covers identity, payments, content and the "canon", the book's word for which rules count. Their point is that no fiscal threshold was crossed to do it. "Government regulation works by cost and bother, not by hermetic seal" (p. 68). Internet service providers were "already some of the most regulated companies on earth" (p. 73). When the United States wanted to stop online cigarette sales, the Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco and Firearms "simply ordered Visa, MasterCard, and AmEx to stop taking online cigarette orders… And that is how, without ever laying a finger on online sellers, the government can impose its power, often without even needing to go to court" (pp. 76–77). Filtering costs nothing because "the router itself has become the censor" (p. 94). The outcome is stated as done: "what we once called a global network is becoming a collection of nation-state networks" (p. 149), and "physical coercion by government—the hallmark of a traditional legal system—remains far more important than anyone expected" (p. 180). Henry Farrell and Abraham Newman, in their 2019 article "Weaponized Interdependence", explain how the state came by those levers: "The asymmetric networks that make up much of the structure of a globalized world were not constructed as tools of statecraft" (2019: 54); states inherited them by jurisdictional accident, without a penny of new taxation.

Put plainly: the book's mechanism says the state gets control of a decisive capability because only a permanent fisc can pay for it. On the identity-payments-legibility stack, the state got control by sending letters to intermediaries. That is not the mechanism. It is a rival explanation for half of what the book predicts, and it explains events that took place before the book says its mechanism started running.

**Why it matters.** This is the largest register change on the sheet, and it touches spine §7 and sentences in four chapters and the Coda. Left alone, a reviewer holds up a 2006 monograph showing half the predicted reassertion already complete, without any fiscal threshold, and says the book did not notice. That puts the wager's calibration in question, not just a sentence. The honest response is not a reversal but a narrowing. The mechanism's claim over the legibility stack is confined to two things it can defend: scale (Goldsmith and Wu themselves note that intermediary control "is generally less effective in small nations", pp. 81–82) and fusion, the point at which the legibility layer is joined to the chokepoint layer, the compute, chips, satellites and munitions where the fiscal cost begins. The state could mandate the canon by letter; holding the plant costs money, and that is where the book's mechanism starts. Saying so requires the book to speak in two tenses at once: the past tense for canon and register, accomplished between 2000 and 2006 by cheap legal coercion of local intermediaries, and the present-conditional for the decisive stack, compelled and unaccomplished. That is the layered tense.

**Your choices.** Choice A adopts the layered tense throughout. The affected sentences in chapter 12 §§III–IV, chapter 1 §V and chapter 2 §§III and VI are recast; spine §7 gains a register amendment; chapter 8 §VII replaces "early in its arc" with a sentence that puts the early arc where it belongs, the hierarchical ranking of fora since 2018, above an intermediary coercion finished by 2006; and Coda §I gains a clause conceding that the legibility stack was substantially re-territorialised by ordinary law before the mechanism ran, that the mechanism's claim over it is confined to scale and fusion, and that the fiscal threshold is therefore defended on the decisive military stack alone. The cost is real: perhaps a dozen sentences across four chapters, 120 to 180 words in the book's most compressed section, and the surrender of a satisfying single tense. It buys the strongest possible position against a reviewer who knows this literature, because the concession is made in your own voice and turns Goldsmith and Wu from rival into the fullest documentation of the state taking the cheap half first. It also makes the book more interesting: a mechanism that explains why the state must move from mandating the canon to holding the plant is a better book than one that treats both as pending.

Choice B adopts the layered tense in the chapters but keeps the Coda's present tenses, on the ground that a method note is the wrong place to relitigate evidence. This saves the Coda's economy, which is worth something. It leaves the book's own summary of its claim, strictly read, behind the record on half the stack it claims, and a sceptical reader looks in the Coda for exactly that summary.

Choice C keeps the single unaccomplished tense everywhere. Nothing is rewritten and the forward-leaning register survives intact. The cost is the reviewer with the 2006 monograph, and the inference that follows, that the book's contemporary reading is thinner than its medieval one.

There is one sub-choice inside A. Row (eeee) also proposes that Coda §I read the artillery ratchet as its French form, with Jan Glete's early-modern navies named as a second form of the same capital-intensity effect, since navies were state monopolies from the start without any wall being broken (Glete, p. 39). That is a separate concession, cheap, and it makes the mechanism broader rather than narrower; it can be taken with A or B.

**My view.** A, as a view: the two-tense position is both the more honest and the more interesting claim, and the Coda is where the sceptic will look for it, so it should not be left to chapter 12 alone.
## Decision 8 — England: the 1530s or the 1690s
*Covers rows: (ee), Check 3, and Y71-a–g (the Youings intake of 16 September)*

**The background.** A fortnight ago you ruled that England's Tudor turn happened in the 1530s, and that the dissolution of the monasteries was the money behind it — in the book's shorthand, its "fisc", meaning the revenue a crown could count on without asking anyone's leave. Chapter 11, section II, now says so in two sentences: "The settlement arrived when the crown found a fisc that no assembly had to grant", and "The dissolution is the English fisc of the settlement." Behind both sits the book's spine, the short doctrine document that states the argument's fixed points; its section 3 says that "standing taxation" was "established" as part of the settlement, and for England that sentence currently reads at the 1530s. The earlier version of this decision treated Thomas Ertman as the only new evidence and said that the two English historians who could settle the fiscal question, Joyce Youings and Richard Hoyle, were not yet in the reading. Youings has been read since, in full, on 16 September, and she settles more than the earlier version expected.

Ertman first. An American political scientist, he published *Birth of the Leviathan* (Cambridge, 1997) to explain why European states formed differently from one another, and his variable is the administration: whether office was held by salaried servants or by men who had bought their posts and treated them as property, which he calls patrimonialism. His section on the decades you ruled for is titled "Deepening Patrimonialism and Its Temporary Demise, 1453–1659" (p. 178). He grants that Thomas Cromwell's new revenue boards of the 1530s "did represent a serious challenge to the Exchequer, that bastion of proprietary officeholding", and then takes it back: "Yet by 1554 the most important of these bodies … had all been incorporated into the Exchequer", because of "the absence of war and thus of the incentives it would have created for military effectiveness" (p. 181). His English fiscal-military state — the term for a state built to raise permanent taxes for a permanent army — belongs to 1660–1714, made by a Parliament sitting every year under the pressure of war (p. 187). The dissolution does not appear in his account of England at all. The assessment of him was blunt: his test is proprietary office, so a permanent confiscation that ended a rival universal institution is invisible to it by construction, and his "little large-scale warfare" quietly skips the 1540s.

Now Youings. *The Dissolution of the Monasteries* (Allen & Unwin, 1971) is the standard account of the operation, and she treats it as a property transaction: "an act of resumption, a restoration to secular uses of land and other endowments provided over many centuries, very largely by laymen" (ch. 1). One caution about citing her: the corpus copy is an electronic reissue that preserves no printed page numbers, so every pin below is to a chapter, and page-image verification is owed before print. She confirms your ruling more strongly than the draft dared. The confiscation "had just about doubled the Crown's resources", and the Court of Augmentations, the new department built to hold the land, "replaced the king's chamber as the bank from which the king's government, and his personal extravagances, could be paid for" (ch. 4). The 1538–39 surrenders ran on commissions that "had no statutory authority" (ch. 3), with no Parliament even planned; the Church was subordinated by paper, "Certainly it was not with the use of armed force" (ch. 1). And she supplies the instruments, which is what the chapter's flagged sentence about a "universalist power" needed: the 1535 Injunctions loosed every religious from obedience to Rome "or to any other foreign power or person" (Doc. 7), and the communities "were never, in so many words, actually dissolved … Institutions which for the most part traced their foundation to gifts of landed property came to an end with the surrender of that property" (ch. 1). The fiscal act was the canonical act.

Then she takes the word "standing" away. "It has long been recognized that the Dissolution did not lead to the permanent augmentation of the annual income of the Crown" (ch. 1). The intention was permanence: the Augmentations act "is the strongest possible argument for the government's determination … to retain its new resources permanently in its own hands" (ch. 2). The outcome was not: "Disposal by the Crown took place from the very beginning" (ch. 3); Cromwell himself was named a sales commissioner in December 1539; land sold "at twenty years' purchase, that is a capital sum of twenty times their current net annual value" (ch. 5); outright grants ran fifteen, thirty-five, seventy-five and a hundred and twelve across 1536–39; and "by the end of Henry VIII's reign over half of the whole of the former monastic estates had been alienated … well over three-quarters by 1558" (ch. 5). The department built to hold the estate spent its life disposing of it and was folded into the Exchequer in 1554. Put plainly: the crown took a stock, not a flow, and sold it at a fixed price to the county families who would fill the next century's Parliaments. She and Ertman converge from two directions — his boards reabsorbed by 1554, her revenue by 1558 — and neither was writing with the other's argument in mind. Two smaller things she adds: the houses that died in 1536 were chosen by the *Valor Ecclesiasticus* of January 1535, a survey with "no justification whatsoever for regarding it as anything more than a taxation assessment" (ch. 2), which is your legibility point in one document; and the money went to fortification, coastal-defence works at Weymouth and Falmouth and garrison wages, but there is no artillery anywhere in her book.

**Why it matters.** This is a large point, because it touches the spine, a ruling you have already made, and the book's own word. The mechanism says weapon cost forces permanent taxation, and permanent taxation builds the administration that subordinates the lords. Youings shows the English crown taking the right asset in the wrong form: France's taille could be levied again every year and could not be spent out; the monastic estate was capital and was. A hostile early-modernist now has two authorities, not one, saying the 1530s produced no standing revenue, and the second is the standard account of the very operation you named as the fisc. The exposure is still one sentence in the spine and two in Chapter 11, but they are sentences the argument leans on. The gain, if you take it, is that the English case becomes the book's clearest demonstration of which link in the chain is load-bearing: permanence of finance, not size of seizure. That reading is the book's inference, not hers, and must be flagged as such. The intake also recorded one objection not yet answered: the gentry who bought the land at twenty years' purchase were the beneficiaries of the windfall, and the chapter does not yet say what that did to the subordination the settlement is supposed to have achieved.

**Your choices.** Choice A is "fisc without permanence": keep the 1530s as England's turn, but reclassify what happened. The dissolution becomes the irreversible confiscation that made the crown the only universal authority in England — a change in who held jurisdiction and property, not in who paid annual tax — and the standing-taxation limb moves to where Ertman and John Brewer put it, the 1690s. Chapter 11 §II gets a recast paragraph and a sentence pointing forward a century and a half; spine §3 gains a line saying that for England the two links arrived separately. It costs a tidy single date and obliges you to say the English case took two stages. It buys a sentence no early-modernist will contest, with Youings now cited for both halves of it.

Choice B keeps the ruling as it stands and names Ertman and Youings as a stated disanalogy — the book's word for a place where the historical case and the modern one are admitted to differ. Chapter 11 §II would carry perhaps two hundred words conceding that the leading authority on administrative development reads the 1530s as a deepening of patrimonialism and that the standard historian of the dissolution reads it as a dissipated windfall, and answering with the 1540s wars, the blindness of Ertman's test to confiscation, and the fact that your turn is about canon and jurisdiction rather than revenue. Nothing moves in the spine. This is cheaper and keeps the Tudor turn tidy. But "standing" stays attached to a receipt that the standard account says was sold off within a generation, and that is exactly where the reviewer will push.

Whichever you take, the Youings intake put three readings of ruling (k) to you and adopted none; they are the sub-choice inside the qualifying clause that Chapter 11 §II now owes (Y71-a), and they imply different readings of spine §3's "standing taxation" for England. The first reads the mechanism as predicting the crown's requirement and its attempt, not the durability of what it got, so that Youings supplies the attempt in its strongest form and the sentence stands with "standing" struck. The second dates the fisc by the moment of acquisition — the Augmentations of 1536 — rather than by how long the crown held it, which keeps the 1530s but concedes the flow in a clause. The third states the stock-versus-flow distinction in the text as the English case's own disanalogy, which turns §II's weakest passage into the sharpest illustration the mechanism has of why permanent finance is the load-bearing link. The third is the boldest and is the one Choice A rests on; the first is compatible with either choice; the second is Choice B in a milder form.

Six smaller sub-choices ride with it. Whether the chapter's flagged sentence on the "universalist power" is recast to rest on effect and instrument rather than motive, since Youings denies the anti-papal motive but her documents supply the instruments, and whether "executed in England as a single act" becomes one apparatus in one decade (Y71-b). Whether the stock-versus-flow distinction goes into the text or stays in the memo (Y71-c), which is really the same question as the three readings. Whether the *Valor* enters §II as one clause, a tax register of January 1535 deciding which houses died in 1536 (Y71-d). Whether the 1538 invasion scare and the coastal-defence warrants enter §II at all, given that her own footnote denies any financial crisis before 1540 and that the passage would claim fortification and never guns (Y71-e). Whether a residual flag on crown ordinary revenue before and after the dissolution is opened, with Hoyle on the retrieval list, or the comparison is dropped, since Youings insists no balance sheet of the operation has ever been drawn and refuses to supply one (Y71-f). And a caution for the record rather than a choice: her book is a volume in Geoffrey Elton's own series, read in draft by Elton, and the project's rules forbid using it as independent corroboration of the Elton frame in the passages where the book engages the Elton-revision debate (Y71-g).

**My view.** Choice A with the third reading, and this is a view: the earlier version of this decision said it would not let "standing taxation" stand against the 1530s until Youings or Hoyle was in the reading; Youings is, and she says it was not standing. Choice A is the only reading that turns that into a finding rather than a retreat.

## Decision 9 — Three smaller England points
*Covers rows: (hhh), (jjj), (iiii)*

**The background.** Three sentences in the English chapters have each drawn one comment from the September reading. Chapter 5, section VI, says "The Wars of the Roses were fought by private armies". Chapter 5, section II, says the council that governed during Henry VI's childhood "governed … remarkably well". Chapter 9, section III, describes the 1422 minority arrangement as designed and as having worked, and then says the fifteenth century "ran the regency experiment perhaps half a dozen times" and that every run returned the same result.

On "private armies", Ertman uses the same phrase without embarrassment: "the power of the great magnates with their regional strongholds and private armies was broken during the Wars of the Roses and their aftermath" (p. 179). The proposed alternative is "retinues", a retinue being a lord's following of retained men — a relationship, not a standing force. That distinction is attributed to J. R. Lander, but Lander is not among the works assessed in September, so his pages cannot be checked and the weight of his objection cannot be judged from what is in front of us.

On the minority council and the regency runs, the comment comes from Charles Tilly, the American sociologist whose account of war making and state making the book cites more than any other. In his 1985 essay "War Making and State Making as Organized Crime" he writes that lords who could raise troops and keep armed retainers were at once the king's instrument and his rivals, and "For that reason, before the seventeenth century, regencies for child sovereigns reliably produced civil wars". That is a claim about the whole period, not about England, so it does not refute Chapter 5; it makes the English council an exception the chapter should know it is claiming. For Chapter 9 the same sentence bites harder, because "every run returned the same result" turns a handful of episodes into a rule, and Tilly's rule points the other way. The pages that would test the 1425 crisis — Ralph Griffiths on Henry VI and Lander again — are cited in the old row but were not assessed, so I can confirm the citations exist but not what they say.

**Why it matters.** These are small points individually, but they are the kind a reviewer collects: a loose phrase, a judgement that looks naive beside the standard comparative statement, and a generalisation from six cases. None touches the mechanism. The third is the most serious, because a sentence that claims regularity from a small count is the easiest thing in a history book to knock down.

**Your choices.** On "private armies", Choice A keeps the phrase, citing Ertman in the text as the surviving scholarly usage, with Lander's objection in a note; nothing changes on the page except a citation, and the chapter is shown to know the difficulty. Choice B rewrites to "retinues" with a gloss for the general reader; it is more exact and slightly less vivid, and it should not be settled until Lander has been read at his pages. On the minority council, there is really one choice: add half a sentence naming Tilly's rule and saying the English council governed well against it. That costs almost nothing and makes the exception interesting rather than naive. On the regency runs, Choice A hedges both sentences — "designed, and it worked" qualified by the 1425 crisis, "every run returned the same result" replaced by a narrower claim — and names Tilly where the second is hedged. The paragraph loses some snap and gains a distinction worth having: a minority was an office temporarily filled by a council, an incapacity was an office that could not be filled at all, and the two failed differently rather than identically. Choice B leaves the sentences as they are, which costs nothing now and leaves a reviewer to supply Tilly's sentence less kindly.

**My view.** Keep "private armies" with Ertman until Lander is read; hedge the council in a clause; and on Chapter 9 hedge both sentences and let the minority-versus-incapacity distinction carry the weight the count was carrying, because a distinction survives a counter-example and a count does not.

## Decision 10 — What "the ratchet" is, and whose thesis it is: artillery only, or capital-intensity in general; Finer, Bean and Tilly as predecessors
*Covers rows: (ddd), (yyy), (ooo), Check 4, and — folded in from Decision 11 — (mmm), Check 2, T75-a–d and B73-a–c (the Tilly 1975 and Bean 1973 intakes of 16 September)*

Decision 11 has been folded into this one. The earlier version of that decision said Bean's 1973 article was not in the corpus; it was read and assessed on 16 September, and the same day's reading of the full Tilly 1975 volume showed that the question Decision 11 asked — how the book differs from the artillery thesis Tilly rejected — is the same question as this decision's, asked about a predecessor rather than a rival. One decision is cleaner than two.

**The background.** "The ratchet" is the book's word for the point at which the change became irreversible: once a crown had bought the siege train and the permanent tax to keep it, no subject could match it and no assembly could take it back. The spine, in section 8(a), speaks of "the ratchet" with a definite article and no qualification, and the Coda and the chapters inherit that usage. Chapter 4 (sections II and VII), Chapter 6 (section VII) and Chapter 10 (sections IV and VII) carry the account of what the guns did; the old rows do not quote a sentence from Chapters 4 and 6, so I cannot show you their present wording, only what they claim.

Three works in the September reading press on that definite article. William McNeill, the American world historian, in *The Pursuit of Power* (Chicago, 1982), agrees on direction — the new weapons enhanced the power of any ruler who could afford them "at the expense of neighbors and subjects who were unable to avail themselves of the new technology of war" and served "to dwarf the Italian city-states and to reduce other small sovereignties to triviality" (p. 89) — but his fifteenth century belongs to Venice: "A regular standing army of known size and capability emerged in the better-governed cities of Italy during the first half of the fifteenth century" (p. 75), and the Fronde's suppression in the 1650s "marked the time at which transalpine states finally caught up with the level of administrative management and control over armed force that had been attained in Venice and Milan two centuries earlier" (p. 125). Civil control of hired force was an Italian achievement made without a siege train; Italy lost anyway because "the sovereignty of mere city-states, however skillfully managed, could not permanently be maintained" against "newly consolidated monarchies, commanding comparatively vast territories" (p. 79). Jan Glete, a Swedish historian of early-modern navies, in *War and the State in Early Modern Europe* (Routledge, 2002), supplies a second form of the same effect: the most capital-intensive and most completely state-owned military organisations were navies, and they did not begin with a broken wall: "Most of the earliest sailing navies were created on the European periphery: in Portugal, England, Denmark-Norway and Sweden … Private trade and shipping interests were of little importance for the early growth of sailing navies" (p. 39); heavy guns "were capital goods, and mobile siege artillery required a permanent organisation of skilled men" (p. 65), but the heaviest ordnance needed "special warships of a size and construction that made them less economic as merchantmen" (p. 65). The third pressure is the fortress. The trace italienne, the low, thick, angled bastion the Italians developed to resist cannon, is said to have cancelled the guns within a generation, and Chapter 10 has never answered it. McNeill does: "Only the wealthiest states and cities could afford the scores of cannon and the enormous labor of construction required by the trace italienne" (p. 90), so that "Imperial consolidation halted halfway" (p. 91). The guns consolidated inside the polities that could pay; the fortress froze the map between them, which is your three-or-four-bloc prediction in sixteenth-century form.

Then the eyewitness. Philippe de Commynes served Charles VIII on the Italian expedition of 1494, and the third volume of Joseph Calmette's edition of his *Mémoires* (Champion, 1925) is in the repository. In the book's grading this is T1, a primary source, where Chapter 10's close now rests on T2, a modern historian summarising another. The Italians "n'entendoient point le faict de l'artillerie … et en France n'avoit jamais esté si bien entendue" (p. 50); Monte San Giovanni, "une très forte place" (p. 91), "fut batue sept ou huyt heures et puis prinse d'assault" (p. 92); "hors le chasteau de Napples, n'y eut nul qui empeschast le roy ung jour naturel" (p. 81); and the chalk with which the quartermasters marked their billets is his (pp. 81–82). The same volume carries the discipline: at Fornovo, the one open battle, "Je ne croy point que l'artillerie des deux costéz tuast dix hommes et ne dura point le combat ung quart d'heure" (p. 192), with Calmette's note reporting wetted powder. An earlier row pinned that line to pp. 210–11, which carry the Apennine recrossing; the pin must be p. 192. And Commynes's own explanation of the campaign is providential; he never credits the tax machine.

Now the new material, which is about whose thesis this is. The book has always cited Charles Tilly, the American sociologist whose "War made the state, and the state made war" is the field's slogan, and it has always treated him as saying nothing about guns. The spine distinguishes the book's claim from his as "not war-made-the-state in general"; Chapter 6 §VII introduces its main objection as coming "from the historian this chapter has cited more than any other"; Chapter 10 §VII, Objection 6, calls him deliberately technology-agnostic. All three are wrong, and wrong twice over. In his 1985 essay "War Making and State Making as Organized Crime" Tilly summarised a 1973 article by the economic historian Richard Bean and rejected it: "Bean's summary does not stand up to historical scrutiny. As a matter of practice, the shift to infantry-backed artillery sieges of fortified cities occurred only during the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries. Artillery did improve during the fifteenth century, but the invention of new fortifications, especially the trace italienne, rapidly countered the advantage of artillery. The arrival of effective artillery came too late to have caused the increase in the viable size of states. (However, the increased cost of fortifications to defend against artillery did give an advantage to states enjoying larger fiscal bases.)" That is a verdict, not a silence. And ten years earlier, in the 1975 volume he edited, *The Formation of National States in Western Europe*, Tilly had put a punctuated, weapon-driven account of state-building at chapter two and praised it from chapter one.

That chapter is Samuel Finer's, "State- and Nation-Building in Europe: The Role of the Military". Finer periodises by weapon ("The Age of Pike and Handgun: 1450–1550"), states the extraction cycle ("More troops — more extraction — more troops", p. 93), names the bastion as the counter ("To counter the new artillery, the tall, thin walls of the medieval castle had to be replaced or girdled by low and thick ones, forerunners of the bastion", p. 105), gives cannon the decisive victories and the constitutional consequence ("Castillon and Formigny — though this was not realized at the time — expelled England from Europe for good", p. 104), and states the French fiscal outcome: "there was no central constitutional organ of opposition to taxation by fist similar to England's and consequently money for the wars could be found more easily in France than across the Channel" (p. 127). Tilly's own summary of him is that the papers show "an alternation of long pauses with giant steps closely following changes in military technology and the scale of war" (pp. 51–52). So the ratchet is substantially in Finer, and Tilly endorsed it. Bean's article, "War and the Birth of the Nation State" (*Journal of Economic History*, 1973), read in full on 16 September, is closer still: it has the cannon, dated to the middle of the fifteenth century; Normandy, "60 fortified places being reduced in 369 days" (p. 207, a figure he takes from Oman); the fiscal appropriation, "In France in the 1440's and in Spain in the 1480's the rulers were able to seize these taxes as a right and thus dispense with the legislative bodies" (p. 213); the nobility "subordinate to the central governments — the process being largely complete by 1500" (p. 220); and the cascade by which one state's tax power forced its neighbours to centralise or be conquered (p. 220). A reader who knows this article will ask what the book adds to it.

What is left to the book is real but narrower than the drafts assume. First, the explanandum, the thing being explained. Finer's variable is the military format and its style of rule; Bean's is the size and number of states, on a cost curve borrowed from the theory of the firm, so that "States larger than the optimal range tend to disintegrate, and those smaller tend to be absorbed" (p. 204). Tilly's refutation was aimed at size, and Poland and Russia refute it. The book's variable is the irreversibility of a fiscal settlement and the closure of the sub-state path to decisive force inside a surviving polity, which a map cannot refute and only a reversed settlement or a replicated instrument could. Bean's own last paragraph concedes that this question is not his: the changes in war "practically guaranteed" the concentration of power, "However, there was no certainty as to which faction within the nation would control the new centralized state … That task remains a challenge to further research" (p. 221). That is spine §8(g)'s split between mechanism and constitutional form, drawn in 1973. Second, the evidence. Finer's two French paragraphs transpose Castillon and Formigny (p. 104 has Castillon in 1450 and Formigny in 1453), put the Italian campaign at 1484 (p. 127), and carry the tradition that the Estates "had in 1439 voted Charles VII the taille" (p. 127), which Russell Major destroyed in 1960 (see Decision 12); Bean's French evidence is a sentence and a figure from Oman and a misdated militia. The book's is the account books, the Bureau service, the 1442 establishment document and the Normandy census. That is the difference between a hypothesis and a demonstration, not between two hypotheses, and the chapter should say so in those words. Third, Bean's window: he has the offensive advantage lasting from about 1450 until "the second decade of the sixteenth century", after which "the balance was again in favor of the defense" (pp. 207–08). If so, the settlement outlasted the capability that set it by three centuries, which is what irreversibility means, and the chapter should say that too. Bean also formulates the bastion better than the book's present answer to Tilly: after 1525 "the minimum effective scale of fortification was considerably larger … there were many fewer places in Europe after 1525 A.D. with effective fortification" (p. 208), the closure of the sub-state path stated from the defensive face.

One objection in the same 1975 volume is unanswered and flagged. Gabriel Ardant, the French fiscal historian, holds that "Productivity, especially agricultural productivity, and demography were together the first obstacle, a really physical barrier to the power of the state" (p. 176), and that "The ambitions of kings for several centuries ran ahead of the economic structure of their states" (p. 193). On his reading the French settlement of the 1440s should not have held, because the base could not carry it. The book's answer would be that a capability no subject could replicate changed the politics of a fixed base rather than its size, but that answer is not yet written anywhere.

**Why it matters.** This is a large point because it protects the claim the book most needs to defend, and because it decides what the book may claim for itself. Read as the claim that artillery invented state control of organised force, the ratchet is refuted by McNeill's Venice; read as the claim that only artillery could produce a capital-intensive monopoly, by Glete's navies; if Chapter 10 keeps saying the guns won battles, by Commynes at Fornovo; and read as a new thesis, by a fifty-three-year-old article and a chapter in a volume the book already cites. A specialist who finds Tilly's 1985 verdict, or Finer at chapter two, in an afternoon will treat the book's description of Tilly as diagnostic of the whole. Defined narrowly — as scale, the end of the small sovereign, the French form of a wider capital-intensity effect, and the irreversibility of a settlement rather than the size of states — the same authors become the book's witnesses: McNeill for direction, Glete for breadth, Commynes for the demonstration, Bean for the shape of the thesis, Finer for its respectability, and Tilly's parenthesis about "larger fiscal bases" for the mechanism in miniature. The novelty then rests where it can be defended, on the object and the evidence, and Objection 6 in Chapter 10 becomes "How is this not Bean?", which is a better objection than an invented silence.

**Your choices.** Choice A is the full package. ## Decision 12 — Taxes, not credit: what kind of settlement 1439–51 was, and when the Estates General met
*Covers rows: (xxx), (ccc), Check 1, and M60-a–e (the Major intake of 16 September). Touches spine §8(a).*

**The background.** Chapter 10, section III, is the book's account of the French fiscal settlement of the 1440s, when the taille — the direct tax on commoners — became permanent to pay for the standing companies and the artillery. The section runs what it calls a control experiment: "At the Estates of Tours in 1484 — the first Estates General in decades, meeting over a royal minority — the assembled realm asserted itself: the taille was granted for two years only and cut to some 1.5 million. The machine absorbed the cut, outlived the assertion, and no Estates General met again until 1560." Later in the section the assemblies are described as "not summoned again for nearly forty years", and the section closes by calling the French case "the coupling's first full demonstration". Chapter 4 §VII and Chapter 10 together lean on a phrase about consolidation moving "fastest where the fisc is deepest". The spine's section 8(a) states flatly that "No Estates General met again until 1484". The earlier version of this decision said the 1468 meeting was "pending Major 1960". It is not pending. J. Russell Major's *Representative Institutions in Renaissance France, 1421–1559* (Wisconsin, 1960) was read on 16 September, and it does three things to this section, one of which reaches the spine.

Take the settled matter first. David Stasavage, an American political scientist, in *States of Credit* (Princeton, 2011), is the standard quantitative account of who in Europe could borrow long-term. A funded debt is a loan secured on a named tax and paid to bondholders indefinitely; his finding is that only small city-states could sustain one before 1500, because only a city could keep an assembly of creditors sitting often enough to watch the money. His table dates the first long-term loans to Arras in 1241 and Venice in 1262, the first by a territorial state to Castile in 1489 and France in 1522. "Prior to 1500, we cannot directly compare city-states and territorial states, because no territorial states had yet created long-term debts." So the French settlement ran seventy-six years without the instrument that "depth of fisc" would normally mean. His Dutch case supports your ordering: the Dutch revolt "was first and foremost a tax revolution … it was only after this point that borrowing became a major source of war finance". Commynes, the eyewitness of Decision 10, supplies the 1484 numbers from the other end: Charles VIII resolved in 1498 to take no more than "douze cens mil frans et par forme de taille, oultre son domayne, qui estoit la somme que les trois Estatz luy avoyent accordée à Tours", while "le peuple, qui paie au jour d'uy plus de deux milions et demy de francs de taille" (Calmette III, p. 304).

Now Major. His first finding is the spine sentence. His appendix, "Assemblies of the Estates General and the Estates of Languedoil, 1421–1615" (pp. 151–52), marks every doubtful assembly with a query symbol. Between the Orléans meeting of 1439 and Tours in 1484 it lists an Estates General at Tours in April 1468 with no query. His chapter on it (pp. 54–58) gives the detail: letters of convocation on 26 February 1468; town deputies "named in general assemblies of the inhabitants" (p. 54); present, "five of the six peers of the church … and nineteen other archbishops and bishops", "over twenty-four nobles", and "the clerical and lay deputies from sixty-four towns; they must have numbered close to two hundred" (p. 55). He expressly denies it was managed: "There is no real proof that either Louis XI or the rebellious nobles tampered with the elections in the towns … The oft-quoted statement of Commynes that Louis summoned whom he wished does not apply to the estates of 1468" (p. 55). Its business was Normandy, Brittany and England; its output was recommendations, "all that Louis could have wished" (p. 57); and no tax was granted. So "No Estates General met again until 1484" does not survive: the gaps are 1440 to 1468, twenty-eight years, and 1468 to 1484, sixteen. Chapter 10 §III's "nearly forty years" fails with it. So does "no Estates General met again until 1560", because the same appendix lists May 1506 at Tours and January 1558 at Paris as restricted Estates General, assemblies to which only a limited number of clergy, nobles and town deputies were summoned, and Major gives the 1558 meeting its own section, at which the clergy offered 2,300,000 livres and the towns 4,600,000 (p. 147). Ertman's formula, "a full, working Estates General was not to meet again until 1560" (pp. 92–93), is the defensible one; the draft dropped the qualifier. What survives, and survives intact, is the substance: 1468 granted nothing; 1484's grant was breached within months; and "There is no evidence of any move to force the promised convocation of the Estates General by the refusal to vote taxes or by any other means" (p. 116).

His second finding is the 1484 figures, and it reconciles the draft with the primary. The Estates offered 1,200,000 livres for two years; the council announced in August 1484 that the taille for 1485 would be 1,500,000 "in spite of the fact the Estates General had granted only 1,200,000 livres for that year" (p. 115), and returned to 1,500,000 for 1486 though "the Estates General had made no offer at all for that year" (p. 115). The cut was from 4,400,000 levied under Louis XI, and "Perhaps no French government ever went further to meet the desires of the people" (p. 100). Commynes's 1.2 million "par forme de taille" is the grant; the draft's 1.5 million is the levy; the draft has conflated them, and the corrected sentence reads: granted 1,200,000 for two years, levied 1,500,000, down from 4,400,000.

His third finding is the largest, because it is about the book's founding date. The textbook story is that the Estates of 1439 consented to a permanent army and a permanent taille, freeing the king from consent thereafter. Chapter 10 §III already holds that story "at tertiary strength". Major goes further: "A completely erroneous theory has developed that the estates at this time consented to the creation of a permanent standing army and voted a permanent taille for its support … it contains scarcely a word of truth, as Antoine Thomas pointed out as early as 1878" (pp. 32–33). Of the 1439 act: "The purpose of these articles was to restore discipline, not to create a permanent army" (p. 33), and "Nothing whatsoever was said about a permanent taille, and only 100,000 livres were voted" (p. 34). What actually closed consent, on his account, happened in two steps inside the artillery years. The aides, the indirect taxes, were converted province by province after 1440 and then "collected without further consultation with the estates" (p. 40). Then in 1451 Charles VII abolished the regular taille as a tax cut and kept only the taille des gens de guerre, the tax for the soldiers: "the king ordered the élus to impose the necessary taxes to support the lances without convoking the provincial estates to give consent. The taille to support the army, like the aides some years earlier, had come to be levied by royal command alone" (p. 42). That is two years into the Normandy campaign and two years before Castillon, and it was done by reducing the burden by a third, not by force. He also supplies the king's own words, from the reply to the rebel nobles in 1442: "it is not necessary to assemble the three estates to levy the tailles because it is only a burden and an expense to the poor common people who have to pay the cost of those who attend" (pp. 34–35), which is the draft's "It was simply not consulted, year after year, until non-consultation was the constitution" said by the man who did it. And a hard number: in 1461, 58 per cent of royal income came from the taille, and "As long as the estates controlled this tax, the crown could not become financially independent" (p. 32).

Two further things Major says cut against the chapter and must be recorded. The Estates General lapsed, on his reading, not because of the guns but because the provinces would not attend and the crown found it "no longer necessary" (p. 36), and the lapse dates from 1440, before the guns and independent of them; indeed "it was far more difficult for Charles to control the deliberations of the provincial estates and the towns than those of the Estates General" (p. 38), so abandoning the national assembly was no victory. The ratchet's object must therefore be the provincial estates and the magnates, not the Estates General. And he denies the inference from the standing army to subordination with arithmetic: two thousand lances and sixteen thousand francs-archers, "less than two modern divisions", could "hardly subject a population of 15,000,000 persons" (p. 9); the towns "had comparable forces equipped with artillery and munitions" (p. 9); and "The compagnies d'ordonnance were composed almost entirely of the local nobility and captained by members of the leading families of the provinces" (p. 9). Finally, a counter-instance on the book's own ground: in 1458, five years after Castillon, in the very province the siege train had reconquered, the king "committed himself to first winning the approval of the three estates of the duchy" of Normandy, whose estates then met every year "for nearly two centuries" (p. 44); and where consent survived, in Normandy and Languedoc, the taille per hearth was roughly three times what it was in the provinces that had lost it (p. 42).

**Why it matters.** Four things of rising weight, and the last touches spine §8(a) directly, so this is now one of the largest decisions on the sheet. The 1484 figures are a plain accuracy point, now settled by a T2 and a T1 that agree. The "nearly forty years", "until 1484" and "until 1560" clauses are a factual exposure of the simplest kind: a reviewer opens Major's appendix and finds 1468, 1506 and 1558. Stasavage's apparatus is aimed at war-and-state-formation arguments, and a specialist will ask whether the chapter claims a fiscal-military state for a polity with no funded debt for three generations; the answer is that your mechanism was never a credit mechanism, but the chapter never says so. And the hinge: the book currently dates its French settlement to 1439–46 on a tradition that the standard authority on French representative institutions called "scarcely a word of truth" sixty-six years ago, when the same authority offers 1451, inside the artillery years, as the point at which the army's tax came to be "levied by royal command alone". That is better for the book than 1439, because it puts the decisive step between the Normandy census and Castillon; but it is a change to the founding date, and Major's own explanation of it is a tax cut and provincial particularism, not a siege train. His army-too-small objection is the same objection Ertman made, from a source thirty-seven years older, and the book's answer, that the crown held the artillery arm as a department of state while the cavalry arm remained a patronage instrument shared with the nobility, is not yet said out loud.

**Your choices.** Choice A is the full correction. Spine §8(a)'s sentence is reworded to Ertman's qualified form: frequent convocation abandoned after 1440, 1468 named as an Estates General that granted no tax, 1484's grant breached within months, no full working Estates General until 1560. Chapter 10 §III drops "nearly forty years" and "until 1560" for the same form; re-bases the 1484 figures on Major and Commynes, granted 1,200,000 for two years, levied 1,500,000, down from 4,400,000, with more than 2,500,000 paid by 1498; gains one or two sentences saying in terms that this was a tax settlement and not a credit one, with Stasavage named and France's first funded debt of 1522 placed; and replaces "fastest where the fisc is deepest" with "the capacity to tax at scale" in both chapters. It costs perhaps two hundred and fifty words, one adjective in the spine, and the surrender of a neat number. It buys a paragraph a quantitative historian cannot dent and a spine sentence that survives its own appendix. Choice B holds the paragraph as drafted and logs Major and Stasavage in the chapter's critiques file only. The section keeps its momentum; the spine keeps a sentence that a published table contradicts; the 1.5 million stays unexplained against a primary and a T2 that both say 1.2 was granted; and "first full demonstration" sits unqualified in a chapter that never says which leg of public finance it demonstrates. I do not think B is available for the spine sentence at all, and the Major intake marked that sub-choice as yours.

Inside Choice A there are five sub-choices, lettered M60-a to M60-e. M60-a is the spine sentence itself, reworded or retired on the 1468 evidence, with §III's two clauses corrected to match; the memo marks this as Roderick's, and I have treated it above as the one part of A that cannot be declined. M60-b is the hinge: whether §III adds 1451, the taille des gens de guerre levied by royal command alone (Major, p. 42), to the 1439, 1445, 1446 and c. 1450 sequence, and whether the ratchet's object is restated as the provincial estates and the magnates rather than the Estates General, which had lapsed for reasons the book does not claim. Taking it means the settlement is dated by the closure of consent in the provinces rather than by a grant that Major says was never made; declining it means the chapter keeps a founding date that its own principal revisionist has demolished and that Finer, in Decision 10, repeated carelessly. M60-c is the 1484 figures, already stated above; the correction is mechanical and I can see no reason to decline it. M60-d is whether §VII answers Major by name on the army-too-small objection, distinguishing the siege train from the companies and conceding that the companies were officered by the provincial nobility, a concession Chapter 10's memo already recorded from Ertman; the alternative is to leave the objection to the critiques file, where a reviewer holding Major's page 9 will not find it. M60-e is whether §III or §VI carries Normandy 1458, consent conceded after the reconquest and kept for two centuries, as the book's own evidence that the mechanism does not determine the constitutional form, which is exactly the point spine §8(g) makes for the present; or declines it with a reason. It is the strongest medieval demonstration available that the same mechanism produced both branches inside one kingdom, and it is not currently in the chapter.

**My view.** Choice A with all five sub-choices, and this is not close: the spine sentence is false as written and a published appendix says so; the 1484 figures now have a primary and a T2 in agreement; the tax-not-credit sentence costs almost nothing and pre-empts the likeliest specialist objection to Part II's centrepiece; and 1451 is a better hinge than 1439 because it is true, it is inside the artillery years, and it is Major's, which means the chapter's principal revisionist supplies its founding date. I would take Normandy 1458 for the same reason I would take Bean's closing paragraph in Decision 10: it makes the mechanism-versus-form split look like the shape the evidence has always had rather than a hedge the book adopted in September.

## Decision 13 — Venice and the funded debt: three corrections
*Covers rows: (aaa), (bbb), (eee)*

**The background.** Chapter 4, section VI, makes three statements about Venice that the September reading contradicts. It calls the Monte Vecchio of 1262 "the first funded sovereign debt in European history". It says the instruments of public debt "emerge wherever scale, commerce and insecurity meet". And, together with Chapter 10, section VI, it diagnoses the Venetian limit as "almost no bureaucracy", a phrase borrowed from Frederic Lane, the American historian of Venice.

On the first, Stasavage's table dates Arras to 1241 and Venice to 1262, and on Epstein's evidence Genoa consolidated its debt in 1259, giving creditors shares in a new fund paying a nominal eight per cent with a right of sale that "created a secondary market". Lane's narrower formulation survives: Venice was the first European state to fund its debt so that interest was paid regularly from one fund to all bondholders equally. On the second, Stasavage's whole argument runs the other way — "geographic scale often proved to be a fundamental obstacle" — because the close creditor supervision that made lending safe was only possible where an assembly could meet weekly. On his sample, a city-state without a debt had a 43 per cent chance of creating one in a given half-century against a territorial state's 5 per cent, and city-states borrowed about two percentage points cheaper. He also re-points the paternity: "it was actually the system of municipal debt developed in northern Europe that would set the model for long-term government borrowing in Europe until the end of the nineteenth century" — the northern annuity, not the Venetian bond. On the third, Glete contradicts the phrase directly: "Venice also had a large permanent and state-administered navy and may in terms of permanent armed force have ranked number one in Europe in 1500" (p. 13). Lane's judgement was about civil administration, not about the absence of an apparatus; and Stasavage adds that Venice "continued to borrow at very low interest rates into the eighteenth century, long after its period as an innovator in industrial production, or as a principal hub of commerce, had ended".

**Why it matters.** These are housekeeping in scale but not in effect, because each is a checkable error in a chapter that sets up the medieval alternatives to the state, and the dates are in a table any reviewer can open. The third correction also strengthens the argument: a Venice that had the permanent navy and the cheap credit and still could not consolidate is better evidence for the book's two-variable finding — armed force and permanent fisc both needed — than a Venice that merely lacked institutions.

**Your choices.** Choice A takes all three: Lane's exact wording replaces the superlative, with Genoa 1259 and Arras 1241 named in the same breath; "wherever scale, commerce and insecurity meet" becomes "a scale small enough to be governed by its creditors", with a clause conceding the northern annuity as the true template; and "almost no bureaucracy" becomes a sentence saying Venice had the permanent naval organisation and lacked the fiscal-administrative reach into a hinterland. The cost is one superlative, one neat triad and one brisk dismissal, and a slightly longer sentence in each place. Choice B keeps the wording and footnotes the doubts. That is not attractive here: the footnote to "first" would have to admit two earlier instruments, which reads worse than writing Lane's sentence, and the navy contradiction is a matter of fact rather than register.

**My view.** Choice A on all three, because in each case the corrected sentence serves Part II better than the one it replaces.

## Decision 14 — What "administrative capacity" means, and admitting the settlement bred venality
*Covers rows: (www), (ff), (iii), Check 5*

**The background.** The project doctrine, CLAUDE.md section 2, states the mechanism as a chain: weapon capital-intensity, then permanent finance, then administrative capacity, then suppression of intermediate authorities. "Administrative capacity" is not defined. Three sentences in the chapters lean on the unglossed term. Chapter 10, section III, calls the French 1440s "the coupling's first full demonstration", and section VII calls the settlement "the template Europe copied for three centuries". Chapter 11, section II, opens the Italian descent of 1494 as "The first continental adventure of the completed fiscal-military state". Chapter 5, section VI, says that with the French settlement "the problem ended".

Glete and Ertman each take one of these apart. Glete is the authority for the very term "fiscal-military state" that Chapter 10 projects back onto the 1440s, and he denies it to them: the type was "practically unknown in 1500" (p. 41), the period 1480–1560 was "largely an embryo" (p. 21), and "the early but very limited permanent French forces practically disappeared in the long civil war period (1562–98)" (p. 39), the decisive French change coming under Colbert in the 1660s. Ertman takes the 1494 army apart by the numbers: it "numbered no more than 20,000 combatants, and was thus smaller than many of the armies assembled during the Hundred Years War" (pp. 94–95); the standing companies supplied only the cavalry, a quarter to a third of the whole; the infantry was Swiss and German by contract "right down to 1558, when they still supplied 21,000 men out of a total infantry contingent of about 40,000"; and even the companies were already patronage assets, each "controlled and commanded by the local royal governor, almost always a member of a prominent magnate family" (p. 94).

Ertman's larger point is about the third link. Venality — the sale of public office, and the treatment of office as heritable property — was built into the French settlement from the start: "the administration of 15th-century France, whether central or local, judicial or financial, was dotted with clans and dynasties of officials who treated their offices as their own property" (p. 81). The price of France's early consolidation was "a substantial loss of effective control to proprietary officeholders, tax farmers, and officeholder-financiers who viewed the state not only as an instrument of princely power but also as a source of income and social standing" (p. 28), and after 1494 war "inevitably led to a massive increase in the role played by private finance, proprietary officeholding, and military entrepreneurship" (p. 91). The German princes who later copied the French offices were "fully aware of the French system of venalité des offices and determined to avoid it" (p. 244). His verdict on the chain is flat: war "certainly encouraged neither rationalization in the Weberian sense nor the creation of proto-modern institutions" (p. 154) — Weberian meaning the salaried, rule-bound, impersonal bureaucracy Max Weber described.

**Why it matters.** This is a large point in doctrine and a small one in words. If "administrative capacity" is read as Weberian rationalisation, Ertman refutes the third link in one sentence, and his France — magnates subordinated, apparatus lost — becomes a standing rebuttal. If it is read as the capacity to assess, collect and pay at campaign tempo — the account books of Chapter 10, section IV — and if the subordination claimed is of armed intermediaries, then his officeholders, who were unarmed, do not touch it. The concession has a price worth paying and a gain worth having: the settlement bred a new rent-taking stratum, and the officeholder-financier lending the crown its own revenue is the nearest fifteenth-century form to the platform that licenses its stack to the state and lends against the contract, which is a prediction Chapter 12 should be making anyway.

**Your choices.** Choice A glosses the term and admits the defect. CLAUDE.md section 2 gains the one-word gloss — administrative capacity means collecting capacity — and states that the fourth link is the subordination of armed intermediaries. Chapter 10, section III, meets Glete's clock by name, calling the 1440s the first complete run of the coupling rather than the first fiscal-military state on his yardstick, conceding 1562–98, and entering venality as a stated defect of the settlement; section VII narrows "template" to the offices copied and the venality refused. Chapter 11 cuts "completed" and carries Ertman's twenty thousand, the Swiss and the magnate governors in a subordinate clause. Chapter 5 says the problem "ended in its armed form", naming the venal stratum and the governors' companies as what the French answer bred — which Chapter 10's critiques file already records, so leaving Chapter 5 unqualified is an internal inconsistency. The cost is a doctrine line, one adjective, two confident phrases and a paragraph. It buys immunity from the rival's best cut, and a defect that Part III can turn into a prediction. Choice B leaves the chain unglossed and the sentences as they stand. The doctrine keeps its compression and the chapters their confidence; the book's own shorthand then invites the Weberian reading its principal administrative rival demolishes, and the defence that the book never meant that is worth nothing until it is written down.

**My view.** Choice A, because it is a clarification of what the book already claims rather than a retreat, and the venality material is a genuine gain rather than a concession.
## Decision 15 — Spain: which intermediaries Castile actually subordinated
*Covers rows: (dd), (hh), (ttt)*

**The background.** Chapter 11, section III, states that the Spanish settlement subordinated "the intermediaries of the core" — that is, the lords, orders and cities of Castile itself, the rest of the composite monarchy being ruled by contract. It does not distinguish between kinds of intermediary. Chapter 8, section VII, separately claims that the English sequence of ranking the courts and subordinating rival jurisdictions happened "nowhere else", and carries a sentence on Spain without dates.

Jan Glete, a Swedish historian of early-modern navies and armies, wrote *War and the State in Early Modern Europe* (2002), a comparative study of Spain, the Dutch Republic and Sweden. It is a T2 source — the book's label for canonical scholarship, as against T1 for primary documents. Glete confirms every armed limb of your claim. In the war for Granada "Much of the war effort was paid for by taxes to the state, not by the participants" (p. 75); "Private armies as instruments of aristocratic factionalism ceased to be important after this war, and the noblemen's castles had become vulnerable to the rulers' new siege artillery" (p. 75); the hermandad, the towns' own policing force, was "abolished in 1498" (p. 75); and the military orders were "permanently incorporated into the crown in 1523, a change that eliminated autonomous military structures" (p. 76). But the fiscal limb runs the other way. The millones, a new tax agreed in 1590, was raised by "the cities represented in the Cortes … on their own", so that "the local elites took control of essential parts of the tax administration" (p. 122); "In the early 1620s, the crown tried to change the tax into a permanent obligation to maintain 30,000 soldiers, but that was denied" (p. 122); there are "few signs of deep penetration of a royal bureaucracy into local communities" (p. 138); and those elites eventually "had gained the power to dismantle large parts of the Spanish fiscal-military state" (p. 126).

David Stasavage, an American political scientist, supplies the constitutional sequel in *States of Credit* (2011). The Cortes — the representative assembly of Castile — won consent, administration of the millones and audit powers, and then "the individual Castilian cities gradually lost control over their representatives", so that "the representatives failed to effectively use even those formal powers that they did have"; after 1665 "the monarchy did away with the institution entirely" (chapter 7). He compares "the Cortes of Castile in the seventeenth century to the Ugandan Parliament in the twenty-first" (chapter 8). This is a documented case of a body that kept its formal powers and lost its real ones, which is the exact shape of the "ornamental" settlement you ruled open two weeks ago.

On "nowhere else", Charles Tilly's disarmament chronology is plural and later: the Tudor programme is one case, Richelieu's is in the 1620s, and the Empire's follows 1648. Disarmament happened everywhere on different clocks; only the ranking of jurisdictions, in England between the 1470s and 1540s, has a claim to priority.

**Why it matters.** This is an accuracy point before it is a rhetorical one, and it is a large one. As written, Chapter 11's unqualified claim is simply false of Castile's fiscal intermediaries, and a reviewer who has read Glete will find the falsity in two pages. Stated with the split, Spain becomes evidence for your two-variable finding — armed intermediaries subordinated, the fisc never made permanent, decisive force fielded for a century and a half and then not sustained — which is the Burgundy finding of Chapter 10 at imperial scale. The "nowhere else" claim in Chapter 8 is a smaller exposure but the same kind: an absolute that one paragraph of Tilly contradicts.

**Your choices.** Choice A is to adopt the split, narrow "nowhere else", and give the Cortes portrait to Chapter 12. Chapter 11 gains a qualifying sentence naming Glete and distinguishing the armed intermediaries (orders, castles, private armies) from the fiscal ones (the Cortes cities collecting the millones on their own account), plus a half-sentence naming the Cortes as the intermediary Castile did not subordinate; the full portrait — powers kept, control lost, abolition in 1665 — goes whole into Chapter 12, where the constitutional form of the settlement is scored and where a documented historical case outweighs any modern indicator. Chapter 8's "nowhere else" becomes a claim about jurisdiction only, and its Spanish sentence gets Glete's dates. The cost is perhaps eighty words in Chapter 11 and a slight loss of pace; the gain is that the reviewer's objection becomes the chapter's own finding.

Choice B is the same split and the same narrowing, but with the Cortes portrait placed in Chapter 11, section III, at the moment the subordination story is told. This is honest and plants Chapter 12's question early. The risk is that section III already carries the armed-versus-fiscal refinement, and a second Cortes passage there reads as the same point made twice; Chapter 12 then has to repeat or back-reference it.

Choice C is to keep the unqualified formulation and the absolute in Chapter 8. Nothing changes and the paragraphs stay clean. Glete's pages 122 and 126 are then available to any hostile reader as a straightforward factual correction, and the book forfeits a case it could have claimed.

**My view.** Choice A, because it is the rare refinement that makes the argument more precise and more favourable at the same time, and keeping the portrait whole in one place stops the book saying the same thing twice.

## Decision 16 — China: the verdict layer by layer, and the word "fusion"
*Covers rows: (kk), (yy), (zz)*

**The background.** Chapter 12, section IV, says that "China is the furthest along … the fused-ownership absorption mode", and the same vocabulary of "fused" and "fusion" recurs in Chapter 2, Chapter 4 ("the Chinese fused model of Chapter 2"), Chapter 11 and Appendix C. Chapter 2, section III, dates the Chinese case by its most visible event: "The demonstration was the crackdown of 2020–21". Chapter 12, section III, describes the absorbed platform's legal form as "operative but never invested". The spine — the book's internal doctrine document, which records your rulings and licenses sentences for use anywhere in the manuscript — currently ranks China first and calls its settlement "substantially executed".

Tai Ming Cheung, a scholar of the Chinese defence industry, published *Innovate to Dominate* (2022), the one full scholarly account of the Chinese defence-industrial stack as a state project. His verdict on the move that puts private capability into the decisive stack is the reverse of the draft's. Military-civil fusion's twenty years are "an underwhelming achievement" (p. 116); "Hybridization has yet to make a significant impact on the Chinese techno-security state" (p. 295); "the public sector will remain the dominant player with the private sector limited to a minor role" (p. 298). The obstruction is domestic and privileged — "two hot ends but a cold middle" (p. 97) — and the state-owned defence monopolies "have so far successfully limited meaningful implementation of these market-opening measures" (p. 172). Private firms enter only by licence, and "The big state-owned defense firms have maintained an iron grip on licenses" at the top category (pp. 114–15); the roughly three thousand firms recruited into the acquisition system are "a participation rate of just 2.3 percent" of 130,000 technology firms (p. 116). Foreign dependence is a standing condition: "China seeks long-term technological self-reliance but is heavily dependent on foreign technology and know-how in the meantime" (p. 298).

There is a second problem with the word itself. In China, "fusion" is a term of art: military-civil fusion, 军民融合, has been a national strategy since March 2015, with its own commission and its own outline document (pp. 51, 89, 94), and Cheung rates it early-stage. Your book uses "fusion" to mean something quite different — platforms ruled as departments of the party-state. A China-literate reviewer will read your "fusion" as his, check Cheung, and find the book apparently claiming as accomplished the very programme its cited authority rates as barely begun.

Two further rows concern dates. Jack Goldsmith and Tim Wu, in *Who Controls the Internet?* (2006), date the first instrument of platform discipline to 2002, when Yahoo "signed a document called the Public Pledge on Self-Discipline for the Chinese Internet Industry in which it promised to 'inspect and monitor the information on domestic and foreign Websites'" (p. 9), and "major commercial operators like Yahoo agreed in 2002 to a binding 'self-discipline pact'" (p. 96). The foreign firms were subordinated by pact two decades before the domestic ones were disciplined by fine. The same book supplies what Chapter 2 currently lacks: a documented internet instance of the investiture question, which in the book's medieval usage means who holds the title to an office as against who merely exercises it. In 1995 Robert Aiken asked verbatim "IS ISOC claiming that it has jurisdiction and overall responsibility for the [Internet] top level address and name space… if NO then who does own it?" (p. 37); Jon Postel split the root on 28 January 1998 and was stopped by Ira Magaziner's telephone call — "Jon, you don't have the legal right to conduct a test" (p. 46) — and thereafter root authority "has remained, without exception, in the hands of the U.S. government". The United States "never actually ceded control over either ICANN or the root" (p. 169). The row calls ICANN "the Sforza form", after Francesco Sforza, the hired captain who took Milan in 1450: the label for an operator who runs the function without holding the title. ICANN is the case where the operator ran it for a generation and the title never passed.

**Why it matters.** Two large things ride on this. The ranking touches the spine's central claims about which bloc is furthest along: "substantially executed" rests on the platform discipline and the legibility instruments, which Cheung does not cover, while his verdict concerns the defence-industrial stack, which the state never lost and never had to take back. The vocabulary is a different kind of risk — not a nuance but a false cognate, and a reviewer's note that writes itself. The dates and the root contest are smaller and are gains rather than corrections: a state that has been doing this since 2002 is better evidence than one that began in 2020, and the 1998 case gives Part I a documented instance where it now has an abstraction.

**Your choices.** Choice A is the full package. Chapter 12's Chinese verdict is rewritten layer by layer: executed at the canon (what may be said and hosted) and the register (who is who), inherited at the defence stack, unexecuted at the seam between the two, and dependent at the foreign chokepoint, with one clause naming the state's own defence monopolies as the over-mighty subjects the mechanism has not subordinated. "Fusion" is replaced wherever it names the platform mode — Chapter 2, Chapter 4, Chapter 11 and Appendix C — and reserved for the Chinese programme. Chapter 2 carries two dates, 2002 and 2020, and the root contest enters Chapter 2's investiture section with ICANN as the case where title never passed. The cost is a rewritten verdict sentence, four or five cross-chapter substitutions, the loss of a compact label, and a retrieval errand for the 1998 primary documents. It buys a verdict that survives the specialist, and a finding — that the Chinese state has subordinated the private barons and not its own state-owned ones — that the book can claim first.

Choice B is the rename and the dates only, keeping the undifferentiated ranking. The false cognate is removed and the sequence is honest, but the spine's "substantially executed" still stands over a defence stack its principal authority calls fragmented, and a reviewer will still ask how a settlement can be executed at the layer the state never lost.

Choice C is to keep "fused" and the single ranking. Nothing moves and the four-bloc casting keeps its cleanest instance. The book's strongest contemporary case is then stated in a form its own principal authority contradicts on two counts.

**My view.** Choice A, because the layer-by-layer verdict is the more interesting claim as well as the safer one; and if you take only half, take the rename, which is close to compulsory.

## Decision 17 — The word "exclusive"
*Covers rows: (vv)*

**The background.** The manuscript uses "exclusive" in four load-bearing places. The Introduction promises an argument about "the world before exclusive territorial sovereignty". Chapter 1, section I, asks "by what specific mechanism Europe's overlapping authorities were, over one long century, hammered into the exclusive territorial states we mistake for nature". Chapter 1, section VI, says both sides of the Westphalia dispute concede "that the exclusive territorial state was *made*, under pressure, out of something else", and then that some force "made the overlapping authorities of 1200 into the exclusive sovereignties of 1700 … and paradoxically produced, out of the densest plurality in European history, the most exclusive form of political authority ever devised".

Stephen Krasner, an American political scientist, wrote *Sovereignty: Organized Hypocrisy* (1999), the realist answer to Hedley Bull that Chapter 1 has always needed. His finding is that exclusive sovereignty was never anyone's practice: "There has never been some ideal time during which all, or even most, political entities conformed with all of the characteristics that have been associated with sovereignty" (p. 238); "the most important empirical conclusion of the present study is that the principles associated with both Westphalian and international legal sovereignty have always been violated" (p. 24); "perhaps half of the countries of Europe have never enjoyed Westphalian sovereignty for a single moment of their existence" (p. 29). He separates four senses of the word — domestic, interdependence, international legal and Westphalian — and insists that authority and control are different things (p. 10), with domestic sovereignty "not necessarily related to international legal or Westphalian sovereignty" (p. 12). He brackets the internal consolidation your book is about — "I offer no explanation for the displacement of other institutional forms" (p. 5) — and then concedes it happened.

John Ruggie cuts the other way and should be kept in view: for him the modern form "has differentiated its subject collectivity into territorially defined, fixed, and mutually exclusive enclaves of legitimate dominion" (1993, p. 151). So "exclusive" is defensible as a description of the doctrine of rule even where Krasner shows it false as a description of practice.

**Why it matters.** This is an accuracy point of the kind a reviewer enjoys, and it touches the Introduction. The book does not need exclusivity. Its whole chain ends at the subordination of internal intermediate authorities under a permanent fisc, which is domestic sovereignty in Krasner's control sense plus a fiscal monopoly of decisive force — a cell his grid does not contain, and the book's own contribution. Every sentence that claims exclusivity is claiming something stronger than the argument needs and weaker than the evidence supports. It also matters for the pendulum image in the spine: if the lock was exclusivity, Krasner has shown there was nothing to lock; if the lock was internal subordination, his book is silent and his own regularity — that strong states lose autonomy only by invitation (p. 29) — implies the consolidators were internally what you say they became.

**Your choices.** Choice A is to recast the four instances. "Exclusive territorial states" becomes states that had subordinated their internal intermediate authorities and monopolised the fiscal capacity for decisive force; "the exclusive sovereignties of 1700" becomes the consolidated sovereignties of 1700; "the most exclusive form of political authority ever devised" becomes a claim about internal hierarchy rather than external purity; and Chapter 1, section VI, gains one sentence conceding Krasner's page 24 by name and saying why the lock is a different cell of his own grid. One use of Ruggie's "mutually exclusive enclaves of legitimate dominion" survives as a quoted description of the doctrine, properly attributed. The cost is four sentence rewrites, one added sentence, and a loss of snap — "exclusive" is a crisp word and its replacements are not. It buys an opening claim exactly as strong as its evidence, and it turns Krasner from a standing objection into an ally.

Choice B is to keep "exclusive" and defend it on Ruggie's ground: the word describes the doctrine of rule as the period articulated it, not the record, and one footnote says so, citing Ruggie for the doctrine and Krasner for the practice. This costs a footnote and preserves the Introduction's cadence. A reviewer who has read Krasner will say, not unfairly, that the footnote is a retreat conducted in small type while the text goes on making the larger claim, and may extend the doubt to the pendulum.

**My view.** Choice A, because "internal subordination plus the fiscal monopoly of decisive force" is a sharper statement of your own thesis than "exclusive" ever was, and it is the claim Part II actually demonstrates.

## Decision 18 — The word "legibility", and James C. Scott
*Covers rows: (rr), and the "legible" half of (bbbb)*

**The background.** "Legibility" and "legible" appear twenty-eight times in the manuscript, including in the thesis statement itself — the "identity-payments-legibility stack" of the Introduction — and as a family of indicators in Appendix C. The word in this technical sense — a state's capacity to see its population and resources in a standard, countable form — is the coinage of James C. Scott, an American political scientist and anthropologist, in *Seeing Like a State* (1998). Scott is cited nowhere in the book, and Appendix A, the annotated bibliography, has no entry for him. Roughly a third of the twenty-eight uses are casual ("the shape of the present becomes legible"; "the test's result is legible"; Chapter 10, section V, uses "legible" in this loose way), and the rest are technical.

Scott's optic is exactly yours. The pre-modern state "was, in many crucial respects, partially blind" (p. 2); surnames, cadastres, registers and standard measures are "comprehensible as attempts at legibility and simplification" (p. 2), driven by "the pressing material interests of rulers: fiscal receipts, military manpower, and state security" (p. 24). His strongest instance is the Amsterdam population registry of May 1941, which guided the deportation of sixty-five thousand Jews: the legibility supplied by the Dutch authorities was "a capacity that in principle could as easily have been deployed to feed the Jews as to deport them" (p. 78). And his warning is stated as a recipe of four elements: "the legibility of a society provides the capacity for large-scale social engineering, high-modernist ideology provides the desire, the authoritarian state provides the determination to act on that desire, and an incapacitated civil society provides the leveled social terrain on which to build" (p. 5). Your book predicts the first and third of those elements combining in at least one bloc.

**Why it matters.** The citation is not optional. A book that uses a living scholar's coinage that often, in its title concept, and never names him, carries an unexploded objection that any reviewer can detonate in one line. The larger question is how much of him to take. Scott is not merely a debt; he is the sharpest available critic of the settlement you predict, since a Tudor settlement run as personal rule over a fully legible society is his four elements assembled. But the book has already built the falsifier his critique demands: Appendix C's first failure condition — a full-stack power defeated by cheap, distributed systems without a patron's decisive layer — is the Scott scenario, formalised and scoreable. Saying so converts him into part of the apparatus.

**Your choices.** Choice A is to name him at the Introduction's first use, take the Amsterdam registry and the four-element recipe into Chapter 12, section III, beside the identity stacks, identify Appendix C's first condition as the Scott scenario, and reword every casual instance to "visible", "readable" or "plain" so that the term of art stays a term of art. The cost is that Chapter 12 then carries a warning at full strength in a chapter already carrying the bloc verdicts, plus an hour's hygiene across the casual uses. It buys the book's strongest possible position on its title concept: prediction stated visibly beside the demonstration of what the same machinery does when the other elements arrive, in his words rather than defensively in yours.

Choice B is the bare citation: Scott named at first use and in Appendix A, the casual uses reworded, but the Amsterdam registry and the recipe left out. This discharges the debt and costs almost nothing. A reviewer would then say the book has cited the man whose warning it most needs to answer and has not answered him, which is a weaker position than not having noticed him at all.

**My view.** Choice A, because once he is in the apparatus the Amsterdam registry is the most economical statement of the disanalogy the book already makes — that registration is a capacity, not a policy — and the recipe lets Appendix C be seen to have priced his objection in.

## Decision 19 — Answering Ruggie, and who had the neomedieval idea first
*Covers rows: (tt), (xx), (ww), (fff)*

**The background.** John Gerard Ruggie's article "Territoriality and Beyond" (*International Organization*, 1993) supplied the vocabulary the neomedieval literature runs on. It bears on the book in four places, each a separate row.

First, the off-switch. Chapter 12, section I, closes its Ukraine week on the sentence "A drone-rich army learned, in one week, that its cheap strike complex aimed through an ally's targeting layer, and that the layer had an owner." Ruggie named that argument in 1993 as an error. Quoting Kapstein's remark that if the United States wished to stop satellite transmission "it could easily do so by shooting the satellite down", he replied: "There is an extraordinarily impoverished mind-set at work here, one that is able to visualize long-term challenges to the system of states only in terms of entities that are institutionally substitutable for the state" (p. 143). His demonstration is the medieval fair: "In no sense could the medieval trade fairs have become substitutes for the institutions of feudal rule. Yet, the fairs contributed significantly to the demise of feudal authority relations", and "local lords at any time could have closed down a fair in their domain … though other lords in other places probably would have been only too pleased to provide alternative sites" (p. 155). On Europe he is equally direct: the Community "may constitute the first 'multiperspectival polity' to emerge since the advent of the modern era", but "There is no indication, however, that this reimagining will result in a federal state of Europe" (p. 172).

Second, the spine's test. Spine section 7 — the spine being the book's internal doctrine document — attributes to Hedley Bull a test with a "new units" limb: whether the dispersing trends replaced sovereignty as the organising principle "or merely reproduced it in new units". Chapter 1 carries a standing marker, a [DELTA] flag, recording that this limb is a reconstruction from Bull's per-trend reasoning rather than a test he states. Ruggie's page 143 denies that substitutability is the right question at all.

Third, the origin story. Chapter 1, section I, presents the neomedieval literature as having "a founding text", and section III calls Bull "the founder first, doubting his own hypothesis". But Charles Tilly, writing in 1975, two years before *The Anarchical Society*, already had the thought and gave it a technological cause: the state "may be losing part of its significance", authority moving "toward the regional grouping and the compact of superstates above, toward the subnational region, ethnic population, or racial group below", and, flatly, "Perhaps, unknowing, we are writing obituaries for the state" (p. 638). Ruggie's own note 23 points further back, to John Herz's "Rise and Demise of the Territorial State" of 1957. Alongside this sits an attribution error. Chapter 1, section II, gives Perry Anderson (1974, pp. 37–38) both the phrase "patchwork of overlapping and incomplete rights of government" and the phrases that follow, and credits the canonisation to Ruggie 1993. Ruggie's own notes divide the sentence: "patchwork of overlapping and incomplete rights of government" is from Strayer and Munro, *The Middle Ages* (1959), p. 115; "inextricably superimposed and tangled" and the "plural allegiances, asymmetrical suzerainties and anomalous enclaves" clause are Anderson's; and the canonising article was Ruggie's 1983 piece in *World Politics*, which is what Kobrin and Zielonka both cite. Anderson's page 37 has not been seen directly, so it remains possible that he is himself quoting Strayer and Munro.

Fourth, the two centuries. Chapter 4 treats the Hanseatic Kontore — the League's overseas trading stations — and the other alternative polities without meeting Ruggie's strongest objection: "the Italian city-states and the Hanse, in fact were viable political alternatives to the territorial state, fully able to levy taxes and raise armies, for the better part of two centuries. In social life, two centuries is no mere time lag" (p. 156). He also supplies, from Mattingly, the phrase the Kontore have been waiting for — the new states "found they could only communicate with one another by tolerating within themselves little islands of alien sovereignty" (p. 165).

**Why it matters.** The four rows are of different weights. The Anderson repair is an error of fact in a reviewed chapter and must be made regardless. The spine correction costs one clause and removes an attribution a specialist can falsify. The origin story is a rhetorical convenience, and giving it up is a gain: the founder of the war-makes-states school predicted dispersal on technological grounds in 1975, so the book's inversion — the same family of technologies now re-concentrating — answers Tilly rather than merely correcting Bull. The off-switch is the large one. Chapter 12's whole opening week is the argument Ruggie discredited thirty years ago, and if the book does not answer him a reviewer who knows the literature will do it for you. The answer is available and cheap: Part I already concedes the platforms are not substitutes for the state; the book's claim is that a non-substitutable authority can nonetheless be subordinated, the switch being the instrument; and Ruggie's alternative sites are the point, because a fab is not a fair and the alternative sites now lie inside three or four commercial worlds. The two centuries are conceded just as cheaply, because they are the two centuries before the ratchet — the book's name for the step at which the price of decisive force rose past subordinate reach — with Ruggie's alternatives flourishing 1300–1500 and the mechanism running 1440–1500.

**Your choices.** Choice A is to answer Ruggie by name at every point. Chapter 12 carries the off-switch answer, placed at section II where the fairs are the natural material rather than in section I where the week works because it is concrete; "multiperspectival polity" is named in section IV as a failure mode and "no indication … federal state" as the null the European conditional scores against; the [DELTA] in Chapter 1 is closed with one clause saying the subordination test is the book's own, and spine section 7 is amended to match; Chapter 1 gives Tilly 1975 and Herz 1957 their priority and demotes Bull from founder to namer, with Herz entering Appendix A; the Anderson sentence is split as Ruggie's notes divide it, the canonisation re-pointed to 1983, and the commit held until Anderson's page 37 has been seen; Chapter 4 concedes the two centuries by name and dates them before the ratchet, with Mattingly's phrase in a note. The cost is perhaps 300 words across three chapters, a spine note, and one verification errand. It buys the book's answers on the record before the reviewer makes the objections, and it buys a better opening: the Tilly passage is stronger material than the sentence it replaces.

Choice B is the housekeeping only: the Anderson repair, the spine clause and the two-centuries concession are made, because each is a matter of fact; the off-switch answer goes into Chapter 12's critiques file rather than its text, and Bull stays the founder. This keeps Chapter 12 lean and saves the reader an excursion into theory. A reviewer would observe that the book's central contemporary exhibit is precisely the move Ruggie discredited and is not obliged to notice that the book has an answer.

**My view.** Choice A, with restraint in section I: the spine correction and the Anderson repair should happen whichever way you rule, and the off-switch answer is worth far more in the text than in a file no reviewer reads.

## Decision 20 — Borrowed terms, each paid for
*Covers rows: (ii), (pp), (qq), (ggg), (qqq), (sss), and the Schmitt/chokepoint half of (bbbb)*

**The background.** Seven small rows share one problem: the manuscript uses words that belong to named scholars without naming them, or names them at one remove. Each is cheap to fix and each is the kind of thing a doctrine that treats provenance seriously cannot leave.

The spine's third route to consolidation — absorption by alliance, the barons asked in rather than revoked — has no name from the literature. Stephen Krasner supplies one: rulers "can extend invitations, sometimes inadvertent, that result in compromises of their own Westphalian sovereignty" (1999, p. 20), and "more powerful states are unlikely to lose their Westphalian sovereignty unless they invite external authority through conventions or contractual arrangements" (p. 29). His invited party is a foreign sovereign, not a firm, so the borrowing is an extension and should say so.

Chapter 12, section IV, reads the Monroe Doctrine through Benjamin Bratton's gloss on Carl Schmitt. The original is now in the corpus: the Doctrine and the Western Hemisphere "encompass a space far exceeding the boundaries of the state proper — a Großraum in the sense of international law" (p. 281), and the three possibilities of a single world sovereign, a hegemonic balance, or "an equilibrium of several independent Großräume … rational, if the Großräume are differentiated meaningfully and are homogeneous internally" (pp. 354–55) — the last being the position your four-bloc prediction occupies. What travels with the citation is the provenance: Schmitt built the Großraum doctrine inside the Third Reich, in the 1939 *Völkerrechtliche Großraumordnung*, with the Reich as the intended ordering centre, and the 1950 *Nomos of the Earth* re-grounds it without confessing the lineage. Chapter 10 is a separate matter: Schmitt's account of where the state came from is a rival cause, the bracketed-war order arising "solely from the emergence of a new spatial order" (p. 140), with no cannon and no fisc in it, so he belongs in that chapter's critiques file and not its text.

Chapter 12 uses the off-switch and the map without their owners' names, and Chapter 10, section VIII, uses "chokepoints" as ordinary language. The terms are Henry Farrell and Abraham Newman's, from "Weaponized Interdependence" (*International Security*, 2019): the panopticon effect, by which states with jurisdiction over network hubs "can use this influence to obtain information passing through the hubs", and the chokepoint effect, "privileged states' capacity to limit or penalize use of hubs by third parties" (pp. 55–56). Their scope condition is your mechanism in modern dress: "States need both leverage over network hubs and appropriate institutions" (p. 75). Their objection — two decades of coercion through deputised private hands with no absorption — cannot be answered away and should be staged as a wager for Appendix C to score.

Chapter 5, section VII, calls the minority council's position "the dignified pretence". The word is Walter Bagehot's, from *The English Constitution* (1867): the "DIGNIFIED parts" that "excite and preserve the reverence of the population" against the "EFFICIENT parts — those by which it, in fact, works and rules" (PDF p. 21). His caveat — "I am not, of course, now speaking of the middle ages" (PDF p. 48) — must travel with any medieval use.

Chapter 3, section I, attributes to Charles Tilly "the protection-market frame". The market is Frederic Lane's; Tilly carries it and hardens it into a racket — "war making and state making — quintessential protection rackets with the advantage of legitimacy — qualify as our largest examples of organized crime". The chapter's conditional clause about weakening central capacity is the book's own and should be marked as such, and widened to cover capacity never formed. Chapter 3, section III, also refers to a "centuries-old taboo against mercenarism"; Ruggie, citing Thomson, dates it to the nation-state: "when the concept of state sovereignty expanded to become the concept of national sovereignty, the use of mercenaries in warfare declined and ultimately was eliminated altogether" (p. 163).

**Why it matters.** Individually these are footnotes. Together they decide whether a reviewer reads the book as commanding its field or as helping itself to it. Two carry real content: the Schmitt provenance, which omitted is the easiest available attack on Part III, and the mercenary date, which turns the return of contracted force from an aberration into a reversion.

**Your choices.** Choice A is to pay every debt as stated: "invitation" adopted with the extension acknowledged in a note; Schmitt cited directly for both passages with provenance and the substance disanalogy in one endnote, and kept out of Chapter 10's text; panopticon and chokepoint adopted by name with the deputisation objection staged as a wager; "dignified" attributed to Bagehot with his caveat in the note; Lane's market and Tilly's racket both named, with the conditional marked as yours; "centuries-old" cut and the taboo dated. This costs six or seven notes and a few reworded clauses, and buys every borrowed term cleanly acknowledged.

Choice B is to attribute only where the word is unavoidable and reword the rest: drop "invitation" for a plain description, replace "dignified pretence" with an unmarked phrase, keep "chokepoint" as ordinary English, and make only the Schmitt, Lane and mercenary corrections. This is cheaper by three notes and loses three genealogies that count in the book's favour, since Bagehot's distinction descends from the very fifteenth-century split Chapter 5 describes.

**My view.** Choice A, because each borrowed term arrives with a pedigree the book can use, and the notes cost less than the appearance of having coined freely.

## Decision 21 — The Alençon scene: what the crown took and what it left
*Covers rows: (gg)*

**The background.** Your earlier ruling, filed as ruling (v), asked the book to itemise what an over-mighty subject loses and what he keeps when the crown breaks him. Chapter 11, section VII, is the place a medieval instance was flagged and left empty: the draft carries a gap marker, and the flag beside it paraphrases what the scene must show, "the crown retaining the artillery and the named fortresses and preserving the family's other interests". The passage does not yet exist.

The evidence is at T1 — the book's label for a primary source in a scholarly edition. Georges Chastellain, the Burgundian court chronicler, transcribes the sentence passed on the Duke of Alençon on 10 October 1458 in the chancellor's own words. Of the condemned peer's movable goods the king left everything to wife and children, "réservé au roy l'artillerie, harnois et autres habillemens de guerre" — reserved to the king the artillery, armour and other equipment of war (tome III, p. 486). The crown took Alençon, Domfront and Verneuil into the royal domain, and left Perche to one son "sans toutevoies aucune dignité ou prérogative de parrie" — without, however, any dignity or prerogative of the peerage (p. 487). The line the sentence draws is between appanage and patrimony: an appanage is land granted by the crown to a royal prince, and reverts to the crown; patrimony is the family's own inherited property. The crown resumed the whole appanage and left the family what was its own — with a single exception. The guns were the duke's own movable property, and were taken anyway.

Two cautions attach. The instrument is judicial, not military; the moderation of the sentence was obtained at the Duke of Brittany's petition; and execution was merely deferred, "jusques à son bon plaisir" — until the king's good pleasure (p. 484). A sentence that a successor may set aside is weaker than a list of transfers suggests. The sentence is also silent on the operational knowledge that went with the train — the gunners and their craft — and saying so costs nothing.

**Why it matters.** This is a placement of evidence rather than a change of argument, but it gives section VII its strongest sentence: of everything a condemned peer owned, the one thing the crown would not leave in private hands was his artillery. It is the mechanism stated in the crown's own voice. The risk is in overstatement. A reviewer who knows the reign will point out that the sentence was reversible, and if the chapter has not said so first the scene reads as more than it is.

**Your choices.** Choice A is to write the scene with the appanage-versus-patrimony precision, keep the Old French "réservé au roy l'artillerie" in the text with a translation beside it, and state the reversibility caveat in the same paragraph. The chapter gains a T1 anchor for the ruling it already made, and the caveat becomes a disanalogy the book carries proudly — a consolidation a succession could undo — rather than one produced against it. The cost is a paragraph, and a small loss of the clean list the flag imagined.

## Decision 22 — The Lombards of Bruges, and the book's three words: command, dependence, capture
*Covers rows: (uuu), (aaaa)*

**The background.** On 14 September you ruled (decision 3, filed as row (x)) that the book should distinguish three ways a state can stand towards the people who supply what it cannot make for itself: command, where the state can simply order; dependence, where it needs the supplier and knows it; and capture, where the supplier has got inside the state's own decision-making. Chapter 10 section VIII is where that triad is to be defined, and at present the section carries only a placeholder marker for the ruling. The chapter's footer ledger, the short record at the end of every draft that counts the chapter's coined concepts, metaphors and aphorisms against the voice rules (at most one of each per chapter), currently reads "none coined".

Two things have since been found. The first is a scene. Georges Chastellain, the official chronicler of the Burgundian court, whose third volume is now in the repository in Kervyn de Lettenhove's edition, records at chapter LXI (tome III, pages 314 to 316) what happened at Bruges in 1457 when three Piedmontese banking tables fled by night with their depositors' money. The duke inventoried the houses, confiscated the deposits of burgesses who had lent at interest, fined them, and put every bank in his lands to composition. Then the sentence the book wants: the towns were long without any Lombards to lend, "là où le peuple toutevoies, selon la nature de luy ancienne, se peut mal passer de eux. Et par ainsi, le duc enfin estoit constraint de les y remettre par la nécessité publique" (page 316) — the people could ill do without them, and so the duke was in the end constrained to restore them by public necessity. This is a source of the highest grade the book uses, what the doctrine calls T1, a primary document in a scholarly edition. The assessment attaches two cautions. The punishment was for a failure and a flight, not for lending as such; and it was fiscal in form, the state profiting from the seizure before dependence forced the restoration. Wherever the scene goes, the extraction must travel with the dependence, or the passage flatters the state.

The second is a check on the words. Your voice rule of 1 August requires any coined concept to be tested against existing scholarship before the ledger claims it. Colin Crouch, the British political scientist whose 2000 Fabian pamphlet *Coping with Post-Democracy* is in the corpus, has two of the three in substance: dependence, where the state "loses touch with the knowledge necessary to understand certain activities" and "is therefore forced to sub-contract further" (Crouch, PDF page 17), and capture, where the state's policy-making machinery "has itself become endogenous to the problem of the power of the corporate elite". Row (uuu) also asks whether Carl Schmitt should stand beside Otto Brunner in chapter 8 section III. The assessment places Schmitt at footnote level in chapters 1 and 2 and out of chapter 10's text; adding him to chapter 8 buys a jurist's confirmation at the cost of a name whose provenance then has to be stated.

If the naval question is left unanswered in the very section that defines the mechanism's vocabulary, the chapter's one coinage rests on a claim its own cited authority contradicts.

**Your choices.** Choice A puts the Lombards at chapter 10 section VIII as the T1 anchor for dependence, claims the triad as the chapter's one coinage with the pedigree paid, and answers the naval question in the same section. The ledger line says plainly that dependence and capture have Crouch's pedigree and command is yours; "chokepoint" is footnoted to Farrell and Newman; chapter 8 section VII carries one cross-referring clause with the Burgundy caution, that here was a prince who enforced without a permanent fisc and financed the enforcement from the seizure; and one sentence concedes that the navy is a second form of the capital-intensity mechanism. The cost is a fuller section and a ledger line that admits two-thirds of the triad is inherited. What it buys is a phrase the century itself supplies, "nécessité publique", two hundred years before the Stationers or the Arsenal, and a coinage claim no reviewer can undo.

Choice B puts the Lombards in chapter 8 instead, at section V or section VII, as a case of enforcement, and leaves chapter 10 to define the triad without a fifteenth-century anchor in the same place. Chapter 8 is about enforcement, and a duke who enforces and then retreats is a genuine instance for it. The cost is that the scene becomes one more example in a chapter that has several, and that the definition in chapter 10 loses its best witness. A reviewer would not fault it, but the argument would be weaker where it needs to be strongest.

This is the safest position on originality and the weakest on force; the book's predictive vocabulary would then belong, on paper, to someone else.

On Schmitt in chapter 8 section III, the sub-choice is simply to add him or omit him; adding him costs a provenance note, omitting him costs nothing.

**My view.** Choice A, and omit Schmitt from chapter 8: the honest ledger line is the whole point of the 1 August rule, and the phrase at page 316 is worth more where the words are defined than where they are merely illustrated. This is a view.

## Decision 29 — Cadillac and Castillon, as Leseur has them
*Covers rows: L-a, L-b, L-c, L-d, L-e, L-f, L-g, L-h, L-i, L-j; the chapter 6 [RE-CHECK AT PRESS] flag on Cadillac; executes your "resolve" ruling of 16 September on row R7*

**The background.** On 16 September you ruled one word on row R7, "Cadillac's storming": resolve. The row asked whether chapter 6 could keep calling Cadillac "the only storming in the record", and your answer was that the claim stands only once the sources have been settled. The sources have now been read, and this decision is the execution of that ruling. What made it possible is Guillaume Leseur's *Histoire de Gaston IV, comte de Foix*, a fifteenth-century chronicle written by the count's household servant of thirty years, in Henri Courteault's two-volume edition of 1893 to 1896. It was sitting in the repository and entered nowhere. In the book's grading it is T1, a primary source in a scholarly edition; Courteault's notes beneath it are T2, the work of a modern scholar, and in places the more useful half.

Chapter 6, section VI, currently says of the 1453 mopping-up that "Cadillac, held against the king after the revolt, is the only place in the whole record taken by storm through a breach", that the storm was "a bombardment wearing an assault's clothes", and that "it was reserved for a place under punitive terms, whose captain the king had executed", with a flag recording that Michael Vale dates the execution 17 September and the chronicler Escouchy dates the storm 19 September. Leseur adjudicates this. The town was carried by storm after Jean Bureau, the crown's master of artillery, "faisoit tyrer l'artillerie jour et nuyt, tant la grosse que la menue" — kept the guns firing night and day, great and small (tome II, p. 24). The castle composed the next day. The garrison were taken prisoner, except the captain, Gaillardet, who "se rendit à la volunté de mondit sieur de Foix" — surrendered to the count of Foix's will — and whom the count "pour ses desmerites luy fist trencher la teste comme à ung traystre", had beheaded as a traitor for his misdeeds, because he was the count's own perjured liegeman (tome II, pp. 25–26). Courteault adds that at the moment Cadillac capitulated "Charles VII était devant Bordeaux" (p. 22 n. 2). So Escouchy is right on the mode and the date and wrong on the command: Courteault says of his attributing the siege to Dunois, "c'est une erreur" (p. 22 n. 2). Hall's 27 September, which the evidence file had treated as a third date for Cadillac, is the fall of Bénauges, a different place. And Vale's sentence, "on his arrival at the place, they surrendered", is the reverse of what stands at the page he cites: tome II, p. 19 carries the aftermath of Castillon, and on Gaston's arrival Cadillac did not surrender. No date for the storm appears anywhere in Leseur's narrative, which is why the flag can be narrowed but not closed: the 17 September date stays for press.

Leseur also corrects and adds around Castillon itself. The draft says "The town of Castillon surrendered two days later under the continuing fire"; Leseur has "troys jours après" (tome II, p. 19), and Courteault's note dates the capitulation to 20 July, three days after the battle of the 17th. The draft attributes the false report that drew Talbot out of Bordeaux to camp-followers on the move; Leseur has the garrison inside the town send to Talbot that the French were loading their guns to flee (p. 16), and Courteault prefers his version to Escouchy's (p. 14 n. 2). And Leseur, who was not at the battle, credits the decision to a charge of fresh Breton cavalry after an hour of hand-to-hand fighting, not to the guns (pp. 17–18); the intake grades this a real qualification the chapter must carry in the text rather than in a note. Three further passages are on offer for other sections: Dunois's empty field at Fronsac in 1451, held all day for an English relief that never came, after which "toutes les villes et places, réservé Bayonne" composed (tome I, p. 205); Bureau and the finance general Le Boursier left behind to begin the citadels of Hâ and Trompette at Bordeaux (tome II, p. 29), with the royal muster and 10,000 francs paid to the count of Foix's private levy from Courteault's notes (p. 8 n. 1); and the storm and sack of Blaye in 1451 (tome I, p. 202). Two cautions travel with all of it. Courteault calls Leseur a panegyrist who "grossit volontiers les chiffres", inflates figures readily, and says no chronicler stands in greater need of correction from the documents. And Courteault's own notes disagree about which Cadillac is meant, the one on the Garonne or the one in the Fronsadais; that question is newly open.

**Why it matters.** This is a medium point with one large consequence. The chapter's census of 1449 to 1453 rests on the claim that places fell to composition, not to storm, and Cadillac is its stated single exception; a hostile reviewer who opened Leseur would find the exception mis-commanded, mis-dated and mis-executed in the draft, and would use it to doubt the census. The Breton charge is the sharper danger: the book's cleanest exhibit of artillery decisiveness has a primary witness who gives the day to cavalry, and a chapter that does not say so invites the accusation that it read only the sources that agreed with it. Everything else here is precision the chapter gains for nothing.

**Your choices.** Choice A rewrites the Cadillac paragraph around Leseur's sequence: the town stormed after battery night and day under Bureau, the castle composing the next day, the captain beheaded by Gaston IV for perjured homage; the sentence "whose captain the king had executed" corrected in the text, not silently in a note; the "three accounts set down as they stand" display demoted to a note with Courteault's "c'est une erreur" quoted there; the Castillon surrender corrected to three days; the false report rewritten to name two traditions, camp-followers and garrison, with the gap flag closed on that basis; the Breton charge stated in one sentence in the dissent paragraph beside Howard, DeVries and Hall; the Fronsac empty field entering section V in summary with one short quotation; Blaye's sack stated in section V with a clause noting the census is Normandy-scoped; and Leseur named in the text once, as the fullest narrative of the Gascon campaign and not as an authority. The Hâ-and-Trompette commission and the Foix muster go to chapter 7. This costs a rewritten paragraph, perhaps two hundred words across the chapter, and one small loss of rhetorical force, because "the king had executed" was doing the work of showing the crown's punitive reach and Gaston's execution of his own vassal does that work less directly. It buys a settled exception, a closed gap, and a dissent the chapter owns before a reviewer produces it.

Choice B takes the corrections that cannot be avoided and holds the rest. The executioner, the three days and the false report are corrected; Leseur enters as a fourth voice inside the existing three-account display rather than replacing it; the Breton charge, Fronsac, Blaye and the naming of Leseur are deferred until the Escouchy re-check and the second-Cadillac question are settled. This is cheaper and keeps the chapter's present shape. The cost is that the display the chapter calls "three accounts set down as they stand" would now stand beside an editor's flat verdict that one of them is wrong, and the Breton charge would remain uncarried in a chapter whose intake says it must be carried.

**My view.** Choice A, with one exception: keep Leseur out of the running text if you find naming a panegyrist beside Basin and Escouchy crowds the section, and let the note carry him. The Breton charge is the item I would not defer under any choice. This is a view.

## Decision 30 — Belloc and the knock-out blow, as Holman has them

**Why it matters.** A small point in the argument and a real one in the manners. The mirror is a closing flourish, not a load-bearing claim, but a reviewer who knows Holman would see a hedge silently removed, a British finding stated as a century's, and Douhet implied where Holman's whole book argues against him. Citing at second hand what is now on the shelf would be noticed. The interdependence point cuts the other way and helps: it is the book's mechanism arriving from the defending side, and it is Holman's finding rather than the book's imposition.

**Your choices.** Choice A cites Holman 2014: 23 directly and drops the Freedman relay, Freedman staying only for the Tokyo exchange of 1941; restores the hedge, so the sentence reads "had almost become an orthodoxy" or "a near-orthodoxy"; qualifies the phrase to interwar Britain in the text; names Grahame-White and Harper, Groves and Baldwin in the note with the Douhet caution beside them; adds one clause carrying Holman's diagnosis, interdependence over morale, at the cost of a longer sentence in a paragraph the voice ledger already counts as dense; reserves the Smuts-to-RAF pin and the 1940 administrative resolution for chapter 12, where the process point is at home; and states the negative finding on Belloc in the chapter's apparatus, in one sentence, so that a reader who checks finds the book checked first. Choice B does the same but keeps the genealogy's names and the Douhet caution out of the chapter entirely, in sources.md only, and leaves the Belloc finding there too. It costs less space and one fewer note; a reviewer would then find the negative finding in the working files and not on the page, which is defensible but not generous.

**My view.** Choice A; the clause on interdependence is the only item that costs anything, and it is worth the length because it turns a decorative mirror into a second instance of the mechanism. This is a view.

A reviewer who knows Finer would notice the first; the second strengthens the book's own discipline.

**Your choices.** Choice A takes both: the composite restated on effectiveness, expense and loyalty, and Brandenburg added at footnote length. Choice B takes the chapter 3 restatement and declines Brandenburg, leaving section VI at its present number, on the ground that a case without guns tests the fisc and not the mechanism.

**My view.** Choice A; the Brandenburg footnote is the cheapest honest test the section can carry. This is a view.
