## 1. The article in two paragraphs

The thesis is that neomedievalism and Varoufakis's technofeudalism are
complementary descriptive frameworks for internet governance, useful within
limits. Neomedievalism supplies overlapping authority — "the increasing
resemblance of contemporary global politics to the Middle Ages' fragmented and
overlapping power structures, challenging the Westphalian system of sovereign
nation-states that has prevailed since the 17th century (Bull 1977: 266)"
(p. 3) — and technofeudalism supplies hierarchy: "The notion of technofeudalism
described by Varoufakis is in line with the neomedievalist vision of a return to
a more fragmented and hierarchical social order… the technofeudal order brought
back digital serfdom" (p. 11). The connective claim is the transfer: "The link
between technofeudalism and neomedievalism also lies in this shift of power
dynamics from the state to private companies" (p. 5).

The analytical core is borrowed from Alfred C. Yen's 2002 metaphor study:
feudalism's two relevant features are "firstly, the intertwining of private and
public power, resulting from the chain of feudal power; and, secondly, the
fragmentation of government" (p. 7), with ICANN and ISOC as "the modern-day
equivalent of feudal monarchs," ISPs as "today's landlords," and terms of
service as the "'constitution'" (p. 7). The essay then treats cyber
sovereignty, the splinternet and filter bubbles, and closes on a prescription:
all three players in the "'platform governance triangle' (Gorwa 2024: 27) –
governments, service providers and users – must participate in the development
of a regulation and a modus vivendi acceptable to all, otherwise the digital
dark ages may come sooner than expected" (p. 11).

## 2. What it CONFIRMS

Nothing at a tier the manuscript may lean on; no sentence of any draft should
acquire it as support. Three convergences, recorded as corroboration of
register only. Ch01 §IV's reading that the territorialising pressure is
returning: the article's own splinternet section concedes the direction —
"State-controlled communication is a sign of modernity and, besides the
alarming effect, it also could be described as a **counter-trend to aspects of
feudalisation**" (p. 9) — and its verdict on cyber-libertarianism, "it is almost
certain by now that the wish for a lawless cyberspace in that famous manifesto
was misplaced" (p. 10). Ch02 §I's ecclesial reading of platform adjudication:
the Oversight Board's foundational documents "contain only distant, high-level
abstract objectives on human rights, without any reference to specific
international, national or regional legal provisions," human-rights standards
entering only "as a tertiary 'source of law', following the guidelines and
values of Meta and the precedent of previous decisions" (p. 6, on Laurence and
Land 2023: 2247 and Charter § 2(2)) — cite those direct, never this. Ch02
§III's double subjection: "much like medieval subjects who owed allegiance to
multiple lords and institutions at the same time" (p. 5).

## 4. What it ADDS

Leads, and one usable concession. **Retrieval leads the book lacks:** Jakob
Linaa Jensen, *The Medieval Internet* (Emerald, 2020), quoted at p. 2 —
medieval "terms and concepts … are suitable for describing and discussing a
plethora of social and political phenomena" of digital media; Yen's "Western
frontier or feudal society?", *Berkeley Technology Law Journal* 17:4 (2002),
1207–63, and his revisit in *IDEA* 60:1 (2020), 134–48 — the law-side genealogy
Appendix A has no entry for; Morozov, "Critique of techno-feudal reason," *NLR*
133–134 (2022) — wanted by ch12's Varoufakis objection; Minc, *Le nouveau moyen
âge* (1993), presently in the book only through Cerny's note on *zones grises*;
Gilpin, *Global Political Economy* (2001), 379, quoted at p. 4 as the camp's
hostile summariser — "New medievalists believe that the end of national
sovereignty and the resulting diffusion of power will enable selfless
non-governmental organizations (NGOs) to solve the world's pressing … problems";
Kelemen's two neomedievalism pieces (2019, 2020).

**The five disanalogies, volunteered** (p. 8): "However, the metaphor is not
perfect. Firstly, the physically violent problem-solving that characterises
feudal systems is (fortunately) not typical of cyberspace; secondly, the social
system based on birthright privileges is not as extensive; thirdly, the physical
vulnerability of the masses is far from being as widespread as in feudal
Europe…" Useful to ch01 in one way: the first disanalogy is this book's whole
subject. The metaphor's own users notice that violence is missing and treat the
absence as a comfort; the book treats it as the reason the vocabulary predicts
nothing.

## 5. What it CONTRADICTS

**At full strength.** Its terminus is a stable, locked dispersal, governed by
negotiation and not by a re-consolidating state: "Varoufakis and Yen warned
that, without intervention, this emerging technofeudalism would consolidate
into a new, immobile social order in which a small elite controls the digital
means of production, reminiscent of the feudal lords who controlled the land and
its crops" (p. 11) — the consolidation being by the platforms, the only exit
"collaborative, multi-stakeholder approaches that account for the plurality of
power, authority and free speech in the digital ecosystem" (p. 11). Beneath it,
sovereignty is left undecided: Yen's third revision asks "whether the new
landlords are really the ISPs or the states themselves" and leaves it open
(p. 8); users act "within environments controlled by corporate interests and
growing state control" (p. 8); the state's regulatory turn is read as
fragmentation, not re-assertion. That is CLAUDE.md §1 claim 1's error —
dispersal treated as stable rather than transitional — in 2025 dress, with a
technofeudal floor under it.

**The book's best answer.** The article's own evidence is against its terminus:
it reports states compelling ISPs to hand over data, China and Russia building
sovereign internets, the EU's regulatory race, the ECtHR adjudicating platform
conduct, and concedes the "counter-trend to aspects of feudalisation" (p. 9).
What it lacks is any variable for capability — and the variable the book runs on
is precisely the one its disanalogy list brackets. The platforms in this essay
hold attention, contract terms and moderation queues; the platforms in ch02 §III
and Part III hold foundries, constellations and models. Rent is negotiable;
decisive force is not. **Honest grade: the answer is strong and cheaply won.**
The contradiction is not a serious threat, because the terminus is asserted in a
closing paragraph on the authority of Varoufakis and Yen, neither of whom is
tested in the piece. The book's real opponent here remains Varoufakis at ch12
critiques Revisions 4; this essay is his transmission, not his reinforcement. No
spine ruling and no DEFEND-tier claim is touched.

## 7. Verdict

It changes neither direction nor register and adds almost nothing to weight;
what it changes is the book's evidence about its own field — that in 2025 the
neomedieval vocabulary travels fast enough to fuse with technofeudalism in a
peer-adjacent venue while still carrying no mechanism, no fiscal variable and no
falsifiable prediction. The single most important thing for the author to know
is that this article is the book's market rather than its rival: careful lawyers
reach for the Middle Ages because the vocabulary is available, then document the
state's return in their own splinternet section without noticing they have
described a counter-trend.

On the fairyland question the finding is mixed and should be carried in both
halves. It does **not** commit the error in Holsinger's strong sense, because it
never converts resemblance into cause: it declares itself a study of metaphors
("Metaphors can be 'useful tools that facilitate understanding and, therefore,
have cognitive value'", p. 6), volunteers five disanalogies (p. 8), and quotes
Eco's own warning that the public "is not interested in medieval facts and
historical background but, instead, uses it as a kind of mythological stage for
the characters of the present" (p. 4). And it is nevertheless the best available
exhibit of what Holsinger and Rengger object to: its Middle Ages is an
undifferentiated "feudalism" sourced to no medievalist and entered through *Game
of Thrones* viewing figures (p. 2). The charge has honest targets; the price of
their honesty is that the vocabulary predicts nothing. That is the contrast Part
I exists to draw.
