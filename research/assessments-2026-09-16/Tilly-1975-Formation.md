## 1. The volume in two paragraphs

The book is the product of a 1970 workshop of the SSRC Committee on Comparative Politics, and its governing decision is stated as a bias: "The singling out of the organization of armed forces, taxation, policing, the control of food supply, and the formation of technical personnel stresses activities which were difficult, costly, and often unwanted by large parts of the population. All were essential to the creation of strong states" (p. 6). Its method is relationships rather than sequences — "none of us thinks the European experience will repeat itself as a set of events or sequences. Yet all of us are willing to entertain the hypothesis that some of the relationships which showed up there are quite general" (p. 17) — and its most quoted sentence closes a paragraph about war as a survival condition: "The building of an effective military machine imposed a heavy burden on the population involved: taxes, conscription, requisitions, and more. The very act of building it—when it worked—produced arrangements which could deliver resources to the government for other purposes. (Thus almost all the major European taxes began as 'extraordinary levies' earmarked for particular wars, and became routine sources of governmental revenue.) It produced the means of enforcing the government's will over stiff resistance: the army. It tended, indeed, to promote territorial consolidation, centralization, differentiation of the instruments of government and monopolization of the means of coercion, all the fundamental state-making processes. **War made the state, and the state made war**" (p. 42).

Finer's ch. 2 is the volume's military chapter and the one this book must reckon with. He relates "the format of the armed forces" — service basis, size, arm composition ("navy against land forces or artillery components against infantry or cavalry and the like"), social stratification (p. 87) — to state-building through six named cycles, of which the decisive one is extraction-coercion: "Troops extract the taxes or the forage or the carts, and this contribution keeps them in being. More troops—more extraction—more troops: so a cycle of this kind could go on widening and deepening. This is precisely what happened in Brandenburg-Prussia from 1653 onward, and in large measure accounts for the state-building process in that country" (p. 93). Ardant's ch. 3 supplies the constraint that gates the whole scheme — "Each increase in the power of states was linked to an increased possibility of levying taxes" (p. 174) — and Braun's ch. 4 the two branches of the outcome: Britain, where "it was the business of Parliament alone … to finance the government and to guarantee the 'national debts'" (p. 297), and Brandenburg-Prussia, where "the monarch and his bureaucratic machinery were forced to rely as much as possible on sources of income which were at their 'own' disposal and not dependent, as far as collection and allocation were concerned, upon the consent and cooperation of the people" (p. 296).

## 2. What it CONFIRMS

**The ratchet — in Tilly's own 1975 words, and the book has never cited it.** Ch. 10 §III's whole argument about a machine that outlives the assembly which fed it has its T2 statement here: "Up to our own time dramatic increases in national budgets, national debts, numbers of governmental employees or any other indicator of governmental scale in European countries have occurred almost exclusively as a consequence of preparations for war. **The general rule, furthermore, has been for some contraction in governmental scale to occur after a war—but almost never a return to the prewar scale.** Preparation for war has been the great state-building activity" (p. 74). Note the collision already recorded at ch10/sources.md (Tilly 1990: 89, the Peacock–Wiseman "ratchet effect"): the 1975 volume states the effect without the name.

**The chain, numbered.** "Recurrently we find a chain of causation running from (1) change or expansion in land armies to (2) new efforts to extract resources from the subject population to (3) the development of new bureaucracies and administrative innovations to (4) resistance from the subject population to (5) renewed coercion to (6) durable increases in the bulk or extractiveness of the state" (p. 73). CLAUDE.md §2's chain is links (1)–(3) with a technology at the front; the rest is Tilly's.

**§8(a)'s object — the closure of everyone else's ability to reverse it.** "Before the period of state preeminence, power-wielding deliberative assemblies were acting at all levels of political life … Such assemblies normally had some control of taxation, which typically took the form of an extraordinary grant for a specific purpose. Building strong royal power meant co-opting, subordinating or destroying these institutions" (p. 22); and, against the Whig reading, "Far from promoting such institutions, early state-makers struggled against them" (p. 37), with Guénée's fourteenth-century prelude — "representative assemblies had their first great successes, and the peoples of Europe seized their most handsome rights from their princes … After that came the reflux" (Guénée 1971: 405, quoted pp. 37–38).

**Ch. 10 §VI, the counter-cases.** "Most of the European efforts to build states failed. The enormous majority of the political units which were around to bid for autonomy and strength in 1500 disappeared in the next few centuries, smashed or absorbed by other states-in-the-making" (p. 38); "The Europe of 1500 included some five hundred more or less independent political units, the Europe of 1900 about twenty-five" (p. 15). And the six survival conditions — "(1) the availability of extractible resources; (2) a relatively protected position in time and space; (3) a continuous supply of political entrepreneurs; (4) success in war; (5) homogeneity (and homogenization) of the subject population; (6) strong coalitions of the central power with major segments of the landed elite" (p. 40) — are a checklist §VI's four-polity set can be scored against; Burgundy is in it by name, as the case where (3) failed: "One can make a case that the mediocrity of the later dukes of Burgundy destroyed the real opportunity for a Burgundian kingdom to the northeast of France" (p. 41).

**Ch. 10 §III's fiscal machinery, from Ardant.** "in the early period of state-making the most important single spur to the expansion and reorganization of officialdom was very likely the effort to collect taxes from a recalcitrant population; the less extensive the market, the more heterogeneous the population and the greater the demand for revenues relative to total production … the greater bulk of the governmental apparatus created in the process" (Tilly summarising Ardant, p. 55).

**Ch. 03 §I.** Tilly's own account of the pre-state condition supports the chapter's frame without the conditional it currently carries: rulers faced rivals "even within the territories he nominally controlled. Landlords who exercised nearly autonomous control of their own estates (and thereby of most of the resources necessary for the waging of war, the maintenance of courts, and other stately tasks) rivaled and resisted the princes at the local level" (p. 76); and Finer's knight is the priced instrument — "mail armor alone weighed thirty pounds and cost the equivalent of a small farm … It would be like expecting private individuals nowadays to help form the army by bringing to the battlefield their own Centurion or Patton tank, or their own Phantom plane" (p. 89, repeated p. 100).

## 3. What it CORRECTS

1. **The slogan's pin.** ch10/sources.md and critiques Objection 6 record the slogan as needing a page-level cite. It is p. 42, in the paragraph quoted in §1 above, whose subject is *survival in war*, not a general theory of the state. Anyone quoting it must carry the sentence before it: "most of the political units which disappeared perished in war" (p. 42). Cite as Tilly 1975a: 42 (the volume's ch. 1), distinct from Tilly 1975b (ch. 9, at ch10/sources.md).
2. **Finer's Castillon and Formigny dates are transposed.** Finer p. 104: "Cannon proved critical in the French victories of Castillon (1450) and Formigny (1453)". Formigny was 1450 and Castillon 1453, as ch06 has them. Never cite Finer for these dates; cite him only for the causal claim in the same sentence.
3. **Finer carries the 1439 taille tradition that ch10 §III has already downgraded to (c).** "the Estates had in 1439 voted Charles VII the taille, and he acted as though this was for his entire reign" (p. 127). This is the textbook sentence the draft calls "a tradition, held at tertiary strength"; a 1975 T2 chapter repeating it raises its visibility, not its grade — and Major 1960 (assessed this run) says it "contains scarcely a word of truth" (Major, p. 33). Finer is superseded here; say so.
4. **Finer on 1484 is wrong in the same sentence.** "the Estates of Charles VIII had in 1484 given him enough to go on his Italian campaign" (p. 127). The Italian campaign is 1494; Major pp. 100, 115 has the 1484 grant at 1,200,000 livres for two years, breached by the council within months.
5. **ch10/critiques.md Objection 6's "Tilly was deliberately technology-agnostic" fails a second time, differently.** Memo Revisions 34 already killed it on the 1985 essay. The 1975 volume kills it again from the editor's chair: Tilly's own summary of Finer says the papers show "an alternation of long pauses with giant steps closely following changes in military technology and the scale of war" (p. 52). The editor of the bellicist canon put a punctuated technological account of state-building in his volume at chapter two and endorsed it in chapter one.

## 4. What it ADDS

**(a) Braun p. 296 — the T2 sentence spine §8(g) item 4 needs, at full strength.** §8(g) item 4 holds that a state financed by stack rents rather than by bargained broad taxation gets consolidation without the representation-and-rights by-products. Braun states the mechanism for the first run, and states it as a *choice made by rulers who understood it*: "In order to maintain such a sociopolitical power structure the monarch and his bureaucratic machinery were forced to rely as much as possible on sources of income which were at their 'own' disposal and not dependent, as far as collection and allocation were concerned, upon the consent and cooperation of the people" (p. 296). He grounds it in a number — in 1778–79 the Prussian crown's own properties "still amounted to 12 million thalers or 45.7 percent of the total net income", while "In Britain, after the seventeenth century, the income from the Crown's domain was insignificant in relation to total income" (p. 295) — and in Stein's doctrine, quoted by Schmoller: "The income from the domains is the economic foundation of the sovereign kingdom and therefore of independent internal and external state-building, because the Crown's domain is the foundation of the material independence of kings against the dominion and power of the strong corporations of the estates" (Schmoller 1877: 71, quoted p. 296). The obverse, also Braun's: "the more advanced states are, the more deeply they are in debt without being insolvent" (p. 295). Placement: ch12 §VII and Appendix C, by name, with Tilly 1990: 207–08.

**(b) Finer p. 138 — the euthanasia of the estates, which is ch10 §III's "It was simply not consulted" in a second polity.** The Great Elector's excise, made compulsory in the towns in 1680, was "indefinitely expansible", and therefore "the Elector no longer needed to call the Estates together for further increases in taxation. With the new revenue, the bureaucracy, the standing army, the eclipse of the Estates and the extinction of local self-government, the Elector found himself absolute!" Finer's word for it is exact: "Not only did the introduction of the excise erode and then destroy municipal self-government, it led to the euthanasia of the Estates" (p. 138). And the comparative punchline: "in England, 1640 had seen the Parliament assert itself against its king and in 1688, the Glorious Revolution put the seal upon its triumph. In Brandenburg-Prussia in 1640 the noble-dominated Estates had ruled the roost … But in 1688 this same Elector saw his Estates broken, as in Cléves, or extinguished, as in Brandenburg and Prussia" (p. 140). This is the comparative-register comparator ch10 §VI lacks for the *fiscal* leg: a polity where the tax made the assembly redundant without any artillery in the story at all.

**(c) Ardant — the in-kind ceiling, for ch10 §III and ch11 §IV.** Ardant's three obstacles are insufficient production, insufficient markets, and the impossibility of assessment (pp. 174–82). The second is the one ch11's timar argument needs: "The net product in itself was not adequate to pay the tax; it had to be transformed, by being sold, into money … For the taxpayer the problem of taxation was the problem of markets" (p. 176). The third is the one that explains why a coin-short state pays its cavalry in kind: "in the primitive economies of the European old regime … each farmer tried to produce all his needs, where he baked his bread, ate the meat, spun the wool from his herd, wove his clothes, pressed his grapes, built his furniture, and made his tools. Where could taxation intervene in this tight economic circle, the shortest of all economic cycles?" (pp. 178–79) — with salt as the one exception, "being physiologically necessary for all men … the weak link in peasant autarchy" (p. 180). And the finding that the in-kind solution kept coming back: "one of the most characteristic pieces of evidence of the obstacles which the economic structure offered to the fiscal possibilities and life of states; i.e., the stubbornness with which this archaic tax kept being reconsidered" (p. 182, on the tithe). Ardant also names France's answer and its cost: "The Emphasis on Constraint. This was the typical political solution of the French monarchy, but we find the same kind of solution in other states, especially Spain" (p. 194), from which the intendant and administrative justice descend (p. 195).

**(d) Tilly p. 36 — what the state-makers actually did, for ch10 §I's "subordinating, not eliminating".** "The European national revolutions of the last few centuries did not so much expand political rights as concentrate them in the state and reduce their investment in other sorts of governments. A large part of the process consisted of the state's abridging, destroying or absorbing rights previously lodged in other political units: manors, communities, provinces, estates" (p. 37). "Abridging, destroying or absorbing" is a better three-verb formula than either "suppressing" or "subordinating", and it is Tilly's.

**(e) Tilly p. 27 — the four processes, for ch12 and the Coda.** The national state "differed from these alternative possibilities in several significant ways: (1) it controlled a well-defined, continuous territory; (2) it was relatively centralized; (3) it was differentiated from other organizations; (4) it reinforced its claims through a tendency to acquire a monopoly over the concentrated means of physical coercion within its territory" (p. 27) — with the four alternatives that lost: empire, theocratic federation, "an intensive trading network without large-scale, central political organization", and the persistence of the feudal structure (p. 26). Part III's blocs, platforms and interstices map onto that list of losers, and Chapter 1 can say so.

**(f) Finer's format-options grid, for ch03 and ch12.** Three formats — foreign paid volunteers, native obligatory service, native paid volunteers — each in an *ad hoc* or permanent form, chosen on three considerations: "The considerations are three. The first is the effectiveness of the force … then the next consideration is—can such a force, of such a size be afforded? This is the consideration of its expense. But there is also a third consideration and this is the one that Machiavelli dwelt upon. Given such and such a format, would it be loyal to the ruler?" (pp. 91, 95). Effectiveness, expense, loyalty is a clean three-variable frame for ch12's absorption question.

**(g) Finer p. 99, for ch10 §II.** "The first standing force of an exclusively 'national' kind, i.e., conceived as owning unique and perpetual allegiance to the nascent state vise the person of its ruler(s), was the French Compagnies d'Ordonnance, set up by Charles VII in 1445"; restated at p. 125, "France had the first corps of a regular standing army as early as 1445." A 1975 T2 statement of the draft's priority claim, from a comparativist with no French axe.

## 5. What it CONTRADICTS

**(i) Finer already has a military-technological account of European state-building, punctuated and priced — and Tilly endorsed it in ch. 1.** At FULL STRENGTH: Finer's first cycle is "economy-technology-format"; Tilly's summary is that Finer "shows how each major military invention over a thousand years (most of the earlier 'inventions' being organizational and tactical rather than technical in any narrow sense) drove those rulers who wished to continue the pursuit of their objectives by force of arms to push their subject populations harder and more continuously for the necessary resources. Those who succeeded used their armed strength to consolidate their control of a territorial base … Many failed; their states or protostates fell into rebellion, disintegration, or absorption by others. For the survivors, instead of a continuous accretion of central power, we witness an alternation of long pauses with giant steps closely following changes in military technology and the scale of war" (pp. 51–52). Finer periodises by weapon — "The Age of Pike and Handgun: 1450-1550" — and puts the gun and its counter in one sentence: "Heavy artillery for siege, and somewhat lighter though very ponderous guns for battle were fast coming into general use … To counter the new artillery, the tall, thin walls of the medieval castle had to be replaced or girdled by low and thick ones, forerunners of the bastion" (p. 105). He gives cannon the decisive victories — "Cannon proved critical in the French victories of Castillon (1450) and Formigny (1453) which ejected the English from all France excepting only Calais" — and he draws the constitutional conclusion: "Castillon and Formigny—though this was not realized at the time—expelled England from Europe for good" (p. 104). He then states the French fiscal-constitutional outcome directly: "For a number of reasons the Estates-General had failed to make good a claim like the English Parliament's, to control taxation … in sum there was no central constitutional organ of opposition to taxation by fist similar to England's and consequently money for the wars could be found more easily in France than across the Channel. If the taxpayer resisted, the troops would make short work of that" (p. 127). Weapon → format → extraction → style-of-rule, with 1450 as the hinge and France as the case, in a 1975 volume the book already cites. **If the ratchet is already in Finer, what is left?**

*The book's best answer.* Four things are left, and only four; the honest grade is **B, not A**, and the claim of novelty must be narrowed accordingly.

First, **the explanandum is the state, not the format.** Finer's dependent variable is the military format and its correlate style-of-rule; his account is *reciprocal by design* — "At any fixed point of time, both have exerted a reciprocal influence on each other" (p. 87) — and Tilly is careful to say "Finer is no narrow technological determinist. He sees the political effects of military reorganization as conditioned by the extent and accessibility of resources within the subject population, on the one hand, the degree of popular commitment to the national cause, on the other" (p. 52). Finer's giant steps are steps in the size and permanence of forces. This book's claim is about the *irreversibility of a fiscal-constitutional settlement* and the *closure of the sub-state path to decisive force* — neither of which Finer names as an outcome to be explained. That is a real difference of object, not a difference of emphasis.

Second, **Finer's own European chronology is Prussian, not French.** His demonstration case for the extraction-coercion cycle is "Brandenburg-Prussia from 1653 onward" (p. 93); his increase-in-size finding is explicit that "the sharp increases come, in every case, after the close of the Thirty Years War, in 1648" (p. 101); his France reaches format-extraction-style alignment only under Louis XIV — "By 1715 format, i.e., the (regular standing army), extraction, and style-of-rule (absolutism)—all mutually supported one another" (p. 134). The 1445 companies and the 1450/1453 cannon are two paragraphs in a nine-hundred-year narrative whose weight sits after 1648. What Finer supplies for the fifteenth century is an assertion; what this book supplies is the account books, the Bureaux, the 1442 establishment document, Normandy and Guyenne, the Praguerie and the Public Weal. Finer's own French paragraph carries two datable errors (§3 above) and rests on the taille tradition Major destroyed in the same decade. The book extends a claim Finer made carelessly and could not have defended.

Third, **the ratchet's object is stated here and nowhere in Finer.** Finer's cycle explains why rulers extracted more. Spine §8(a) claims something narrower and harder: that what the guns of 1449–53 made irreversible was *everyone else's ability to reverse it*. Finer's Prussia furnishes the closest analogue — the excise that was "indefinitely expansible" and so produced "the euthanasia of the Estates" (p. 138) — and the analogue is instructive precisely because it is *fiscal-technical, not military-technical*: Finer's estates die of a tax instrument, not of a weapon. The book's claim is that in the French case a weapon did the same work on the same organ, and that is a claim Finer does not make and the volume does not contain.

Fourth, **Finer and Ardant disagree about what the book calls the mechanism, and the disagreement is the book's opening.** Ardant's first obstacle is a ceiling — "Productivity, especially agricultural productivity, and demography were together the first obstacle, a really physical barrier to the power of the state" (p. 176) — and his conclusion is that ambition ran ahead of infrastructure: "The ambitions of kings for several centuries ran ahead of the economic structure of their states … This being-out-of-phase, this distorted relationship, caused the states to resort to overly burdensome taxes" (p. 193). On Ardant's reading the fifteenth-century French settlement should not have held; the base could not carry it. This book's answer — that a capability which no subject could replicate changed the *politics* of a fixed base, not its size — is a claim neither Finer nor Ardant makes, and it is falsifiable against Ardant's own series.

The honest concession the book now owes, in ch10 §VII and once in the Introduction: **Finer 1975 anticipated the shape of the argument.** The chapter must name him, quote pp. 51–52 and p. 105, and say what it adds — the datable price step, the settlement's irreversibility, and the closure of the sub-state path — rather than presenting a technological account of state-building as new. Grade: the differentiation holds, and it is thinner than the book has assumed.

**(ii) Ardant's rule cuts against ch10 §VIII's bequest to Part III.** "the nonfiscal methods of financing were either a partial abdication of the state, or required an advanced economic infrastructure" (p. 192); the whole chapter is a demonstration that fiscal capacity is set by the economic base and not by political will. Part III's wager that blocs will build the fiscal-administrative machinery the stacks require must reckon with a T2 authority who says the machinery follows the base. *The book's answer* is that Ardant's constraint is satisfied several times over in a legible, financialised economy — which is ch12 §III's legibility argument, and should be stated as an answer to Ardant by name rather than assumed. Good, but currently unstated.

**(iii) Tilly's "Why Europe Will Not Occur Again" is a standing objection to Part III's method.** "The European state-building experiences will not repeat themselves in new states. The connections of the new states to the rest of the world have changed too much" (p. 81); "our ability to infer the probable events and sequences in contemporary states from an informed reading of European history is close to nil" (p. 82). *The book's answer* is the one the ch. 9 assessment already records — relationships travel, sequences do not (ch. 9, p. 601) — and ch. 1 states the same licence more guardedly: "There appears, for example, to be a strong and general connection between the ultimate bulk of national governments and the extent of their reliance on land armies in their formative periods" (p. 82). The answer is good and is already the book's method; what the chapter must not do is quote p. 82's licence without p. 81's warning.

**(iv) Braun's Britain is a warning about the ornamental route's falsifiers.** §8(g) item 2 requires "ornamental" to be a demonstrable separation rather than a synonym for oligarchy. Braun's eighteenth-century Britain is exactly the hard case: a parliamentary monarchy with real fiscal control, "rule by the 'Venetian Oligarchy' with an aristocratized monarchy" (Finer, p. 124), in which the standing army was safe because "it was officered and commanded by an extension of the very same families who controlled Parliament" (Finer, p. 123) and in which, on Macpherson's reading Braun endorses, "full individuality was produced by consuming the individuality of others" (Macpherson 1962: 261, quoted p. 309). A settlement can be genuinely consent-based, genuinely representative of the propertied, and ornamental with respect to everyone else. Ch12's falsifier list must survive that case or say why it does not apply.

## 7. Verdict

This volume does not change the book's direction and it does not change its register, but it changes its **weight and its manners**: the single most consequential fact in it is that Samuel Finer, in chapter two, already ran a military-technological, punctuated account of European state-building through France, Britain and Prussia, with cannon at 1450 and the French Estates' failure to control taxation as its constitutional outcome — and that Tilly endorsed it from the editor's chair as "an alternation of long pauses with giant steps closely following changes in military technology and the scale of war" (p. 52). The book's differentiation from Finer survives, but only on the explanandum (the irreversibility of a fiscal-constitutional settlement and the closure of the sub-state path, not the format or the size of armies) and on the evidence (Finer's two French paragraphs contain two datable errors and rest on the 1439 taille tradition Major had already destroyed); ch10 §VII must now name Finer, concede the anticipation, and rest the novelty where it actually lies. The second thing the author should know is that Braun's p. 296 — rulers relying "as much as possible on sources of income which were at their 'own' disposal and not dependent … upon the consent and cooperation of the people" — is the T2 sentence spine §8(g) item 4 has been reaching for, and it is 1975, not an extension of Tilly the book has to mark as its own.
