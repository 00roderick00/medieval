## 1. The book in two paragraphs

Major's purpose is stated in the first sentence of the Preface: "It is intended to demonstrate the popular, consultative nature of the Renaissance Monarchy, not to give a detailed description of each assembly" (p. v). The book opens by naming the reading it exists to destroy — "'Francis I was an absolute monarch.' So wrote Henry Lemonnier … How often this statement has been made; but does it give a true picture of the Renaissance Monarchy of France?" (p. 3) — and answers that French royal power between Charles VII and Henry II was not absolutist but *decentralised, consultative and dependent on consent*, exercised through provincial estates, sovereign courts and privileged towns which the crown could neither coerce nor bypass: "the government of Renaissance France was decentralized. The boundaries of its inferior jurisdictions were confused, special privileges were poorly defined but still respected, the system of taxation placed the burden on the poor and the weak, the seigneurial nobility opposed the growth of monarchial power, and the army and the bureaucracy were inadequate and difficult to control" (p. 13). His positive thesis is that the estates were an instrument of monarchy rather than a rival to it — "kings encouraged and developed assemblies of the estates … They regarded these institutions as tools for their use, like their councils and their judicial and financial courts" (p. 16) — and that the decline of the *national* assembly is a story of provincial preference, not of royal conquest: "**The provincial estates of France abandoned the Estates General because of a preference for local consent, and the government abandoned that same institution because it was of too little use to justify the work and trouble it caused**" (p. 116).

The passage this book must reckon with is the frontal attack on the standing-army-to-absolutism inference, in chapter one: "**It has generally been assumed that the establishment of a standing army during the reign of Charles VII furthered the growth of royal absolutism, but no one has bothered to prove exactly when and how the new military force was used to subvert existing society or institutions.** Indeed, even a casual examination of the size and composition of the new army dispels any illusions as to the possibility that it could have been effectively used against the civilian population. The compagnies d'ordonnance or cavalry created in 1445 consisted of only 2,000 lances of six men each, and the francs-archers, an infantry militia established three years later, numbered but 16,000 men. If we assumed that these two units were kept up to strength, and they certainly were not, the new standing army and militia together would have numbered less than two modern divisions … such a force could hardly subject a population of 15,000,000 persons" (p. 9). And, against the monopoly-of-force claim: "**this was doubly true because the subjects themselves were well armed.** Anne de Montmorency is reported to have brought a retinue of eight hundred horsemen when he came to court in 1560 … Troyes marched 3,875 men before the Duke of Orléans in 1544, Amiens had 3,000 men in 1597, and many other towns had comparable forces equipped with artillery and munitions" (p. 9).

## 2. What it CONFIRMS

**Ch. 10 §III — "It was simply not consulted, year after year, until non-consultation was the constitution."** Major supplies the king's own words for it, from the reply to the rebel nobles at Nevers in 1442: the king "had the right to levy the taille on his own authority because of the emergency in which the kingdom found itself. Then he added significantly, '**it is not necessary to assemble the three estates to levy the tailles because it is only a burden and an expense to the poor common people who have to pay the cost of those who attend.** Several seigneurs have requested that we cease to make such convocations and are content for these reasons that we send commissioners to the élus in accordance with the good pleasure of the king'" (pp. 34–35). The book's sentence and Charles VII's sentence say the same thing from opposite ends.

**Ch. 10 §III — the gradualism *is* the finding, and Major dates the step.** After the 1451 reduction: "**Small wonder little protest was raised when, in the same breath, the king ordered the élus to impose the necessary taxes to support the *lances* without convoking the provincial estates to give consent. The taille to support the army, like the aides some years earlier, had come to be levied by royal command alone**" (p. 42). The step is the *taille des gens de guerre*, the date is 1451, and the mechanism is a tax cut: "Charles abandoned the regular taille altogether. With the approach of peace, the royal domain, aides, and gabelle provided sufficient revenue for the government, and the taille des gens de guerre supported the army" (p. 42); "The effect of this reform on the people was immense, the total tax burden on Auvergne being reduced by over a third" (p. 42).

**Ch. 10 §III — the aides went the same way, earlier.** "One by one the provincial estates were cajoled into paying the aides or offering an equivalent in their stead … and **when once the aides or the equivalent was agreed upon, they were often collected without further consultation with the estates**" (p. 40); "where there were no provincial estates or privileged towns, the aides were collected without consent" (p. 41). And the arithmetic of what remained: in 1461 the crown drew "only 3 per cent of his income from the domain and 9 per cent from the gabelle … the aides which, including the equivalents, amounted to 30 per cent of the royal income. The remaining 58 per cent came from the taille. **As long as the estates controlled this tax, the crown could not become financially independent**" (p. 32).

**Ch. 10 §III — the machine absorbed the 1484 cut.** "In August, 1484, the council announced that the taille for 1485 would be 1,500,000 livres in spite of the fact the Estates General had granted only 1,200,000 livres for that year. On May 4, 1485, an additional 463,500 livres was requested. The council returned to the figure of 1,500,000 livres for 1486, but the Estates General had made no offer at all for that year" (p. 115); the provincial estates paid, and "There is no evidence of any move to force the promised convocation of the Estates General by the refusal to vote taxes or by any other means" (p. 116).

**Ch. 10 §VI, Burgundy — the estates as an instrument of consolidation, not resistance.** "an Estates General was created which 'not only gave the prince an opportunity to deliberate with his subjects as a whole; but it also provided the most potent of the means of unification which had brought together the seventeen Burgundian provinces' … The very existence of an Estates General weakened the power, influence, and prestige of provincial estates and furthered the unification of the various ducal lands" (p. 18). The Burgundian counter-case gains a constitutional limb.

**Ch. 11 — the comparative register, supplied by Major himself.** Spain: "Ferdinand and Isabella used the Castilian Cortes to break the power of the nobles, after which they sought to avoid summoning that body … However, between 1497 and 1516 Ferdinand had to convoke the Cortes twelve times to get money for his foreign wars" (p. 18); Philip II "not only summoned it frequently, but he kept it in session for long periods of time" (pp. 18–19). England, Sweden, Denmark, the Empire and the Burgundian Low Countries are all treated on the same page-run (pp. 17–19) as monarchies *using* assemblies.

## 3. What it CORRECTS

**This is the section that matters, and it reaches the spine.**

1. **Spine §8(a)'s sentence "No Estates General met again until 1484" does not survive Major's Appendix.** The Appendix (pp. 151–52) lists, between the Orléans meeting and 1484:

   | Date | Location | Major's type |
   |---|---|---|
   | Oct.–Nov. 1439 | Orléans | Estates General ? |
   | Feb.–Aug. 1440 | Bourges | Estates General ? |
   | Apr. 1444 | Tours | ? (footnote 2: "The king never met with the assembled deputies") |
   | June 1448 | Tours | ? |
   | Dec. 1463–Jan. 1464 | Montferrand | Estates of eastern Languedoil |
   | Jan. 1464 | Tours | Estates of western Languedoil |
   | **Apr. 1468** | **Tours** | **Estates General** (no query) |
   | Jan.–Mar. 1484 | Tours | Estates General * |

   The 1468 row is one of the very few in the whole table that Major marks "Estates General" without his query symbol ("? Type of assembly unknown or in doubt"). It is, on his evidence, unambiguously a national assembly. **The gap in the record is 1440 to 1468 (twenty-eight years), then 1468 to 1484 (sixteen years).** Stasavage via Blockmans (ch10 memo Revisions 33) was right, and Ertman's n. 137 sent to this page for precisely this reason.

2. **What 1468 was.** A full, large, genuinely elected national assembly, convoked for political support and not for money. Major: "On February 26, 1468, during a brief truce, the letters of convocation were issued" (p. 54); the towns "held their election at different times during the month of March … the deputies were named in general assemblies of the inhabitants" (p. 54); present were "five of the six peers of the church … and nineteen other archbishops and bishops. For the second estate, over twenty-four nobles are named as attending … The largest single group was the clerical and lay deputies from sixty-four towns; they must have numbered close to two hundred" (p. 55). Its business was three questions about Normandy, Brittany and England (pp. 55–56); its output was a set of recommendations — "The recommendations of the estates were all that Louis could have wished" (p. 57) — and **no tax was granted**. Major is explicit that the elections were not rigged: "There is no real proof that either Louis XI or the rebellious nobles tampered with the elections in the towns, or that the king convoked only those great prelates and nobles who would do as he said. The oft-quoted statement of Commynes that Louis summoned whom he wished does not apply to the estates of 1468" (p. 55). **So the narrowing the ch10 memo anticipated is available and is exactly right: 1468 was an Estates General that granted nothing.** But it *was* an Estates General, it *was* large, it *was* elected, and it *did* meet.

3. **Draft §III's "no Estates General met again until 1560" is also wrong on Major's evidence.** The Appendix lists May 1506 at Tours and January 1558 at Paris, both "Estates General **", his symbol for "Restricted assembly: Only a limited number of clergymen, noblemen, and deputies of the towns were summoned" — and Major devotes a section to "The Estates of 1558" (pp. 144–47), at which the clergy offered 2,300,000 livres and the towns 4,600,000 (p. 147). Ertman's formula, "**a full, working** Estates General was not to meet again until 1560" (Ertman pp. 92–93), is the defensible one; the draft has dropped the qualifier.

4. **Draft §III's 1484 figures conflate the grant with the levy.** The draft: "the taille was granted for two years only and cut to some 1.5 million." Major: the estates offered and the crown was pressed to accept "1,200,000 livres for two years and 300,000" (p. 103); the council's counter and the sum actually levied was 1,500,000 (pp. 100, 115). The cut is real and larger than the draft says — "they were willing to cut the taille from 4,400,000 livres levied by Louis XI during the preceding year to 1,500,000 livres. **Perhaps no French government ever went further to meet the desires of the people**" (p. 100) — and the duration clause attaches to the 1,200,000, not to the 1,500,000. The draft's sentence should read: granted 1,200,000 for two years; the council levied 1,500,000, down from Louis XI's 4,400,000, and kept it there — "The taille, which had stood at 4,400,000 livres during the last year of the reign of Louis XI, was reduced to 1,500,000 livres for 1484 and was limited to this figure again as late as 1507" (p. 117).

5. **Draft §III's ordonnance paragraph is vindicated and can be strengthened.** The draft already holds the permanent-taille tradition "at tertiary strength". Major goes further: "**A completely erroneous theory has developed that the estates at this time consented to the creation of a permanent standing army and voted a permanent taille for its support, that the king was thereby freed from the necessity of getting consent for taxation, and that national representative institutions ceased to be convoked except in rare emergencies. Simple and convenient as is this explanation of the decline of national representative assemblies in France, it contains scarcely a word of truth, as Antoine Thomas pointed out as early as 1878**" (pp. 32–33). On the 1439 act's army articles: "**The purpose of these articles was to restore discipline, not to create a permanent army**" (p. 33). On the money: "**Nothing whatsoever was said about a permanent taille, and only 100,000 livres were voted**" (p. 34). And on what the assembly was *for*: "The towns were only asked to give their deputies powers to discuss the proposed peace with England, almost certain proof that the crown had no intention of asking for any radical change in financial policy" (p. 33). This closes the Major limb of the §III [GAP] and it closes it in the draft's favour.

6. **The "first Estates General in decades" at 1484 is still true, but by a shorter margin.** Sixteen years, not "nearly forty". The draft's clause "the assemblies', not summoned again for nearly forty years" (§III, the ratchet paragraph) fails outright; the 1440-to-1468 gap is twenty-eight years and 1468-to-1484 is sixteen.

## 4. What it ADDS

**(a) The 1451 step, which is a better hinge for §III than 1446.** Major's sequence — aides converted to equivalents and then collected without consultation (1440–51); the regular taille abolished in 1451; the *taille des gens de guerre* thereafter "levied by royal command alone" (p. 42); the élus created in Limousin and La Marche where there had been none (p. 42); "the last decade of Charles's reign saw a decay in the representative institutions of this region" (p. 43) — dates the closure of consent in the *pays d'élections* to **1451, two years into the Normandy campaign and two years before Castillon**, and attributes it to a tax reduction rather than to force. That is a gift and a problem at once: it puts the decisive constitutional step inside the artillery years, and it says the mechanism was the crown making the estates' consent not worth having.

**(b) The killer comparative datum: consent survived where it was defended, and cost more.** From the Charles VIII investigation: "in the généralités of Languedoil and Outre-Seine, where there were no provincial estates, the inhabitants paid the taille at per hearth rates of 19 sous and 27 sous respectively. **In Normandy and Languedoc, where the provincial estates had continued to give consent, the taille was collected at a rate of 60 sous, 8 deniers, and 67 sous per hearth**" (p. 42). Consent tripled the burden. This is the sharpest single fact in the book for Part III and for §8(g): representation and extraction were *positively* correlated in fifteenth-century France, and the provinces that surrendered consent were rewarded for it. Placement: ch10 §III in text; ch12 §VII and Appendix C by name.

**(c) Normandy 1458 — consent *strengthened* after the guns had done their work.** "in the Charter of 1315 the king had merely promised to levy taxes only in case of necessity, but **in 1458 he committed himself to first winning the approval of the three estates of the duchy**. That December the estates voted the king over 270,000 livres … For the few remaining years of the reign, the estates met each November or December and at no time voted less than 250,000 livres for a year. The Normans had learned how to pay for their privileges and their estates were convoked periodically for nearly two centuries" (p. 44). In the one province the artillery state reconquered with its siege train, the crown *conceded* a consent right five years after Castillon and kept it for two centuries.

**(d) Charles VII's own preference was for the national assembly, and he lost it.** "He would undoubtedly have preferred to win approval for taxation in one large assembly as did his English rival … **Indeed, it was far more difficult for Charles to control the deliberations of the provincial estates and the towns than those of the Estates General.** When the representatives of the kingdom met, they were in his power. Persuaded by the royal councilors, impressed by the royal majesty, surrounded by the palace guard, and far from their homes, they rarely mustered sufficient courage to resist the king's desires … Thus it was the provincial estates and the privileged towns that were the true checks on royal taxation" (p. 38). The abandonment of the Estates General was therefore, on Major's reading, a *defeat* for royal centralisation, not its instrument — "The ease with which Charles was able to abandon the large representative assemblies should by no means be considered a victory for the crown" (pp. 37–38).

**(e) The book-level frame Major offers and the book should engage: the Renaissance Monarchy as a type.** "We would do well to picture the Renaissance Monarch as the 'first gentleman' of France rather than the 'Sun King'" (p. 15); the type dies of four things at once — "the separation of the king from his people by the elaborate ritual of the court, the growing power of the administrative nobility, and the creation of a large army under effective royal control" plus the printing press as "a new means to control public opinion" (p. 20). A monarchy that governed by managing public opinion, replaced by one that governed by controlling the press and the army, is a three-line precedent for ch12's ornamental settlement from a source with no stake in it. Placement: ch12 and the Coda, by name.

**(f) Around 1505 France had "approximately 12,000 royal officials … a nation of 15,000,000 inhabitants … or one official for each 1,250 inhabitants"** (p. 5), against France in 1934 with "one official for each seventy inhabitants" (p. 5). A hard number for ch10 §III's administrative claim, and a hard limit on it.

## 5. What it CONTRADICTS

**(i) At FULL STRENGTH: Major denies the inference from the standing army to the subordination of intermediate authorities, and denies it with arithmetic.** Not "the army was small" but: the army was too small to matter, badly manned, and not the crown's to command. "even a casual examination of the size and composition of the new army dispels any illusions as to the possibility that it could have been effectively used against the civilian population … less than two modern divisions … such a force could hardly subject a population of 15,000,000 persons" (p. 9). The subjects were armed, and armed with the same technology: "many other towns had comparable forces **equipped with artillery and munitions**" (p. 9). And the loyalty of the instrument was not the king's: "**The compagnies d'ordonnance were composed almost entirely of the local nobility and captained by members of the leading families of the provinces.** The francs-archers were bourgeois and peasants subject to call in case of war. Such an army was but a small step forward from the feudal levies of the Middle Ages. The mercenaries so numerous in time of war were no more trustworthy … **Louis XIV was the first French king to establish effective control over a large military force.** Earlier, troops and civilians alike obeyed their monarch if they saw fit, but not otherwise" (pp. 9–10). Major's conclusion — "In the final analysis, the Valois, like the Tudors, had to rely on the support of their more powerful subjects" (p. 10) — is a denial of the fourth link in CLAUDE.md §2's chain for the whole period 1445–1643. He then makes the aristocratic point the book's ch11 half-concedes: "if the kings of the Renaissance had a master, it was the nobility … There is no better proof of the powerful position of the nobility in France than that this class alone was able to escape nearly all taxation" (pp. 10–11). This reaches spine §8(a) and the DEFEND-tier mechanism.

*The book's best answer, graded honestly.*

The answer has four limbs. **Grade: B+ overall — the objection is survivable but it forces two real changes.**

**Limb one — the explanandum, again. GOOD.** Major is refuting *absolutism*, defined as a king able to coerce his subjects and to govern without consulting them. The book does not claim that. §8(a) as ruled claims something narrower: that the fiscal settlement became irreversible because the assemblies' and the magnates' *ability to reverse it* was closed. Major's own evidence supports exactly that narrower claim — the aides collected without consultation after 1440 (p. 40), the *taille des gens de guerre* "levied by royal command alone" after 1451 (p. 42), the 1484 grant breached within months and never enforced (p. 115), the provincial estates declining to force a convocation (p. 116). Major says the crown was weak; he also says the crown collected what it liked and the estates did not stop it. The book must therefore write §III in Major's terms — not "the crown subdued the assemblies" but "the assemblies ceased to be the point at which the tax was decided, and never became it again."

**Limb two — the artillery train is not the compagnies. GOOD, and under-stated in the draft.** Major counts lances and francs-archers and finds them too few to overawe fifteen million people. The book's instrument is not the field army: it is the siege train, the powder, the founders, the gunners' wages, the carriage and the siege pay — the establishment §IV prices. Major does not count it, does not cost it, and does not mention the Bureaux. His demonstration is that Charles VII could not *garrison* France; the book's claim is that he could *reduce any fortified place in it*, which is a different capability with a different political effect — it does not subject a population, it ends the defensibility of a magnate's or a town's walls. The 1449–50 census is the demonstration and Major never engages it.

**Limb three — "towns had artillery" is the serious limb, and it is not answered by ignoring it. PARTIAL, grade C+.** Major's examples are sixteenth-century (Troyes 1544, Amiens 1597, Montmorency 1560) and are about *field retinues and municipal arsenals*, not about siege trains at campaign tempo — which is precisely the distinction ch10 §VII already draws against Hoffman ("Any city could buy guns; Ghent sent two hundred carts of them to the field in 1382"). But the book has been making that distinction against *price series*, and Major makes it against *possession by armed corporate subjects in being*, which is a harder version. The chapter's answer must be the same one, stated once more and with Major named: possession is not the same as a standing, provincial-scale instrument at campaign tempo on permanent taxation. The answer is good as far as it goes; what would close it is a count of who could field a siege train in France between 1470 and 1530, and the book does not have one. Flag as an open evidentiary gap.

**Limb four — the loyalty of the instrument. WEAK, and it must be conceded.** "The compagnies d'ordonnance were composed almost entirely of the local nobility and captained by members of the leading families of the provinces" (p. 9) is Ertman p. 94 in a source thirty-seven years older, and ch10 memo Revisions 31 already recorded it as the venality limb. The book cannot claim that the crown held an instrument its magnates could not touch while conceding that the magnates officered it. The honest form of the claim is that the crown held *the artillery arm* — the *recette*, the founders, the Bureau service, the powder — as a department of state, while the cavalry arm remained a patronage instrument shared with the nobility; §IV already documents the first, and §VII should say the second out loud. **This is a register decision for the rulings sheet, not a research finding.**

**(ii) The abandonment of the Estates General had nothing to do with the guns, and Major says why.** "The mystery is not so much why Charles VII eventually abandoned the idea of summoning these provinces to the Estates General, but rather why he persisted so long in calling them" (p. 35): Languedoc and Rouergue would not attend, would not vote when they did attend, and extracted a promise not to be summoned outside their province; the deputies at Bourges in 1440 were kept waiting six months and then robbed on the way home (p. 34); "Charles VII decided to abandon the Estates General and the estates of Languedoil **because they were no longer necessary**" (p. 36). Provincial particularism and administrative decentralisation, not a siege train.

*The book's answer.* Two things can be said and both are true. First, this is the book's own ch. 10 §III finding in a stronger form — nobody abolished the constitution of consent; it lapsed — and Major supplies the motive the draft currently leaves blank. Second, and this is the concession: **the lapse of the national assembly is dated 1440 and caused by particularism, which is before the guns and independent of them.** The ratchet's object must therefore be restated as the *provincial* estates and the *magnates*, not the Estates General, because the Estates General had already lapsed for reasons the book does not claim. Good, and it changes a sentence of the spine.

**(iii) Normandy 1458 is a counter-instance on the book's own ground.** Five years after Castillon, in the province the siege train had reconquered, the crown bound itself to seek the estates' approval and kept the bargain for two centuries (p. 44). *The book's answer* is that this is Tilly's and Stasavage's point — bargaining where the base is rich and organised, imposition where it is not, and the *pays d'états* / *pays d'élections* divide is the observable outcome — and that the artillery state's own record therefore shows the mechanism producing *both* branches of §8(g) inside one kingdom. That is a strong answer and it is not currently in the chapter. It should be, because it is the best available medieval demonstration that the mechanism does not determine the form.

**(iv) The whole revisionist frame cuts against Part III's register, not just ch10's facts.** Major's monarchy governs by popularity, patronage and consultation, and dies when it acquires press control and a reliable army (p. 20). A reader holding Major will say that the book has mistaken an administrative reorganisation for a constitutional revolution. *The book's answer* is that ch10 §III's own vocabulary paragraph already concedes the retrojection of "fiscal-military state" and grades the label (c); the same honesty must now extend to the settlement itself — the claim is that a *fiscal machine* became permanent and unarguable, not that a *state* became absolute. The answer is good and mostly already written; what is missing is Major's name on the objection.

## 7. Verdict

Major changes the book's **register in ch10 §III and §VII and forces one spine correction**; he does not change its direction, because his own evidence — the aides collected without consultation after 1440, the *taille des gens de guerre* "levied by royal command alone" after 1451, the 1484 grant breached within months and never enforced — documents the closure of consent more precisely than the draft currently does, while denying the absolutist reading the book does not hold. The spine correction is unavoidable and is Roderick's: **an Estates General did meet at Tours in April 1468**, on Major's own unqueried entry, elected in general assemblies of the inhabitants in sixty-four towns, with some two hundred town deputies and twenty-four archbishops, bishops and peers — so "No Estates General met again until 1484" is false as written, and so is the draft's "not summoned again for nearly forty years" and its "no Estates General met again until 1560"; what survives, and survives intact, is the substance, because 1468 granted no tax, 1484's grant was breached within eight months, and the provincial estates never tried to force another convocation. The single most important thing the author should know is that **Major destroyed the 1439 permanent-taille tradition in 1960 and named 1878 as the date it should have died** — "it contains scarcely a word of truth, as Antoine Thomas pointed out as early as 1878" (p. 33) — which vindicates the draft's decision to hold the textbook sentence at tertiary strength, closes the §III [GAP] on that limb, and hands the chapter a much better hinge than 1439: the aides after 1440 and the *taille des gens de guerre* after 1451, both of them inside the artillery years and neither of them ever put to an assembly again.
