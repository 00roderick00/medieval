## 1. The book in two paragraphs

Youings treats the Dissolution as a property operation. It "was an act of
resumption, a restoration to secular uses of land and other endowments provided
over many centuries, very largely by laymen" (ch. 1, sc. 1140); "its inspiration
and execution owed little to religious considerations. There was not even any
sustained effort to convict the religious of harbouring Roman loyalties, which
indeed very few of them did" (sc. 1159); and "It can hardly be argued that they
formed, or were seriously thought by Henry VIII and his ministers to form, a
challenge to the royal supremacy" (sc. 1166–67). Its place in historiography is
"primarily as a revolution in landownership, second only to that which followed
the Norman Conquest" (sc. 1212–13).

Her second claim is the one that matters here, and she reports it as the field's
rather than her own: "It has long been recognized that the Dissolution did not
lead to the permanent augmentation of the annual income of the Crown, and indeed
that the former monastic lands were 'dissipated' to a very great extent by Henry
VIII during the later years of his reign" (sc. 1268–70). She refuses the usual
alibi: "by the time of Cromwell's fall four years had passed since the first
assault in 1536 … and … in December 1539 Cromwell himself was named as one of the
first commissioners to sell monastic and other Crown lands" (sc. 1270–74). The
dispersal was in the design from the second year. Otherwise the book is a working
survey of an unfinished problem: no balance sheet of it, she insists, has ever
been drawn.

## 2. What it CONFIRMS

**"The dissolution is the English fisc of the settlement" (§II)** — confirmed
more strongly than the draft dared: "As the collector of the new revenues from
the monastic lands which had just about doubled the Crown's resources, the court
of Augmentations replaced the king's chamber as the bank from which the king's
government, and his personal extravagances, could be paid for" (ch. 4,
sc. 4402–05; *[index locator: pp. 110–11]*). That is the exact sequel to §II's
Grummitt paragraph: the Chamber displaces the Exchequer from c. 1487, the
Augmentations displaces the Chamber after 1536.

**"A fisc that no assembly had to grant"** — the 1538–9 induced surrenders ran
on commissions "issued on the initiative of Thomas Cromwell" which "had no
statutory authority" (ch. 3, sc. 3075–78); at the start of 1538 "there were not
even any plans for calling Parliament in the near future" (sc. 2956–58).

**§II's subordination-through-paper reading** — extended from the peerage to the
Church: "Certainly it was not with the use of armed force" (ch. 1, sc. 1364–65);
"It may well be that the greatest single factor ensuring the success of the
Dissolution was that it proceeded slowly enough for almost all concerned to know
exactly where they stood" (sc. 1384–85).

**The administrative-capacity leg**, with a novelty claim: the Augmentations'
seventeen Particular Receivers and ten Auditors on standing county circuits were
"an apportionment of continuing regional responsibility for which there is no
known precedent" (ch. 4, sc. 3810–12); its strength lay "as a working government
department, in its independence" (sc. 3780–81).

## 3. What it CORRECTS

No figure or date in ch11 is corrected — the chapter carries none. Three points
of precision are owed. (i) §II's "houses worth less than two hundred pounds a
year": Youings's point is that the act contradicts itself, the preamble
condemning houses "under the number of twelve persons" while the enacting clause
names those "which have not in lands … above the clear yearly value of two
hundred pounds" (ch. 2, sc. 2139–44; Doc. 10, sc. 5772, 5806) — and the money
test won, since "it was the information contained in the *Valor Ecclesiasticus*
and not the reports of the Visitors which determined the fate of each community"
(sc. 2145–46). (ii) The act of 1539 was not a second dissolution act: it "has so
often been mistakenly regarded" as one when it confirmed title (ch. 3,
sc. 3496–3501; Doc. 23). (iii) §II's "executed in England as a single act"
overstates her: she separates the canonical breach (1533–4) from the fiscal move
(1536–40) and doubts the connection — "Might not some quite considerable
diversion of monastic resources have taken place even if there had been no breach
with Rome?" (ch. 1, sc. 1160–63).

## 4. What it ADDS

**(a) The receipts** (the full list, with dates, is at memo Revisions 19).
Wolsey's twenty-nine houses yielded net income "about £1,800" (1524–9, ch. 2,
sc. 1603–04). In early 1536 Cromwell "was indeed assured … that by such means
there might accrue to the Crown landed property worth £40,000 a year"
(sc. 2075–76) — "by such means" is the founder's-escheat route, not the statute.
Exemption fines to Michaelmas 1538 came to "£5,948 6s 8d" (Doc. 28a,
sc. 8001–02). The new bishoprics of 1539 involved "monastic revenues of nearly
£20,000 a year" for a net loss to the crown of "rather over £5,000" (ch. 3,
sc. 3596–98). Richardson's Augmentations yield was "£899, 120 odd, or about £112,
390 a year" over eight years — with her caveat, "these receipts were those of the
Treasurer, not of the Crown" (ch. 4 n. 32, sc. 4432–37). Treasurers' warrant
payments 1540–4 totalled "£37,754 11s 7¼d" (Doc. 28c, sc. 8136–8157). Sale ran
from December 1539 "at twenty years' purchase, that is a capital sum of twenty
times their current net annual value" (ch. 5, sc. 4757–59), the rate reaching
thirty by the late 1550s. Fifteen outright grants were made in 1536, thirty-five
in 1537, seventy-five in 1538, one hundred and twelve in 1539, and "by the end of
Henry VIII's reign over half of the whole of the former monastic estates had been
alienated … well over three-quarters by 1558" (sc. 4651–57).

**What she does not carry, so the gap stays open:** no national *Valor* total, no
crown ordinary-revenue figure before or after, no total for either act — by her
own insistence that "Only after all the accounts, spreading over more than a
decade, have been analysed … will it be possible to compile a really satisfactory
balance sheet of the whole operation" (ch. 1, sc. 1284–86). Omission over
invention: no such total may be taken from her.

**(b) Whether it was a fisc.** She holds two things. *The intention was
permanence*: the Augmentations act's contents "would appear to argue the existence
at this juncture of plans not only to proceed with the confiscation of all
religious houses and their property but also to retain the greater part towards
the permanent augmentation of the Crown's resources" (ch. 4, sc. 3771–74); its
establishment "is the strongest possible argument for the government's
determination … to retain its new resources permanently in its own hands" (ch. 2,
sc. 2300–01); the selling ceilings "argue restraint" (ch. 5, sc. 4785–87).
*The outcome was not*: the dissipation verdict at §1 above; "Cromwell's ability,
if not his determination, to secure the former monastic lands as a permanent and
irreduceable part of the Crown's resources has usually been exaggerated. Disposal
by the Crown took place from the very beginning" (ch. 3, sc. 2870–71); and even
while held, "the Crown extracted nothing like the maximum possible return from its
new resources. Whether this was through design or administrative inertia still
remains to be determined" (ch. 1, sc. 1287–89). She heads her dispersal chapter
with the witness §II already quotes: "for selling of a king's livelihood is
properly called delapidation of his crown, and therefore is of great infamy" —
Fortescue (ch. 5 epigraph, sc. 4631–32). **On the wars of 1542–46 she does not say
the lands were sold to pay for them.** She carries the disposal peak "in 1542–4";
the 1543 commission's stated purpose, "towards the supportation and relief of our
great charges being presently at hand to be employed for the defence, maintenance,
and safeguard of this our realm" (Doc. 33, sc. 8381–83); "war expenses" among the
Treasurer's warrants; and November 1545, when of £20,000 needed in a week "£3,000
[came] from the Augmentations" (ch. 4, sc. 4406–09). The link is documented and
partial; state it at that strength.

**(c) The universalist power** — in the instruments, not the commentary, which is
what the [BOOK'S ARGUMENT] flag needed. Doc. 7, the Injunctions of 1535 given to
every house in advance of any finding, requires observance of the statutes "for
the extirpation and taking away of the usurped and pretended jurisdiction of the
bishop of Rome within this realm" (inj. 2, sc. 5594–95) and declares the religious
"absolved and loosed from all manner [of] obedience, oath and profession by them
heretofore perchance promised or made to the said bishop of Rome or to any other
in his stead or occupying his authority **or to any other foreign power or
person**" (inj. 3, sc. 5597–5601) — the last clause is general — and encloses an
international order: "that no monk or brother of this monastery by any means go
forth of the precincts of the same" (inj. 4, sc. 5602). Beside it: the
Dispensations Act of 1534 "cut the last link of the English men and women
religious with Rome and, in the opinion of a recent writer" — Scarisbrick —
"affected the virtual nationalization of English monasticism at one blow" (ch. 2,
sc. 1801–04); papal exemption became royal commission, the exempt houses now
"visited or vexed" only "by commissioners appointed by the king under the Great
Seal" (ch. 3, sc. 2598–2600); Cromwell as vicegerent held "powers considerably
greater than had been enjoyed by the secular hierarchy" (sc. 1966–68); the act of
1540 vested in the Crown "all the 'franchises and temporal jurisdictions' of the
monasteries" (ch. 3 n. 54, sc. 3548–51). **The decisive sentence** states the
mechanism: the communities "were never, in so many words,
actually dissolved. Nowhere in the formal instruments were they forbidden to
pursue their corporate existence … Institutions which for the most part traced
their foundation to gifts of landed property came to an end with the surrender of
that property" (ch. 1, sc. 1146–54). The fiscal act *was* the canonical act, in
the source's own words. **The honest counterweight:** she denies the universalism
was the target (sc. 1159, 1166–67), so the book's sentence stands on effect and
instrument, not motive, and should say so.

**(d) The instruments.** Legibility first: the Valor commissions of 30 January
1535, instructed "to search and know the number and names of every abbey,
monastery, priory, and house religious and conventual … and their whole and
entire, distinct and several yearly values" (ch. 2, sc. 1895–1905), returned "to
the intent that the tenth of the premises may be taxed" (sc. 1915–16). "There is
no justification whatsoever for regarding it as anything more than a taxation
assessment" (sc. 1961–62) — and thirteen months later it decided which houses
died; it was accurate to "barely 3 per cent" on resurvey (sc. 1948–49). Then the
department (Doc. 25): "a court of record" with its own great and privy seals
(sc. 7119–21), four central officers, ten Auditors, seventeen Receivers, all
sworn (sc. 7137–41), the Receivers "without exception, local men by birth and
family" (ch. 4, sc. 3813–14). And inside it the Grummitt paradox one reign later:
pension lists were "subject to Cromwell's personal scrutiny – his signature
appears on most of their lists", "a fine example of the propensity, recognized by
Professor Elton, of this energetic chief minister to reserve to himself what he
saw as vital controls, if necessary setting aside the machinery he himself had
erected" (ch. 3, sc. 3196–3200).

**(e) The military.** Greps for *artillery, ordnance, navy, bulwark, Boulogne*
return **no hits**. What she connects is confiscation to **fortification and war
finance**. The 1534 plan is headed "FOR AN INCREASE AND AUGMENTATION TO BE HAD
FOR MAINTENANCE OF HIS MOST ROYAL ESTATE, AND FOR THE DEFENCE OF THE REALM"
(Doc. 5, sc. 5464–65) and asks money "towards the charges of the wars now present
and begun for defence of Ireland as [well as] for the making of the haven of
Dover and divers piles, fortresses, blockhouses and other munitions against
Scotland" (sc. 5531–34) — with "[to use and disburse for the defence of the
realm]" standing **erased** at the smaller-monasteries clause (sc. 5487–88 n. 2).
The final phase was triggered by the scare of "a possible invasion", the crown
facing "very heavy expenditure, principally for the putting into a state of
readiness of the coastal defences. Cash was an urgent necessity" (ch. 3,
sc. 3329–34) — though her own footnote holds that Dietz and Richardson are right
"that until 1540 there was no real financial crisis" (ch. 3 n. 40, sc. 3340–42).
Confiscation money then paid the works — Arundell directed "to provide whatever
sums should be needed to the paymaster of the coastal defence works at Weymouth
and Falmouth" (May 1540, ch. 4, sc. 4222–25) — and the garrisons: "for wages of
various captains, lieutenants, deputy keepers and other soldiers of the King at
various castles, fortresses, … [etc.] amounting to £1,361 15s 6d" (Doc. 28c,
sc. 8140–42). That is the whole of it. There is no artillery in this book.

## 5. What it CONTRADICTS

**At full strength.** §II's "The settlement arrived when the crown found a fisc
that no assembly had to grant" implies a standing revenue. Youings's settled
position is that none resulted: the Dissolution "did not lead to the permanent
augmentation of the annual income of the Crown" (sc. 1268–69). The crown took a
**stock**, not a **flow**, and sold it at a fixed twenty years' purchase, over
half within eleven years and three-quarters within twenty-two, to the county
families who would make the next century's Parliaments. The department built to
hold it spent its life disposing of it, "leading inevitably towards its own
eventual redundancy" (ch. 4, sc. 4612–13), and was folded into the Exchequer in
1554. Against Part II's chain — capital-intensive force → *permanent* finance →
administrative capacity → subordination — England's 1530s supply a finite
windfall. This converges with Ertman (memo Revisions 11) from a different
direction: he shows the boards reabsorbed by 1554, she shows the revenue
reabsorbed by 1558.

**The book's best answer.** The mechanism predicts the crown's requirement and its
attempt, not its durability, and Youings supplies the attempt in its strongest
form. What England shows is a consolidator who took the right asset in the wrong
form: France's taille of 1439–46 was renewable and could not be spent out; the
monastic estate was capital and was. Read so, the case is not a refutation but the
book's clearest demonstration of *which link is load-bearing* — permanence of
finance, not size of seizure — and it explains, without conceding Ertman's frame,
why the English fiscal-military state waited for a Parliament sitting annually
under war pressure. **The answer is good; it is the book's own inference, not
Youings's, and must be flagged as such.** Reach: **spine §3** and **§8(h) ruling
(k)**; recorded, not resolved.

reads *stock, not flow; doubled the crown's resources; over half alienated by

## 7. Verdict

The book does not change the direction of *The Artillery State* and does not touch
its register; it changes the **weight** of one paragraph decisively and the
**confidence** with which ruling (k)'s second half can be stated. The single most
important thing for the author to know is that Youings supplies both halves at the
page: the dissolution *was* the English fisc — doubling the crown's resources and
making the Court of Augmentations the bank of government — and it was *not a
permanent* one, because what was taken was capital and was sold at twenty years'
purchase, over half of it before Henry VIII died. The stock-and-flow distinction is
the book's to draw and is not hers; drawn, it turns the English case from an
embarrassment into the sharpest illustration the mechanism has of why permanent
finance, and not merely a large seizure, is the link in the chain that matters.
