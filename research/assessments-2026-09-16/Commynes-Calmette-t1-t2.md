## 1. The book in two paragraphs

These are the *Mémoires* proper — Livres I–VI, the reign of Louis XI — dictated from memory between 1489 and 1491 (Introduction pp. xiii–xiv) by Charles the Bold's chamberlain from 1464 to August 1472, Louis XI's councillor thereafter. No original survives; every manuscript is an indirect copy with faults and lacunae, and Calmette prints the Dobrée manuscript where Mandrot printed the Polignac (pp. xviii–xix, xxvii–xxviii). **The division into books and chapters is Denis Sauvage's, 1552, which Calmette calls arbitrary and keeps** (p. xxvi) — which is why "Scoble, Book I ch. X" and "Calmette, Livre I ch. X" are the same chapter. Calmette's bibliography lists Sleidan's Latin and a Danish version; **he nowhere mentions Scoble or any English translation.**

For this project the two tomes are the corpus's most valuable T1. Commynes states the fiscal-military settlement as a settlement, from inside, three times over: he gives the revenue arc with figures (II, 220, restated II, 290); he names Charles VII as the king who first imposed tailles "sans consentement des Estatz", dates it to the Normandy and Guyenne conquest, gives its two justifications, and records that the lords consented in exchange for pensions paid out of the levy on their own lands (II, 289); and he states the doctrine the settlement broke in one interrogative sentence (II, 217). He is a partisan of consent, an admirer of the English parliament, and Louis XI's grateful servant. The tension is his, not the book's, and it is what makes him usable.

## 3. What it CORRECTS

- **The Estates of Tours, 1484 (ch10 §III, ll. 279–282: "granted for two years only and cut to some 1.5 million").** Commynes was present. "Et estoit la somme demandée de deux millions cinq cens mille francz, qui estoit assez et à cueur saoul, et plus trop que peu" — and the sum demanded was two million five hundred thousand francs, which was enough and to the heart's content, and rather too much than too little [TRANS. CLAUDE] (II, 221, Livre V ch. XIX). Tome III p. 304 gives the taille limb as 1.2 million. The two reconcile — 1.2m of taille inside a 2.5m total demand — and **neither is 1.5m**. The two-year horizon is confirmed but belongs to the Estates' request, not the grant's term: "Et supplyèrent lesditz Estatz que, au bout de deux ans, ilz fussent rassembléz" (II, 222).
- **"A rough tripling in a generation" (ch10 §III).** 1.8m to 4.7m is about 2.6×, and Commynes frames it as a difference of about three million a year, not a multiple. Replace with his own arithmetic, or "more than two and a half times".
- **The Burgundian artillery pin** (§2.4) and **the attribution of the Louis XI sentence** (§2.3).
- Nothing else. No date, name or sequence in the manuscript's Commynes-adjacent passages is contradicted.

## 4. What it ADDS

**The revenue arc, at the page.** II, 220 (Livre V ch. XIX): "Lors que je diz, chascun estimoit le royaume estre bien coutant … pour ce qu'ilz avoient porté et souffert vingt ans et plus de grandes et horribles tailles qui ne furent jamais si grandes à troys millions de francz près, j'entendz à lever tous les ans. Car jamais le roy Charles VII^e ne leva plus de dix huit cent mil francs pour an, et le roy Loys, son filz, en levoit à l'heure de son trespas quarante sept cens mil francs sans l'artillerie et autres choses semblables. Et seürement c'estoit compassion de veoir et sçavoir la povreté du peuple" — At the time I speak of, everyone reckoned the kingdom well content … because they had borne and suffered twenty years and more of great and horrible tailles which had never been so great, by three millions of francs — I mean levied every year. For never did King Charles VII levy more than eighteen hundred thousand francs a year, and King Louis his son levied, at the hour of his death, forty-seven hundred thousand francs, without the artillery and other such things. And truly it was a pity to see and to know the poverty of the people [TRANS. CLAUDE]. Calmette's n. 2 supplies the T2 distinction: the taille rose from 1,200,000 lt at Louis XI's accession to 3,900,000 at his death, "et le total des impôts royaux atteignait, à cette même date, 4,700,000 livres" (citing Mandrot's Jean de Roye II, 138 and *Dépêches des ambassadeurs milanais* III, 183).

The same figures return at II, 290 (Livre VI ch. VI) with the decisive gloss: "Ledict roy Charles VII^e levoit, à l'heure de son trespas, XVIII^c mil francs **en toutes choses sur son royaulme**, et tenoit environ XVII^c hommes d'armes d'ordonnance pour tous gens d'armes … A l'heure du trespas du roy nostre maistre, il tenoit XLVII^c mil francs; d'hommes d'armes, quelque quattre ou cinq mil; gens de pied, tant pour le camp que mortes payes, plus de vingt cinq mil" — … eighteen hundred thousand francs in all things upon his kingdom, and kept about seventeen hundred men-at-arms of ordinance for all his men-at-arms … At the hour of the death of the king our master, he kept forty-seven hundred thousand francs; of men-at-arms, some four or five thousand; of foot, both for the camp and the *mortes-payes*, more than twenty-five thousand [TRANS. CLAUDE].

**Does the draft's arc survive? Yes, with three refinements.** (i) The lower limb is a ceiling, not an average — "jamais … ne leva plus de". (ii) The upper limb **excludes the artillery** — "sans l'artillerie et autres choses semblables" — which sharpens the chapter's case: the guns sat outside the headline figure. (iii) The draft's gloss "the whole levy, not the taille alone" is now verbatim — "en toutes choses sur son royaulme" — with Calmette's note separating the 3.9m taille from the 4.7m total. Commynes's three limbs are internally coherent: 1.8m, a difference of about three million, 4.7m.

**The settlement named, dated and priced (II, 289, Livre VI ch. VI).** "Le roy Charles VII^e fut le premier (par le moyen de plusieurs saiges et bons chevaliers, qui luy avoyent aydé et servy en sa conqueste de Normandie et Guyenne, que les Angloys tenoient), lequel gaigna ce point que de imposer tailles en son pays et à son plaisir, sans consentement des Estatz de son royaume. Et pour lors y avoit grant matière, tant pour garnyr les pays conquis que pour departir les gens de compaignye qui pilloyent le royaulme. **Et à ceci se consentirent les seigneurs de France pour certaines pensions qui leur furent promises pour les deniers que on levoit en leurs terres.**" — King Charles VII was the first (by means of several wise and good knights who had helped and served him in his conquest of Normandy and Guyenne, which the English held) who won this point: to impose tailles in his country and at his pleasure, without consent of the Estates of his kingdom. And at that time there was great cause for it, both to garrison the conquered lands and to disband the companies that were pillaging the kingdom. And to this the lords of France consented, for certain pensions promised them out of the money that was levied in their lands [TRANS. CLAUDE]. The verdict follows at II, 290: "il chargea fort son ame et celles de ses successeurs, et mist une cruelle playe sur son royaume, qui longuement seignera, et une terrible bride de gens d'armes de soulde qu'il institua à la guyse des seigneurs d'Italye" — he greatly charged his soul and those of his successors, and laid a cruel wound upon his kingdom which will long bleed, and a terrible bridle of paid men-at-arms which he instituted after the fashion of the lords of Italy [TRANS. CLAUDE].

**The doctrine of consent, and the English contrast.** II, 217, the opening sentence of Livre V ch. XIX: "Doncques, pour continuer mon propoz, y a-il roy ne seigneur sur terre qui ayt povoir, oultre son dommaine, de mectre ung denier sur ses subgectz sans octroy et consentement de ceulx qui le doyvent payer, sinon par tyrannie et violence?" — So then, to continue my argument, is there king or lord on earth who has power, beyond his domain, to lay a single penny on his subjects without the grant and consent of those who must pay it, save by tyranny and violence? [TRANS. CLAUDE]. Restated at II, 222: "Car nul prince ne le peult autrement lever que par octroy … s'il ne le faict par tyrannye et qu'il ne soit excommunyé." The English comparison is at II, 8: "le roy ne peult entreprendre une telle œuvre sans assembler son parlement, qui vault autant comme les troys estatz, **qui est chose très juste et saincte, et en sont les roys plus fortz et myeulx serviz** quant ainsi le font en semblables matières" — the king cannot undertake such a work without assembling his parliament, which is worth as much as the three estates, which is a thing most just and holy, and kings are the stronger for it and the better served when they do so [TRANS. CLAUDE]; with "il ne se liève nulles aydes en Angleterre, si ce n'est pour passer en France ou pour aller en Escosse". Calmette's n. 4 records Bourrilly reading this as Commynes anticipating French liberalism.

**The commons.** II, 216: "après avoir payé des tailles trop plus grandes qu'ilz ne deüssent, encores ne donnent-ilz nulle ordre sur la forme de vivre de leurs gens d'armes, lesquelz, sans cesser, sont par le pays sans riens payer … Je diz cecy pour nostre royaume, qui est plus pressé et persecuté de ce cas que nulle autre seigneurie que je congnoisse" — after paying tailles far greater than they ought, still they give no order for the manner of living of their men-at-arms, who are ceaselessly about the country paying for nothing … I say this for our kingdom, which is more pressed and persecuted in this matter than any lordship I know [TRANS. CLAUDE]. With II, 289: "si avoit plus chargé le peuple que jamais roy ne feit" — he had burdened the people more than ever king did.

**Louis XI's practice, at first hand.** The Somme towns, bought back for 400,000 écus and demanded back by Charolais (I, 8; I, 74; the treaties I, 86). The standing companies at Montlhéry: "y estoyent toutes les ordonnances du royaulme, qui povoient bien estre environ vingt et deux cens hommes d'armes" (I, 21), the royal archers "tous archiers d'ordonnance, orfaveriséz et bien empoint" against Burgundians "sans ordre et sans commandement" (I, 25). *Francs-archers* throughout — and their quality at Guinegate, 1479: they "se misdrent à piller le charroy dudit duc" (II, 275). The pension as instrument: the Breton coalition bought in 1472, 80,000 francs for the duke, 6,000 and Guyenne for Lescun, offices for the rest, "Le tout dura à son maistre et à luy jusques au trespas dudict roy" (I, 241–242); Edward IV's court on the French payroll, "la pension de cinquante mille escuz … (eulx l'appelloyent tribut)" and sixteen thousand more to his household, "et de tous ces personnages icy se trouveront les quictances en la Chambre des comptes à Paris" (II, 241–242); the Swiss at 40,000 Rhenish florins a year and a million florins across the Burgundian war (II, 116). **Artillery is named at I, 14, 46, 61–65, 106, 153, 160, 235–237 and II, 8, 18, 102, 188 — and its administration nowhere.** No ordinance, no treasurer, no account series. The administrative state is invisible to him; the money is not.

**Burgundy, the counter-case (ch10 §VI), diagnosed from inside.** In 1465 the Burgundian army was 1,400 men-at-arms "mal armez et mal adroitz, car long temps avoyent esté ces seigneuries en paix", with eight or nine thousand archers, and the subjects "en grande richesse, à cause de la longue paix qu'ilz avoient eu et pour la bonté du prince soubz qui ilz vivoyent, **lequel tailloit peu ses subjectz**", their lands better called "terres de promission que nulles autres seigneuries qui fussent sur la terre" (I, 13). Charles the Bold inherited movables beyond any prince in Christendom, but "le duc Philippes n'avoit de long temps point levé de tailles; toutesfois trouva-il plus de trois cens mil escuz contens" (II, 93). The ordinance companies are attested in the field — "douze cens lances d'ordonnance, qui avoient trois archiers pour homme d'armes … car ilz estoient bien payéz" (I, 226), matching the Abbeville establishment ch10 §VI carries. And the end, at Nancy: fewer than four thousand men in the camp, "dont il n'y en avoit que douze cens en estat de combattre. **D'argent avoit ledict duc assez, car il avoit au chasteau de Luxembourg … bien quatre cens cinquante mil escuz, et des gens eust-il recouvert assez**" — of money the duke had enough, for he had in the castle of Luxembourg a good four hundred and fifty thousand écus, and men he could well have recovered [TRANS. CLAUDE] (II, 150). A hoard is not a fiscal system.

**For ch06.** Neuss, 1474–75: a town held by the landgrave of Hesse with eighteen hundred horse, and the duke "trouva les choses plus dures qu'il ne pensoit" (II, 11); the year lost there wrecked the Anglo-Burgundian invasion. Beauvais, June–July 1472: relieved in time, then "l'artillerie dudict duc tira continuellement, l'espace de quinze jours ou environ. Et fut la place aussi bien battue que jamais place fut, jusques en l'estat d'assaillir" — and it held (I, 236–237). Both are *délai de secours* cases that ran the other way, from a witness on the losing side of each.

## 5. What it CONTRADICTS

1. **The witness holds the settlement to be a crime.** No prince may levy beyond his domain without consent except "par tyrannie et violence" (II, 217, 222); Charles VII's achievement is "une cruelle playe sur son royaume, qui longuement seignera" (II, 290). The book's mechanism is his indictment.
2. **He credits the settlement to consent bought, not to administration built.** No généralité, no élu, no ordinance, no account: what he gives is a bargain (II, 289). ch10 §IV's institutional half is Contamine's, not his.
3. **The infantry the money bought was not obviously good, and the ascendancy was exercised by not fighting.** The francs-archers plundered the baggage at Guinegate (II, 275); Louis "ne mectoit riens en hazard et ne vouloit pour riens les batailles" (II, 276).

**The book's best answer.** To (1): quote him. A falsifiable, non-celebratory argument (CLAUDE.md §2) gains from a contemporary who calls the mechanism a wound, and the chapter should print "une cruelle playe … qui longuement seignera" and let it stand. To (2): say plainly that the administrative half is reconstructed from the accounts and the participant saw only the bargain — and note that his bargain is itself the chain's fourth link, the suppression of intermediate authorities, purchased rather than fought for. To (3): the chapter already locates decisiveness at the walls and the exchequer; a Louis XI who bought Edward IV, the Bretons and the Swiss and avoided battle is that claim in its purest form. None of the three reaches a spine §8 ruling.

## 6. Placement

**ch10 §III:** the arc re-pinned to II, 220 with the "en toutes choses" gloss from II, 290 and the "sans l'artillerie" qualifier; the 1484 figures corrected from II, 221–222 with tome III p. 304; **II, 289 into the text** as the T1 statement of what the settlement was and what it cost the lords; II, 217 as the doctrine it broke. **§IV:** the pensions and the Chambre des comptes receipts (II, 241–242; II, 116); the ordinance companies (I, 21, 25). **§V:** "so much shooting" re-pinned to I, 62; the Louis XI judgement to I, 67 with the Scoble caveat; the Somme towns (I, 8, 74, 86); Louis's declared readiness to break Conflans and Péronne (I, 232) beside "null and void as signed under duress". **§VI:** the Burgundian artillery to II, 8; I, 13 and II, 93 as the counter-case's own diagnosis; Nancy's 450,000 écus (II, 150) as its closing image. **ch06:** footnote — Neuss (II, 11) and Beauvais (I, 236–237). **ch11 §VII:** II, 289 and I, 241–242 as the French counterpart to Fortescue's programme and Lander's Bergavenny — bought, not broken. **ch04:** nothing; the Italian material is tome III's. **Role:** ally as witness, adversary as moralist, and the distinction visible in the prose wherever he is used.

## 7. Verdict

Tomes I–II close the row open since 14 September and do more than close it. Four of the drafts' five Commynes citations are verified at the page and one is re-pinned away from its intermediary's error; the revenue arc is confirmed at Vale's exact reference, restated seventy pages later with the "in all things upon his kingdom" gloss the draft had been carrying as inference, and qualified in the book's favour by "without the artillery". And ch10 gains, at T1, the sentence it has been reconstructing: that Charles VII was the first to tax without the Estates, that he did it to garrison the conquests and disband the companies, and that the lords of France consented for pensions paid out of the levy on their own lands. The single most important thing to know: **the witness who supplies the mechanism condemns it**, calling it a cruel wound and a terrible bridle — and a book that claims a mechanism is operating, not that the settlement is desirable, has nothing to lose and much authority to gain by printing its best primary source's verdict against it. For the record, the tome II scan has lost printed pp. 214–215 and 218–219, inside the chapters on the role of the Estates; a clean copy of those four pages is the one thing still wanting.
