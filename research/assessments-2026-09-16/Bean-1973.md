## 1. The article in two paragraphs

Bean's explanandum is stated twice and is unambiguous: not the state, not absolutism, but **size and the concentration of power**. "The phrase 'rise of the nation-state' is quite vague. For the purposes of this paper we shall focus on power. The phenomenon we shall try to explain is the consolidation of political and military power that occurred in Europe in the early modern period. A political map of Europe for the year 1400 A.D. shows a plethora of independent and semi-independent states—kingdoms, principalities and duchies abounded. By 1600 A.D. the number had been sharply reduced, and within each of the remaining states the power of the central authority had been greatly enhanced. In 1400 A.D. no prince could prevail against any substantive portion of his feudal barons unless he enjoyed the active support of a similar number of the barons. In 1600 A.D. most princes could be confident that their standing army would suppress all but the most widespread rebellions" (p. 203). His model is the theory of the firm: economies of scale (a larger free-trade area; "per capita defense costs should tend to fall because of simple geometry") against diseconomies of command and control, yielding "a 'U-shaped' long run average cost curve" for polities, so that "the interaction of centripetal and centrifugal forces will determine the optimal range of size of the state. States larger than the optimal range tend to disintegrate, and those smaller tend to be absorbed" (p. 204). The thesis follows in one sentence: "**The central argument of this paper is that changes in military technology or in administrative technique can alter this range of optimum sizes of the state**" (p. 205).

The technological content is the cannon, dated to the middle of the fifteenth century and evidenced from Normandy: "In the middle of the fifteenth century, the cannon drastically altered the balance between offense and defense. **In 1449-50 A.D., Charles VII of France regained Normandy in a small portion of the time it had taken the English to take it earlier in that century. His new siege train knocked down the old high masonry walls with dispatch, 60 fortified places being reduced in 369 days.** The breaching of the walls of Constantinople in 1453 by Turkish siege guns further emphasized the decline in the effectiveness of fortification" (p. 207). The window is explicitly closed: "More than fifty years passed before the art of fortification caught up with the new techniques of siege-craft, but by the second decade of the sixteenth century, the balance was again in favor of the defense" (p. 207) — and the closing leaves a permanent residue, because "in the latter period, the minimum effective scale of fortification was considerably larger. This was true of both construction costs and garrison size. As a consequence, there were many fewer places in Europe after 1525 A.D. with effective fortification than had been the case before 1450 A.D." (p. 208). The fiscal consequence is stated flatly: "In France in the 1440's and in Spain in the 1480's the rulers were able to seize these taxes as a right and thus dispense with the legislative bodies. These fundamental alterations in the implicit 'constitutions' made 'absolute' monarchy a possibility in those countries" (p. 213).

## 2. What it CONFIRMS

**Ch. 10 §VII and ch. 6 — the chain, compressed, in a 1973 economic historian's words.** Bean's conclusions state the book's chain in four links: "changes in the art of war in the fourteenth century greatly increased the importance of infantry …; **the maturation of the siege cannon in the mid-fifteenth century practically eliminated the castle as an important factor in war**; in the later fifteenth century standing armies began to appear in Europe; at the end of the fifteenth century the size of armies increased sharply, as did the revenues of central governments and their expenditures on military affairs; and finally, in the 1450-1550 A.D. period the first generation of European nation-states coalesced under the control of central authority. My argument is that these events were casually [*sic*: causally] connected" (p. 220).

**Ch. 10 §VII — the capital-intensity step, in the vocabulary of the firm.** "The siege train of cannon, like units of infantry, showed considerable economies of scale, and the flowering of the cannon in the mid-fifteenth century further increased the minimum effective size of states and **drastically reduced the ability of the feudal nobility to resist the authority of the central government**. All over Western Europe the feudal nobility became subordinate to the central governments—the process being largely complete by 1500" (p. 220). "Subordinate", not eliminated — the same verb the ruling on ch10 §I settled on, in 1973.

**Ch. 10 §VIII — the competitive cascade.** "Once the power to tax had been successfully appropriated by any one sovereign, once he had used that power to bribe or coerce his nobility into acquiescence, that state could face all neighboring states with the choice of being conquered or of centralizing authority and raising taxes" (p. 220). This is Tilly 1990's "mount the same sort of military effort at great cost, accept conquest, or find a safe subordinate niche" (p. 190), seventeen years earlier and with the fiscal step named first.

**Ch. 10 §II and §IV — the French standing army as the first.** "The Compagnies d'Ordonnance were raised in France beginning in 1445 A.D. There were originally twenty companies of 600 men each, posted at various points throughout the country to preserve order. These troops were organized in the modern style in that the officers were royal appointees and served at the pleasure of the king. This contrasts with the previous system of mercenaries, where an individual leader leased his band as his personal property or raised the band under contract" (p. 218). And the establishment cost, which is exactly the draft's 1442-test move applied to the companies: "In France after 1445 A.D., when the Compagnies d'Ordonnance were established as a standing army of heavy cavalry some 12,000 strong, their payroll alone stood at 720,000 Livres tournois, 'L(t),' out of a budget of perhaps 3 million L(t)" (pp. 216–17, on Oman, *Middle Ages*, II, 432).

**Ch. 10 §III — the revenue arc, at a higher level of generality than Commynes.** "the revenues of the central governments of Western Europe rose sharply in the second half of the fifteenth century and continued to rise, though more slowly, thereafter. The original increase represented at least a doubling of the real per capita burden upon the citizens. Most of this new revenue was spent on war, with the lesser portion being divided between a more luxuriant court and **the support of the expanded bureaucracy that made possible the collection of the new revenues**" (p. 217). This is McNeill's second-hand doubling (ch10 memo Revisions 35, p. 105 n. 35) at source.

**Ch. 10 §VI — the Swiss and the anti-determinist concession are in Bean too.** He is careful that the fourteenth-century infantry revolution precedes and is independent of the gun (pp. 206–08), and he concedes the field-battle point the book's own critiques concede: "Until the seventeenth century, cannons were usually of little importance in the field because of their slow rate of fire and their immobility" (p. 206).

2. **Bean misdates the francs-archers.** "A few years later Louis XI instituted another innovation in the French military system when he established Francs Archers, a 16,000 man militia intended to be a national infantry" (p. 218). The francs-archers are Charles VII's, 1448 (ch10 §II; Major 1960: 9). Do not cite Bean on this.
3. **Bean's "Formigny in 1450" as a cannon victory** (p. 206: "finally the cannon allowed the French to force the English to move out of their essentially defensive formation at Formigny in 1450") is the standard account and consistent with ch06; it is noted only because Bean's neighbouring francs-archers error makes his French detail a T3-grade carrier of T2 claims.

## 4. What it ADDS

**(a) The single most important addition: Bean states spine §8(g)'s split — mechanism one claim, constitutional form another — in his closing paragraph, in 1973.** "The changes in the art of war practically guaranteed the weakening of the feudal nobility, the concentration of power within each state, and the reduction of the number of states. **However, there was no certainty as to which faction within the nation would control the new centralized state.** In France and Spain the crown managed to gain control of power to tax. In Holland and England these powers were retained by elective bodies in which merchants were heavily represented. In Eastern Europe the great landowners retained substantial control. … This paper has presented an explanation for the concentration of political power in Europe from 1450 to 1550 A.D. **It has not explained why different groups gained control of this power in different states. That task remains a challenge to further research**" (p. 221). Spine §8(g) as reworded — "(i) the independent technological powers are subordinated to the state — the mechanism; (ii) the constitutional form of the state that absorbs them is open" — is Bean's two claims, scored separately. Placement: ch12 §VII and Appendix C, by name; the book should say that the split is not an improvisation forced by the second-reader review but the standing structure of the artillery thesis since 1973, and that what is new is the wager on which way the democratic bloc goes.

**(b) The bastion as a cost-threshold, not a refutation — Bean's own formulation, which is better than the one the book currently answers Tilly with.** "The main difference in the defense-offense equation between the periods 1100-1440 A.D. and 1525-1790 A.D. was that in the latter period, the minimum effective scale of fortification was considerably larger. This was true of both construction costs and garrison size. As a consequence, there were many fewer places in Europe after 1525 A.D. with effective fortification than had been the case before 1450 A.D." (p. 208), with the earlier statement of what the gun destroyed: "By about 1200 A.D., almost all of Western Europe was dotted with stone castles that were nearly immune from force … The existence of numerous castles made it possible for quite small regions successfully to resist much larger opponents" (pp. 206–07). The *trace italienne* raises the entry price of a defensible place and thereby **reduces the number of sub-state holders of defensible places** — which is the closure of the sub-state path stated from the defensive face. Mangini and Petroff (ch10 critiques) are downstream of this. Placement: ch10 §VII, in text, answering Tilly's second limb.

**(c) The fiscal-constitutional claim, dated to the 1440s and paired with Spain.** "These taxes were normally granted only in real military emergencies and were usually only one-time levies. **In France in the 1440's and in Spain in the 1480's the rulers were able to seize these taxes as a right and thus dispense with the legislative bodies.** These fundamental alterations in the implicit 'constitutions' made 'absolute' monarchy a possibility in those countries. In England the Tudors and then the Stuarts failed to establish a royal right to levy taxes directly, and the attempt of Charles I to bypass Parliament in raising revenue was the direct cause of the English Civil War" (p. 213), followed by the Swedish case — "in Sweden, for example, Karl Knutsson was deposed in 1457 A.D. for trying to introduce new taxes" (p. 216). The **comparative register** here is already the book's: France, Spain, England, Sweden, on the same fiscal-constitutional axis, at the same dates. Placement: ch10 §III and ch11.

**(d) Bean's counter-example to gun-determinism is administrative, not Swiss.** His model admits *two* movers, "changes in military technology **or in administrative technique**" (p. 205), and he never resolves which did the work — a modesty the book can use in ch10 §VII to show that the artillery thesis in its original form was already a loop, not a one-way arrow.

**(e) Mercenaries as the cause of standing armies, for ch03.** "At the end of major wars unemployed bands of mercenaries frequently became brigands and this problem was the probable cause for the creation of the first standing army in Europe since before the Middle Ages" (p. 218) — the écorcheur argument that ch03 and ch10 §II both carry, stated by an economic historian as a *disorder-management* rather than a war-fighting rationale. Footnote at ch10 §II.

## 5. What it CONTRADICTS

**(i) At FULL STRENGTH: this article is the book's thesis, and it is fifty-three years old.** Bean has the gun, the date (1449–53), the Normandy campaign, Constantinople, the establishment cost of the companies, the fiscal appropriation "in France in the 1440's", the subordination of the nobility "largely complete by 1500", the comparative set, the cascade, and the mechanism-versus-form split. He even has the word: "consolidation of political and military power" (p. 203). A reader who knows this article will ask what CLAUDE.md §1's "specific mechanism that ended it once" adds to *Journal of Economic History* 33:1.

*The book's best answer, and an honest grade.* The answer has three limbs and grades **A on the first, B on the second, and A on the third.**

**Limb one — the explanandum. GOOD, and decisive.** Bean's dependent variable is the *number and geographic size of states*: the U-shaped cost curve, the "optimal range of size", states outside it disintegrating or being absorbed (p. 204); the phenomenon is "a plethora of independent and semi-independent states" in 1400 reduced by 1600 (p. 203). The book's dependent variable is the *permanence of the fisc and the closure of the sub-state path to decisive force inside a surviving polity* — what §8(a) calls irreversibility. These are different questions with different failure conditions. Bean's can be refuted by a map (which is precisely how Tilly refuted it: Poland and Russia "render ambiguous the claim of a significant increase in geographic scale", 1985, History Talks, para 2; and Stasavage's "muted" thesis, ch10 memo Revisions 33). The book's cannot: it is refuted by showing an assembly that reversed the settlement or a magnate who replicated the instrument. **The book must therefore cite Bean and say in one sentence that its claim is not Bean's claim — and it can, because Bean's own last paragraph concedes that the question of who controls the consolidated state is not his (p. 221).**

**Limb two — the evidentiary base. B, and this is where the book is thin about its own descent.** Bean's fifteenth-century French evidence is one sentence from Oman, one figure from Oman, and a footnote to Petit-Dutaillis in Lavisse, with a datable error about the francs-archers. The book's is Contamine's accounts, the Bureau service, the 1442 establishment document, the audited artillery *recette*, Depreter's Burgundian comparator, the Normandy and Guyenne census. The book therefore *evidences* an assertion Bean made from secondary literature. That is a real contribution and it is a modest one; it is the difference between a hypothesis and a demonstration, not between two hypotheses. The chapter should say so in those words rather than implying novelty of thesis.

**Limb three — what happened to the thesis afterwards. A, and it is the chapter's best rhetorical asset.** Tilly read Bean, summarised him accurately, and rejected him on chronology and on the *trace italienne* (1985, History Talks, paras 1–2; ch10/sources.md, STEP 3 block). Stasavage names Bean's as "the clearest exposition" of the technology-and-scale argument and rejects it as "muted" (ch. 1, n. 3 and part0008_split_000). North (1981) adopted it. The thesis has therefore been **in the literature, named, and judged for half a century** — and the judgement was passed on the *size* claim while the fiscal-constitutional claim at p. 213 went untested, because nobody went to the French archives to test it. That is the gap the book occupies. Stated that way the chapter gains rather than loses by putting Bean in text.

**(ii) Bean's window closes, and the book's does not say what happens after it.** "More than fifty years passed before the art of fortification caught up … but by the second decade of the sixteenth century, the balance was again in favor of the defense. From then until the end of the eighteenth century, war was again generally an interminable sequence of sieges punctuated by occasional bloody but indecisive battles in the open field" (pp. 207–08). If the offensive advantage lasted roughly 1450–1520, then the artillery state's ratchet had a seventy-year window in which to set, and the settlement outlasted the capability that set it by three centuries. *The book's answer* is that this is the ratchet's definition and its strongest evidence — irreversibility means the settlement survives the technical conditions that produced it — and the chapter should say so explicitly, because it converts Bean's chronological limitation into the book's central finding. Good, and currently unstated. **This bears on Part III directly**: Appendix C should ask how long the compute-led stack's decisive window is expected to stay open, and whether the settlement it induces would survive the window closing.

**(iii) A DISANALOGY the bridge must carry.** Bean's mechanism works by *raising the minimum viable size of the unit*. The contemporary stacks do the opposite on their face: they are not territorial, they do not make small polities indefensible, and the interstitial spaces of Part III are precisely places where the minimum viable size has not risen. The book's claim is about the closure of the *sub-state* path, not about the *inter-state* map, and CLAUDE.md §6 requires the disanalogy stated: **Bean's consolidation reduced the number of polities; this book's predicted consolidation is expected to reduce the number of independent holders of decisive capability inside blocs while leaving the map of polities largely as it is.** That sentence is owed in ch10 §VIII or ch12.

## 7. Verdict

Bean does not change the book's direction, and he does not change its register; he changes what the book is allowed to claim for itself, which is a larger change than either. This article is the artillery thesis — cannon, the fifteenth century, Normandy 1449–50, the destruction of the castle, the subordination of the nobility, the seizure of the tax power in the 1440s — and the book's mechanism is its descendant, not its discovery; what the book adds is the archival demonstration of a claim Bean made from Oman, the reframing of the explanandum from the size of states to the irreversibility of the settlement, and the answer to the objections (Tilly's chronology, the bastion, the naval limb) that sent Bean's version into fifty years of neglect. The single most important thing the author should know is that **Bean's closing paragraph already draws spine §8(g)'s line** — the changes in the art of war "practically guaranteed" the concentration of power, but "there was no certainty as to which faction within the nation would control the new centralized state" (p. 221) — so the consolidation/form split is not a concession the book made in September 2026, it is the shape the thesis has had since 1973, and saying so makes the wager look disciplined rather than hedged.
