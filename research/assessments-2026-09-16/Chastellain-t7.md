## 1. The book in two paragraphs

Tome VII is not chronicle. It is the collected occasional prose and verse
of the duke of Burgundy's *indiciaire* — Kervyn's word (p. ix); the *Douze
Dames* letters call him "messire George, orateur de Monseigneur le duc
Philippe de Bourgongne" (p. 145) — written between Louis XI's accession in
1461 and about 1470, and almost throughout a mirror for princes addressed
to two named men. Its centre of gravity is 1467: Philip the Good dies on 15
June, and the volume gives, in sequence, a funerary ballade (*Le Lyon
rampant*, pp. 207–208), a prose portrait of the dead duke and the living
one (pp. 213–236), a mystery-play lamentation (pp. 237–280), rhymed
epitaphs (pp. 281–284), and the long *Advertissement au duc Charles*, which
Kervyn dates by a household payment — in July 1467 a servant, Jean
Chenebaut, carried "un livret rédigé par son maître, qui valut au messager
soixante sous pour son vin" (p. xvi; T2). Chastellain's claim on the reader
is stated by his editor: "loin de se mêler aux flatteurs qu'il méprisait et
condamnait, il revendiqua constamment le privilège de faire entendre la
vérité aux princes et aux grands" — far from mixing with the flatterers he
despised, he constantly claimed the privilege of making truth heard by
princes and the great [TRANS. CLAUDE] (pp. v–vi). That is an editor's claim
for his own author, not a finding.

Its value here is narrow and real. It holds the spider the manuscript has
cited unpinned since 3 September; it holds the corpus's most sustained
statement of what a prince owes his lands, his council and his subjects,
addressed to the one prince in this book whose state failed at a single
death; and it holds — as a negative finding worth as much — no artillery,
no bombards, no *taille* and almost no finance. *Artillerie* does not occur
in the volume. War in these pages is never a budget; it is a wound in the
*povre peuple*. That is the register the book itself keeps, and it should
be used knowing that this witness supplies the moral half and none of the
fiscal half.

## 2. What it CONFIRMS — and the [PIN PENDING] closed

**The verse, printed p. 207** (PDF 231), first stanza of *Le Lyon rampant*
(Kervyn's text "d'après les Mss. 1717 et 12788 de la Bibl. imp. de Paris"):

> "Lyon rampant en crouppe de montaigne, / Mort immortel en honneur
> triumphant, / **Lyon fameux, tryacle contre araigne**, / Trempé en fer,
> vertueux et vaillant … Et corps absent et présent en mémoire, / Lyon
> transy tout refulgent en gloire"

— Lion rampant on the shoulder of a mountain, dead undying, triumphant in
honour; famous lion, treacle against the spider; tempered in iron,
virtuous and valiant … body absent and present in memory; lion passed over,
all refulgent in glory [TRANS. CLAUDE] (sidecar "montaigue", "refulg-ent" —
OCR, restored). The refrain of the three stanzas and the envoi is "Second
Hector et derrein Alexandre"; the envoi closes "En paradis te plaise l'ame
prendre" (p. 208).

**Kervyn's note, printed p. 208** (PDF 232, running to p. 209): "Cette
ballade, comme toutes celles de Chastellain, eut un grand retentissement,
et le premier vers, inspiré par les insignes héraldiques de la maison de
Bourgogne, devint un refrain populaire … Ils adoptèrent en même temps, pour
désigner le monarque qui étendait ses intrigues sur toute l'Europe, la
figure de l'araignée aux toiles perfides, au venin dissimulé, et elle passa
des vers de Chastellain, qui ont été insérés plus haut, dans d'autres
compositions historiques ou poétiques. **Plus tard, en 1467 et en 1468**,
quand Dammartin, envoyé avec une petite armée en Champagne, y essuya une
défaite … le vers de la ballade politique devint de plus en plus acéré." —
the poets of duke Charles's court adopted, to designate the monarch who was
extending his intrigues over the whole of Europe, the figure of the spider
with its treacherous webs and concealed venom, and it passed from
Chastellain's verses into other compositions; later, in 1467 and 1468 …
[TRANS. CLAUDE]. His *Notice* summary, p. xiv: "Le Lyon rampant. Ces
strophes auraient peu fixé l'attention, si l'on n'y rencontrait une
énergique allusion à l'araignée qui distillait son venin pour perdre le
noble lion."

**"L'universel araigne" is at printed p. 209 — and is not Chastellain's on
the edition's own ruling.** The note continues: "en faisant remarquer que,
selon nous, la première des ballades suivantes lui a été injustement
attribuée, tandis qu'il faut la restituer à Molinet, comme le porte le Ms.
12490 (208) de la Bibl. imp. de Paris" (p. 208 n.). The ballade then
printed has the lion speak: "Accompagné de mes petits lyons, / **Ay
combattu l'universel araigne** / Qui m'a trouvé par ses rébellions / Lyon
rampant en crouppe de montaigne", and later "M'a fait avoir victoire en la
Champaigne" and "Tremblez, Liégeois!" (pp. 209–210). Three answering
ballades follow — Gilles des Ormes, "le petit Darc, de Rouen", one
anonymous (pp. 210–212) — all setting the *cerf volant* against the lion.

**Who the lion is.** The ballade never names him; the volume does. The next
piece is headed "PHILIPPE DE BOURGONGNE, **CELUY QUI SE NOMME LE GRAND DUC
ET LE GRAND LYON**" (p. 213). The *Advertissement* opens on "la mort du
très-excellent prince, l'auguste duc Philippe, **le grant lyon**, le grant
duc de Bourgongne, le pillier de l'honneur de France" (p. 285). The
*Rhythmes* carry the chronogram "**Léo MagnVs oCCIdentIs ConCVssVs est**"
(p. 283) and the death: "En mille quatre cents soixante sept années /
Furent ses grans vertus par la mort terminées. / Soixante-onze ans vesquit
le chief des créatures" (p. 283). So the dead lion is Philip the Good; the
lion of the Molinet ballade, alive, victorious in Champagne and threatening
the Liégeois, is Charles the Bold. The figure passes from dead father to
living son — which is what "elle passa des vers de Chastellain … dans
d'autres compositions" describes.

**The date.** Kervyn nowhere dates *Le Lyon rampant*. His only date, "Plus
tard, en 1467 et en 1468", attaches by *plus tard* to the imitations and
places Chastellain's earlier. Against that, the subject is a dead
Burgundian lion; Philip died 15 June 1467; the neighbouring pieces are a
1467 lament cycle, and *La Mort du duc Philippe* dates itself from within —
"Or ont esté sept fois dix ans / Nos yeux en luy s'esjoyssans … Mais puis
cinq mois l'avons perdu" (p. 242), five months after 15 June, matching
Kervyn's "composé dès le mois de novembre précédent" (p. xiv). **1467 is a
sound inference from the volume's contents and sequence; it is not the
edition's statement, and must be carried as inference or dropped.**

## 3. What it CORRECTS

- **ch10/draft.md §V (l. 468): "Chastelain's image, in a ballade of 1467,
  generalised by Molinet as 'l'universelle aragne,' the universal spider."**
  (i) Spelling: **Chastellain**. (ii) *Ballade* survives — Kervyn's own
  word (p. 208 n.), and the form is a three-stanza ballade with envoi and
  refrain, though the *Notice* says "ces strophes" (p. xiv). (iii) "of
  1467" is not the edition's; see §2. (iv) Chastellain's spider is not
  universal: his line is "tryacle contre araigne" (p. 207); "l'universel
  araigne" is p. 209, in the ballade Kervyn restores to Molinet. The
  draft's *structure* — Chastellain's image, generalised by Molinet — is
  vindicated at the page; its wording is not. Note also that the form the
  draft prints, "l'universelle aragne", does not occur here (full-text
  search: *aragne*, no hit; *araigne* at pp. xiv, 207, 208, 209 only), and
  must be pinned to its own Molinet edition or replaced by this one.
- **ch10/draft.md §V, "the losing side's description of the fiscal state's
  operator".** Half-corrected. Kervyn's spider designates "le monarque qui
  étendait ses intrigues sur toute l'Europe" — intrigue and broken faith,
  not the exchequer. The fiscal charge is in this volume, in other pieces
  (§4); the sentence should not rest on the epithet alone.
- **Nothing else.** No date, name or sequence in ch05, ch09 or ch10 is
  contradicted.

## 4. What it ADDS

**ch10 §V — the fiscal charge, T1, same hand.** *Le Prince*, twenty-five
stanzas, pp. 457–462; Kervyn's note, "Il est à peu près inutile d'apprendre
au lecteur que ce prince est Louis XI" (p. 457 n.), dated by him "vers
1470" (p. xx): "Prince aimant mieux argent et grosses sommes / Que le franc
cœur et l'amour de ses hommes … S'il pert et peuple et terre et baronnage,
/ Quant luy propre est la cause du dommage / Et qu'ainsi veult, de quoy
fait-il à plaindre?" (p. 458); and "Prince qui tout enfonse et escrutine /
Et tout applique à privée rapine … / Fors que tout tourne en son sac marc
et livre?" — a prince who sounds out and scrutinises everything and turns
it all to private rapine … so that all goes into his sack by mark and by
pound [TRANS. CLAUDE] (p. 461). With, from
the *Recollection des merveilles* in its continuation to 1466, the two acts
the Burgundian remembered: the princes' pensions withdrawn — "Oster de son
francq chois / Sa droite pension / Aux princes de son sang" (p. 202) — and
the pledged towns bought back for cash — "Grant finance et grant plue, /
Comme infinis argens, / Dont ses lieux engagiés / Luy furent tous rendus"
(p. 203). CAVEAT: the reply printed after *Le Prince* (pp. 463–486) is
Meschinot's, aimed at Charles the Bold, and is never Chastellain's.

**ch10 §VI — "one life long" gets the century's own figure.** *La Mort du
duc Philippe* makes the dead duke a glass phial hung from heaven on a
thread: "Or estoit ce vaissel de voirre / Pendant au ciel, ce me sembloit,
/ A un fillet qui le saingloit. / Par quoy appris que la fracture / En
pendoit fort en adventure; / Car quoyque riche fust et bel, / Sy estoit-il
matériel / Et se pouvoit rompre et desfaire / Par le seul fillet luy
fourtraire" (p. 240); then "Que le filet a pris escoeil / Et est couppé
tout au travers" (p. 242). With it: "Reste tout le plus fort à ce derrain:
c'est que tu congnoisses que tu es mortel homme et mourras comme ton père"
(*Advertissement*, p. 332), and, of the living duke, "C'est pité quant il
est mortel" (*La Paix de Péronne*, p. 451).

**ch10 §VI and ch05 — the lands' privileges as a standing limit.** The
*Advertissement*'s dame *Pesanteur de tes pays*: "Elle est grande en
considération de leur ancien tout viel ploy et usage, dont ne se
souffreroient frustrer, ne rompre. Elle est grande en considération de
leurs richesses et grandes puissances, de leurs usages et privilèges, et de
leurs lois lesquelles veullent avoir observées" (p. 306). Doctrine where
tome III gave an incident. Against it at T2, Kervyn on Charles's
joyous-accession demand at Valenciennes, March 1468 — "une somme de cent
mille livres tournois 'qu'envis luy accordèrent'" (p. xv, citing tome V p.
375, not in repo).

**ch09 — a continental vernacular of person against office.** "Un tyran tel
fois possède et règne … mais sy n'a-t-il titre pour tant, fors de régner …
Porter nom de prince tant seulement, c'est povre titre … Mais porter nom de
prince *princiant* est un haut titre" (p. 312); "prince ignorant … est
comme un homme despect et imparfait, **demy-homme, demy-riens**" (p. 318);
and, flattest, "l'effet demeure toudis et de l'effet procède le titre.
**Quant l'homme fault, jamais titre ne fault pour tant**" (p. 329) —
PENDING VERIFICATION whether *fault* is *fails* or *dies*; the words are
certain, the sense is not. The office as a commission to be accounted for:
"Tu trouveras à compter là-haut, comme tu auras usé de ta recepte en sa
commission çà-bas" (p. 332); to king and duke together, "Vostre
sempiternité n'est pas droit-cy, mais elle est bien ailleurs" (*Livre de
Paix*, p. 396). For the single-point-of-failure figure, the *Entrée* of
1461: the French throne is "le pôle immobile et celuy en qui tout tourne",
and if it fails "tout chiet et tombe, et fait rompre et cheoir à l'envers
toute la machine chrestienne" (pp. 30–31).

**ch09 and ch05 — the prince who will not be counselled.** The Roboam
typology: Charles is warned not to do as Roboam did at Solomon's death,
"abandonnant le conseil des vieux et des sages preud'hommes, déclina au
conseil des joveneurs à sa dure meschance" (p. 295); positively, "obéyr au
conseil des sages preud'hommes et expers de eage … et que tu ne mettes pas
ton cœur en mains de légière sorte, ne ton secret en bouche de flatteur"
(p. 309); the vice in *Le Prince*, "Prince qui hayt remoustrance et
doctrine" (p. 461). Kervyn quotes Chastellain's own summary of the man
addressed: "chaud en sa cause, bouillant en son mettre avant, dur en
opinion, aigre en son vouloir, périlleux en courroux" (p. xvi).

**ch05 — the magnate as royal creation, and what magnate capability was.**
Philip the Bold, "fils du roy Jehan de France", who "gouvernoit seul ce
royaume dessoubs son frère, et portoit le thrône de France, dont il estoit
pillier, sus ses espaules" (pp. 290–291). And, four pages on, the sentence
§VI wants: "Prince qui est bien aimé de ses nobles, ne peut succomber. Il
est riche de précieuse chair et a de sang à commandement, comme d'eau en
mer … Ton père avoit cœurs, foies et poumons; il avoit les vies et les
substances de ses subjets" — he is rich in precious flesh and has blood at
command as water in the sea; your father had hearts, livers and lungs, he
had the lives and the substance of his subjects [TRANS. CLAUDE] (p. 310).
The pre-fiscal definition of power, which is what the artillery state
replaces. Colour: the *Entrée* of 1461 makes France Mary, Paris Bethlehem
and the duke of Burgundy Joseph, guarding the royal house "en la garde de
toute humble administration" (pp. 6–7); and of the France Charles V
inherited, "le royaume espuisé en finance jusques à faire monnoye de cuir"
(p. 326).

**NEGATIVE FINDINGS, stated to close lines of enquiry.** Full-text search:
*artillerie* — none; *bombarde* — none; *canon* — four, all canon-law (pp.
83, 90); *taille* — two, both in the Gilles des Ormes ballade at p. 210,
not Chastellain's; *aide* — four, none fiscal-technical; *finance* — two
(pp. 203, 326); *aragne* — none. The tome supplies no ordnance and no
fiscal-institutional evidence whatever, and touches no English fact: nothing
on Henry VI, the protectorates or any incapacity, and the chronicle lacuna
of July 1453–August 1454 is not repaired.

## 5. What it CONTRADICTS

At full strength, two things, neither large.

1. **The spider is a heraldic quarrel before it is an economic diagnosis.**
   On the page the figure belongs to a dynastic bestiary — the lion of
   Flanders against Charles VI's winged stag, the spider added by "les
   poètes de la cour du duc Charles" (p. 208 n.) — and the vice it names is
   intrigue and concealed venom. Three of the four answering ballades argue
   about who is servant and who lord ("Que serf pervers sur son seigneur
   domine", p. 211). A reader who checks ch10 §V's citation finds a poets'
   war of badges, not a diagnosis of the fisc.
2. **The Burgundian witness lays the mortal-node charge against France
   too.** On Louis XI's accession: "au tour d'une main et en l'instant, par
   changement et mutation de roy, mutation aussi s'y trouva subite, et tout
   retourné au contraire … et d'anciennes salutaires coustumes fit
   abolissement volontaire" — in the turn of a hand and in an instant, by
   the change and mutation of a king, a sudden mutation was found there
   too, and everything turned to its contrary; and of ancient wholesome
   customs he made a wilful abolition [TRANS. CLAUDE] (p. 326, in the
   variant Kervyn prints from Ms. 8349). If a kingdom with standing
   companies and a permanent taille also turned over at one man's death,
   "one life long" discriminates less than ch10 §VI wants.

**The book's best answer.** To (1): concede and re-base. The fiscal charge
against Louis XI is in this volume at T1 — *Le Prince* p. 461, the
*Recollection* pp. 202–203. Carry the spider as the epithet it is and the
exchequer from those pins; stronger than the present sentence, not weaker.
Good. To (2): the distinction is between the polity and the policy. What
changed at the 1461 succession was personnel and direction, and
Chastellain, partisan of exactly the men dismissed, felt it as the end of
the world; what did not change was the *ordonnance* companies, the taille
and the two account series running unbroken from 1418. What changed in
Burgundy at Nancy was the existence of the state. Worth making in the text,
because a hostile contemporary crying that everything had turned to its
contrary, and being demonstrably wrong about the machinery, is the
ratchet's best witness. Neither objection reaches a spine §8 ruling or a
DEFEND-tier claim; (1) disciplines one sentence of ch10 §V, (2) sharpens
one of §VI.

  *Le Prince* p. 461 and the *Recollection* pp. 202–203; the ballade's

- **ch10 §VI:** T1, one sentence and notes — the glass phial (p. 240) for

  100,000 l.t. (p. xv) at T2 in a note.
- **ch09:** T1, by name once — p. 329 and p. 318 as the continental

- **ch05:** T1, footnote to §V/§VI — pp. 290–291 and p. 310; the Joseph
  figure (pp. 6–7) as colour.
- **Role:** evidence and rival at once. These are not narrative but
  *commissioned advocacy* — the *Advertissement* was carried to the duke by
  the author's own servant for sixty sous of wine — and the partisan caveat
  recorded for the chronicle on 14 September extends here in a sharper
  form. Kervyn's apparatus is T2 and is itself an advocate's; where he
  attributes or disattributes (p. 208 n.), the attribution travels with his
  name.

## 7. Verdict

The tome changes the book's weight at one pin and its accuracy at another:
it closes the [PIN PENDING] standing since 3 September, and in closing it
corrects the citation — Chastellain wrote "tryacle contre araigne" at p.
207, and "l'universel araigne" at p. 209 is, on Kervyn's own judgement,
Molinet's. It changes nothing in the book's direction or register, and
supplies no artillery and no fisc. The single most important thing to know
is that the edition does not date *Le Lyon rampant*: the only date in
Kervyn's note, "Plus tard, en 1467 et en 1468", belongs to the imitations,
so "a ballade of 1467" is an inference from the volume's own contents — a
funerary ballade on a dead lion, printed between the portrait of Philip the
Good and the November 1467 lamentation, in a house whose dead duke is "le
grand duc et le grand Lyon" — and must be carried as inference or dropped.
