# Assessment — William H. McNeill, *The Pursuit of Power: Technology, Armed Force, and Society since A.D. 1000* (University of Chicago Press, 1982)

## 1. The book in two paragraphs

McNeill's subject is not the state but the market, and the engine is the contest between "command" and "market" as ways of mobilising men and metal for war. The thesis is Chinese in origin: "it is the hypothesis of this book that China's rapid evolution towards market-regulated behavior in the centuries on either side of the year 1000 tipped a critical balance in world history" (p. 25 / PDF 34); thereafter "political authorities found it less and less possible to escape the trammels of finance" (p. 25). Europe is the society that let the market run: "Commercialization of organized violence came vigorously to the fore in the fourteenth century when mercenary armies became standard in Italy" (p. 69 / PDF 78), and "the arms race that continues to strain world balances in our own time descends directly from the intense interaction in matters military that European states and private entrepreneurs inaugurated during the fourteenth century" (p. 70 / PDF 79).

The whole millennium is, for McNeill, an interlude. The European "venture toward the sovereignty of the market in military as in other forms of management" is "an eccentric departure from the human norm of command behavior—the sort of behavior that dominated ancient times and has reasserted itself with remarkable power since the 1880s" (p. 116 / PDF 125). The Conclusion imagines the retrospect from centuries hence: "Political management, having monopolized the overt organization of armed force, resumed its primacy over human behavior" (p. 386 / PDF 395). That is the book's Part III wager, stated in 1982 by a world historian who thought it neither desirable nor optional. Tilly is not cited; the book predates the fiscal-military-state vocabulary, and its own coinage is the "military-commercial complex" (p. 117 / PDF 126).

## 2. What it CONFIRMS

**(a) The reconquest as artillery-driven, the fisc as its precondition (ch. 6 §§IV–VII; spine §8(a)).** "The French army that drove the English out of Normandy and Guienne, 1450–53, did so by bringing heavy artillery pieces to bear on castle walls, one after another, whereupon previously formidable defenses came tumbling down in a matter of hours, if the garrison did not prefer to surrender" (p. 83 / PDF 92) — with the surrender clause "walls and minds" needs. The preceding sentence carries the co-evolution: "an expanded tax base allowed the king to collect enough hard cash to support an increasingly formidable armed force" (p. 82). Fisc first, guns decisive: the ruling's order.

**(b) The affordability claim, in his exact words.** "The clumsy bombards of 1453 had already altered the balance between besieger and besieged, but the resulting disturbance to established power relationships was enormously magnified by the French and Burgundian invention of mobile siege guns between 1465 and 1477. Wherever the new artillery appeared, existing fortifications became useless. The power of any ruler who was able to afford the high cost of the new weapons was therefore enhanced at the expense of neighbors and subjects who were unable to avail themselves of the new technology of war. In Europe, the major effect of the new weaponry was to dwarf the Italian city-states and to reduce other small sovereignties to triviality" (p. 89 / PDF 98). The vocabulary matters: "cost", not "price", but "the high cost of the new weapons" — the object is the weapons, not the establishment. Rulings-sheet row (a) is not McNeill's formulation; he sits nearer the unit-cost reading Hall and Hoffman have made untenable, and must be cited for the direction of the effect ("neighbors and subjects"), not the vocabulary. His one establishment-level statement is fiscal, in the footnote the Rogers-ed assessment left at second hand: Bean (1973) "calculated that central government tax revenues in western Europe doubled in real, per capita terms between 1450 and 1500, but grew more slowly thereafter" (p. 105 n. 35 / PDF 114).

**(c) The condottieri market and its domestication (ch. 4 §§II–III).** Manuscript: Italy domesticated its mercenaries "by *market competition among solvent buyers*". McNeill: the whole ran "from a nearly free market … towards oligopoly … followed by quasi-monopoly within each of the larger and better-administered states" (p. 77 / PDF 86). Venice escaped Sforza's fate "partly by dividing contracts among several different, mutually jealous captains, and partly by bestowing civic honors and gifts upon loyal and successful condottieri and arranging suitable marriages" (p. 76 / PDF 85) — Colleoni's bargain, at T2.

**(d) The Swiss control group (ch. 10 §VI).** "If Charles the Bold had been able to bring his artillery to bear against the Swiss in 1476–77, the history of Europe might have taken a very different turn" (p. 118 n. 1 / PDF 127); the Swiss then "became a pillar of the French monarchy" (p. 136 / PDF 145).

**(e) The Ottoman turn (ch. 11 §IV).** Western superiority "became apparent to the Ottoman Turks in the war of 1593–1606, when, for the first time, Turkish cavalry met disciplined infantry gunfire" (p. 98 / PDF 107, citing İnalcık 1975).

## 3. What it CORRECTS

**(i) The manuscript does not cite him, and the words it remembers are not his.** manuscript.md contains no "McNeill"; no sources.md, critiques.md or appendix carries him. The only trace is ch06/memo.md l. 142, reported via Wikipedia (T4). The tasking's recollected phrases — "made the old style of fortification obsolete"; "the balance of power … in favour of the central authorities" — do not occur in this text. His words are "existing fortifications became useless" (p. 89) and, for Asia, "once a decisive advantage accrued to central authorities through the use and monopolization of heavy guns" (p. 98 / PDF 107). Nothing may be cited in the remembered form.

**(ii) The Bureaux, Formigny, Castillon and Charles VII are absent.** None is named anywhere; the reconquest is one sentence (p. 83). The memo's negative finding on the trunnion is verified at first hand: McNeill credits "the gunfounders of the Low Countries and France" with the mobile gun, trunnions, trail and limber, no names (pp. 87–88 / PDF 96–97). Drop the Wikipedia intermediary.

**(iii) His technical chronology is superseded and must not enter ch. 6 §III.** Iron shot and corned powder arrive together "between 1465 and 1477" (pp. 87–88); the 1453 guns are "clumsy bombards" (p. 89). Hall 1997 (in corpus) dates Bureau's new powder and cast-iron shot to 1453. McNeill's authorities are Cipolla 1965 and Duffy 1979 (p. 88 n. 21; p. 89 n. 22); Hall is later and closer to the accounts. Record the disagreement; cite Hall.

**(iv) Ch. 10 §VI, the Swiss export.** Manuscript: "the first formal Franco-Swiss troop convention following in 1521." McNeill: "In 1479 Louis XI of France disbanded his French infantry forces and made a contract with the Swiss instead" (p. 136 n. 18, citing Contamine, *Guerre*, p. 284, in corpus). Carry 1479.

## 4. What it ADDS

**(1) The trace italienne as the reason the settlement runs inside blocs rather than across them — for ch. 10 §IV (which stops at 1494) and ch. 12.** The manuscript never uses the phrase; Hall and Rogers-ed left the objection standing. McNeill supplies, six years before Parker, the two-sided answer: "By the 1520s, fortifications on the new Italian model were again quite capable of resisting even the best-equipped attackers. But their cost was enormous. Only the wealthiest states and cities could afford the scores of cannon and the enormous labor of construction required by the trace italienne" (p. 90 / PDF 99); and the consequence: "high technology once again favored local defenses, at least in those regions where governments could afford the cost of the new fortifications … This put a very effective obstacle in the way of the political consolidation of Europe into a single imperial unity" (p. 91 / PDF 100); "Imperial consolidation halted halfway" (p. 91). The guns consolidated within the polities that could pay; the fortress froze the map between them. That is "three or four blocs" as sixteenth-century mechanism, and it belongs in the text by name.

**(2) The gunpowder empires and the innovation freeze — for ch. 11 §IV, ch. 12 and Appendix C.** Outside Europe "the Italian riposte to cannon fire was not forthcoming", and the Mughal, Muscovite and Ottoman empires' extent "was defined in practice by the mobility of their respective imperial gun parks" (p. 95 / PDF 104). Then the signature of completed consolidation: "once a decisive advantage accrued to central authorities through the use and monopolization of heavy guns, further spontaneous improvements in gunpowder weapons ceased" (p. 98); the law recurs in drilled Europe, where "changes in handguns … almost came to a halt after about 1690" (p. 141 / PDF 150). A consolidated stack that stops improving is a measurable, dated indicator; Appendix C has none of that kind.

**(3) Liège, 1492 — the foundry's leverage, for chs 2–3 and 12 §III.** "Their very weakness thus allowed the Liégeois to set their own prices. Even the mightiest rulers had to pay what was asked, or do without" (p. 113 / PDF 122); and the condition: "As long as no single political command structure could reach out to every corner of Latin Christendom … the sovereignty of the market over even the greatest ruler of the age remained an ultimate reality" (p. 114 / PDF 123). The foundry in the neutral jurisdiction is the sixteenth century's Hsinchu; the bloc settlement is the removal of that condition inside each bloc. Own the bridge as (c).

**(4) Wallenstein, for ch. 4 §IV beside Prigozhin.** "The logic of his position perhaps required him to imitate the usurpation that Sforza had carried through in Milan in 1450" (p. 121 / PDF 130); assassinated 1634, lands and offices confiscated (p. 120).

**(5) China, for ch. 2 and the spine's ranking.** Sung officials "might prefer preemption, and simply turn the business in question into a state monopoly" (p. 49 / PDF 58); "Uninhibited linkage between military and commercial enterprise, such as was to take place in fourteenth- to nineteenth-century Europe, would have seemed truly disastrous to Chinese officials" (p. 40 / PDF 49). Against Andrade (in corpus): both hold that China had guns first and nothing left to ratchet against — "The technology of Chinese armament also lent itself to the maintenance of bureaucratic supremacy" (p. 36 / PDF 45); they disagree on the divergence, McNeill's cause being institutional (p. 112 / PDF 121) where Andrade's is walls and the frequency of war. Cite both.

**(6) Small pins.** France 1478: "4,142 'lances' outnumbered Milan's more than 4 to 1" (p. 83 n. 14; Contamine p. 200). Cast iron from 1543 "cheapened big guns to about a twelfth of their former cost" (p. 86 / PDF 95). Spain's 40 per cent debt service by 1600 (p. 110 / PDF 119, Thompson) for ch. 11.

## 5. What it CONTRADICTS

**(i) The consolidator is the market, and the ratchet is Venetian and administrative, not French and ballistic.** "a regular standing army of known size and capability emerged in the better-governed cities of Italy during the first half of the fifteenth century" (p. 75 / PDF 84) — before the guns mattered; the suppression of the Fronde "marked the time at which transalpine states finally caught up with the level of administrative management and control over armed force that had been attained in Venice and Milan two centuries earlier" (p. 125 / PDF 134). Civil control of force was solved by contract administration in the Po valley without a siege train; the northern monarchies took two centuries to copy it; artillery is an episode inside a story whose motor is credit. This reaches spine §8(a) (the technology at the irreversibility point) and the DEFEND-tier mechanism.

*The book's best answer.* McNeill supplies it. The Italian solution was sovereign only until a polity with siege guns arrived: "When newly consolidated monarchies, commanding comparatively vast territories, chose to intervene in Italian affairs, the sovereignty of mere city-states, however skillfully managed, could not permanently be maintained" (p. 79 / PDF 88). Venice solved civil control of force; it did not solve the minimum scale of a sovereign, which is what ch. 6 §VII's last sentence prices, and ch. 10 §VI already carries Venice as "the fisc without the ratchet". Good — but the manuscript should say plainly, naming McNeill, that the administrative achievement was Italian and earlier, and that the guns added scale and the end of the small sovereign, not the invention of civil control. Stated so, the 2 September ruling (the ratchet's object is others' ability to reverse the settlement) survives him.

**(ii) The state did not absorb the stack; the stack held the state to ransom for two centuries.** "command simply could not prevail against the market as a way to marshal men and resources" (p. 114); Philip II "knew how to tax and how to confiscate just as effectively as Chinese and Islamic officials did … But alas for the command principle! … His repeated efforts to establish factories producing cannon and other needed commodities always failed to flourish" (p. 113); "even the mightiest European command structures became dependent on an international money and credit market for organizing military and other major undertakings" (p. 115 / PDF 124). Read straight: the artillery state subordinated its barons and was itself subordinated by its bankers and gunmakers. This reaches CLAUDE.md §1 claim 3(i) and spine §8(b) ("the state will absorb the stack").

*The book's best answer.* Three limbs. First, the dependence ran on jurisdictional refuge — "Dozens of other refuges for entrepreneurs were scattered across the face of Europe, thanks to its peculiarly fragmented political geography" (p. 114) — and the bloc settlement is the closure of refuge inside the bloc; the interstices are where Liège survives. Second, McNeill concedes the hybrid: "in the long run European states actually were strengthened by their involvement in the fiscal web spun by international bankers and suppliers" (p. 114). Third, his long arc ends where the book's does (p. 116). Honestly assessed: good on direction, exposed on timetable. The barons went first and the bankers held out for two hundred years; if the analogy is exact, the compute-foundry layer keeps its price long after the private-force layer has been commissioned. Ch. 12's per-bloc verdicts should carry that lag as a stated risk, and Appendix C should score the foundry's pricing power separately from the contractors' subordination.

**(iii) Irreversibility is dated to drill, not to guns.** "until after the middle of the seventeenth century, even in the best-governed states of western Europe, subjects retained the option of armed revolt against royal tax collectors and could expect to prevail if enough of their fellows felt the same way" (p. 105); only after Maurice's drill did "Neither aristocratic challenges to royal power nor lower-class protests against perceived injustice" have "the slightest chance of success as long as well-drilled troops were available to defend royal prerogatives" (p. 139 / PDF 148). Roberts's chronology with a psychological mechanism; it cuts at "irreversible" in §8(a).

*The book's best answer.* The ruling's object is narrow: what became irreversible in 1449–53 was the assemblies' and magnates' ability to reverse the fiscal settlement; no Estates General met until 1484. McNeill's residual is the popular tax revolt — ch. 7's material, already treated as the mark of a settlement made over the heads of those who paid. Adequate if ch. 10 §VII states the two-stage closure — top of the polity in the 1450s, bottom after 1650 — and names McNeill for the second; not adequate if "irreversible" is left to imply both.

## 7. Verdict

McNeill changes the book's weight and one piece of its architecture, not its direction: he is the T2 world-historical statement of the affordability effect and of the market-to-command return the book predicts, and his trace italienne pages hand ch. 10 the answer it has not yet given to the fortress objection — the guns consolidated inside polities while the bastion froze the map between them, which is the bloc settlement in sixteenth-century form. The single most important thing the author should know is that McNeill's fifteenth century belongs to Venice, not to the Bureaux: civil control of organised force was solved by Italian contract administration before the guns mattered, and artillery's contribution in his account is scale and the end of the small sovereign — say so by name and let the ratchet be defined that narrowly. Secondary: his sixteenth century is the neomedieval objection from inside the consolidation — the barons went first and the gunmakers of Liège held their price for two centuries — and ch. 12's timetable must carry that lag.
