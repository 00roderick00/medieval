# Assessment — Jan Glete, *War and the State in Early Modern Europe: Spain, the Dutch Republic and Sweden as fiscal-military states, 1500–1660* (Routledge, 2002)

*Offset: printed p. N = PDF p. N + 9 throughout, notes included (printed p. 10 = PDF p. 19; printed p. 218 = PDF p. 227). Read in full: Prologue, chs. 1–6 (pp. 1–217). Notes searched by author and keyword and read at ch. 1 nn. 10–14 and ch. 2 nn. 24–48; bibliography and index not read. Text layer clean; the four tables are scrambled and nothing is quoted from them. Negative findings, verified full-text: "Charles VII", "Charles VIII" and "1440" do not occur; "cannon" once (p. 135). The manuscript does not cite Glete; ch10/memo.md l. 204 names him as the scholar who extended Brewer's term "backward to 1500+"; ch10/sources.md l. 474 lists him only as post-dating Spruyt. Role: T2; ally on the mechanism, rival on the chronology.*

## 1. The book in two paragraphs

Glete's keyword is neither coercion nor consensus: "If a single keyword should be selected, it is organisation rather than coercion or consensus" (p. 2 / PDF 11). The fiscal-military state was "a complex organisation, a new type of social system with a dynamic and logic of its own" (p. 2 / PDF 11), resting on "a double contractual relationship, one between rulers and the society and another between rulers and their armed forces" (p. 4 / PDF 13). Its engine is not the gun: "In this study, the 'motor of change' is the bargaining process about political and organisational innovations to enforce a monopoly of violence and an efficient defence" (p. 8 / PDF 17). The theory is Lane's protection-selling married to Williamson's transaction costs — market or hierarchy decided by asset specificity, of which "Weapons and trained military units are typical examples" (p. 57 / PDF 66) — and the finding is that the market system "had by 1700 been almost totally replaced by a system where each European state ran its own permanent army and navy" (p. 56 / PDF 65).

The chronology is his quarrel with this manuscript. Three phases: co-operation within the traditional states 1480–1560, when "The fiscal-military state had begun to take shape, but it was largely an embryo where taxes and customs were sufficient only to support the nucleus of permanent armed forces" (p. 21 / PDF 30); crisis 1560–1660, whose selection is his "only claim to originality" (p. 16 / PDF 25); and a Europe of fiscal-military states after 1660. The pioneers are Spain, the Dutch Republic and Sweden; the type was "practically unknown in 1500" (p. 41 / PDF 50). His sentence for the mechanism: "Wars were not decided by the existence of resources but by how these resources were organised" (p. 215 / PDF 224).

## 2. What it CONFIRMS

**Ch. 10 §I, §IV, §VII — the guns as the state's, and why.** The establishment-cost answer to Hoffman is carried at T2 in transaction-cost form: "Heavy guns were capital goods, and mobile siege artillery required a permanent organisation of skilled men … To realise the full potential of improvements in guns, metallurgy, warship design and fortification, costly experiments were necessary. They needed to be based on consistent demand expressed in willingness to pay for development and unavoidable failures. This gave the state a comparative advantage in violence control, as even the limited power of late medieval states was larger than that of any individual power-holder in a territory" (p. 65 / PDF 74). The before-state: "Artillery, especially the heavy siege guns, were usually provided by the state, but primitive gunpowder weapons were widely dispersed in late medieval society" (p. 11 / PDF 20).

**Ch. 6 and Ch. 10 §II.** "In the early 1450s, the French kings had been able to conquer the large French territories earlier controlled by the English kings: Normandy and Guyenne. Efficient siege artillery was important in these victories" (p. 19 / PDF 28), on Hall, for whom gunpowder weapons "were important in the French victory in the Hundred Years War (1450–53), in the War of Granada (1481–92)" (n. 10, p. 221 / PDF 230). The compagnies: "Its nominal size varied from 14,000 to 17,000 men" (p. 32 / PDF 41). England "sank into a deep political crisis from the 1450s to the 1480s" (p. 20 / PDF 29).

**Ch. 10 §III — the retrojection sentence.** The manuscript's "extended backward by later scholarship to about 1500; no scholarly authority applies it to Charles VII's France" (l. 6008–12) is exactly right; Glete is that scholarship. See §5(i).

**Ch. 11 §III — Spain's moves.** In the Granada war "Much of the war effort was paid for by taxes to the state, not by the participants … This was the basic feature of the fiscal-military state" (p. 75 / PDF 84); "Private armies as instruments of aristocratic factionalism ceased to be important after this war, and the noblemen's castles had become vulnerable to the rulers' new siege artillery" (p. 75); the hermandad forces "abolished in 1498" (p. 75); the orders "permanently incorporated into the crown in 1523, a change that eliminated autonomous military structures" (p. 76 / PDF 85).

**Ruling (b), "subordinating".** "The older power structures were mobilised for a more ambitious policy and in that process were transformed rather than suppressed" (p. 7 / PDF 16).

**Spine §8(g) — the form is open.** "Strong organisational structures may be compatible with highly authoritarian, constitutional and in the end also democratic political systems" (p. 212 / PDF 221); "the Riksdag is proof that a strong, centralised and militarised state was fully compatible with a parliament" (p. 193 / PDF 202); and the precedent for (g)(2)'s demonstrable separation — in 1680 Karl XI "could use these resentments to create absolutist rule (without abolishing the parliament) with the consent of the peasants" (p. 195 / PDF 204).

**Ch. 10 §VII, Objection 6.** Glete on *Coercion, Capital*: it "is uncertain about its geography and chronology, and so much centred on the dichotomy between the logics of coercion and capital that it fails to observe that both paths of development resulted in permanent organisations and fiscal-military states" (n. 28, p. 227 / PDF 236); Tilly "was not interested in the result of the state-formation process, the state as a complex organisation with a dynamic of its own" (p. 49 / PDF 58).

## 3. What it CORRECTS

1. **Manuscript (Ch. 10 §II), flagged tertiary, "some 24,000 men by 1483"; Glete p. 32 / PDF 41: "14,000 to 17,000 men" nominal, "40,000 to 45,000" only for "Temporary war efforts".** With Parrott p. 30, two T2 authorities now stand against the figure.

2. **Manuscript (Ch. 10 §VI): Venice "had the centre and the capital without the apparatus".** Glete p. 13 / PDF 22: "Venice also had a large permanent and state-administered navy and may in terms of permanent armed force have ranked number one in Europe in 1500." Lane's "almost no bureaucracy" is not "no apparatus"; say Venice had the permanent naval organisation and lacked the fiscal-administrative penetration.

3. **Manuscript (Ch. 11 §III): the monarchy "fielded decisive force for a century and a half on Castile, credit and silver".** Glete p. 24 / PDF 33: "usually regarded as a Castilian fiscal-military state in which the other parts contributed little to the war effort, but actually Habsburg Italy, Portugal and the Habsburg-controlled parts of the Netherlands shared much of the economic burdens of war"; the peacetime tercios in Italy "were paid for by the local tax-payers, who in this form bought protection from Spain" (p. 79 / PDF 88). Add Naples and the Italian fisc.

Nothing else in chapters 6, 10, 11 or 12 is contradicted on a fact or date.

## 4. What it ADDS

**Ch. 12 §IV and Ch. 3 — the vocabulary for "control of the decisive layer, provision by contract".** "complex military systems depend on a wide spectrum of different competencies. Some may be described as core or system competencies, highly specific for such a complex and policy-determined military system, while others are less specific to that system and easier to specify in a formal contract with an entrepreneur … The asiento system as it developed in the Spanish monarchy to an increasing extent left core competencies to entrepreneurs, who developed skills so specialised that the state often had no realistic alternative but to negotiate new contracts with little competition" (p. 131 / PDF 140). The navy run on contract "decade after decade" weakened the organisation "as a social power container for skills" (p. 134 / PDF 143); by 1633 the crown was "practically making the entire Spanish weapon industry private" (p. 135 / PDF 144) — Parrott's cast-iron point from the other side. This is the T2 statement of what an American state may license out and what it cannot.

**Ch. 12 §III — legibility at T2, a century before Scott's cases.** Sweden's bailiffs "compiled registers of all farms in their districts and the taxes they should pay"; from the 1620s "about 2,000 local priests" kept "lists of the inhabitants in their parishes and the ages of the men" (pp. 189–90 / PDF 198–99); "The system was independent of local elite groups, which probably made it unique among early modern fiscal-military states … Similar systems in other countries have usually been created only in the nineteenth century" (p. 189). Ch. 11's fifth move in its purest form.

**Ch. 12 §IV — the Dutch, against the Tilly assessment's EU worry.** Tilly's capital-intensive path was read as predicting a durable EU without apparatus. Glete: the Dutch army reached "5–6 per cent of the population, while the French army never rose above 2 per cent" (p. 157 / PDF 166); "the Dutch state developed the core competence that was necessary to run a large, modern, permanent navy" despite the market (p. 166 / PDF 175); after 1652 it kept "two navies": "the battle fleet, paid for by taxes and serving the interests of the federation and the territorial state; and the cruiser fleet, paid for by customs duties and serving the interests of trade" (p. 171 / PDF 180). The route was "from below (induced by demand)": "it was easier to build a strong state from below than for a central ruler to penetrate local society in order to become powerful" (p. 149 / PDF 158). A third EU reading beside binary and Dutch-niche: consolidation by member demand, the strategic layer financed apart from the commercial one.

**Quotable.** Trivulzio's "money; more money; and still more money" (1499) against Olivares, 1637, "men, money, order and obedience" (p. 126 / PDF 135, via Hale p. 232 — verify at Hale); "The situation of medieval states varied between that of the United Nations and NATO in the late twentieth century" (p. 12 / PDF 21).

## 5. What it CONTRADICTS

**(i) No fiscal-military state before 1560, and France's early machine did not hold.** "practically unknown in 1500" (p. 41 / PDF 50); 1480–1560 "largely an embryo" (p. 21 / PDF 30). "France, which is often regarded as the pioneer for a permanent army, might have developed similar forces if it had won the imperial power struggle in Italy. Instead, the early but very limited permanent French forces practically disappeared in the long civil war period (1562–98)" (p. 39 / PDF 48); "The most decisive change was that which took place in France in the 1660s … Colbert was in many respects the architect of the French fiscal-military state" (p. 28 / PDF 37). Spine §8(a) says 1449–53 made the settlement irreversible; Ch. 10 §III calls the French 1440s "the coupling's first full demonstration". On Glete the first full demonstration is Spain and the French instance reversed within a century. This reaches §8(a) and the DEFEND-tier mechanism.

**(ii) The motor is bargaining; the gun is a window, and hedged.** "This technological explanation has been common for a long time, but it has been challenged by specialists in Renaissance warfare" (p. 18 / PDF 27); "The emergence of efficient heavy guns *may* thus have created a window of opportunity for centralising rulers" (p. 19 / PDF 28; emphasis added); "efficient political interest aggregation was a necessary precondition for the growth of large and complex military organisations" (p. 216 / PDF 225). Thompson, endorsed: "the cost of war hardly increased markedly during the early modern period … the Military Revolution was a redeployment of the costs of war between society and state" (p. 47 / PDF 56). Black's prime mover for 1470–1530 is "compromises and co-operation between rulers and social elites" (p. 46 / PDF 55).

**(iii) The ratchet is reversible: Spain de-articulated.** His pioneer is his one absolute failure: "The Spanish military and naval decline was absolute, not relative, and there is no comparable case among other early modern fiscal-military states" (p. 139 / PDF 148). The core's fisc was never permanent: the millones of 1590 were raised by "the cities represented in the Cortes … on their own", so that "the local elites took control of essential parts of the tax administration" (p. 122 / PDF 131); "In the early 1620s, the crown tried to change the tax into a permanent obligation to maintain 30,000 soldiers, but that was denied" (p. 122).

**(iv) Castile-as-core (rulings (h), (n)).** The manuscript: the settlement "was executed in Castile, and Castile ruled the rest of the monarchy by contract". Glete: the monarchy "was a purely dynastic union of several sovereign kingdoms and principalities. Essentially, it was a military alliance with highly integrated operational forces under the command of the king" (p. 117 / PDF 126); Castile's centrality "was more or less given by the circumstances" after 1559 (p. 104 / PDF 113); the monarchy "was never subordinated to the interests of the Castilian elite groups" (p. 117). And the core was the least consolidated part: "one of the marked features of the future Spanish fiscal-military state remained the low level of permanent forces on the Iberian peninsula. In Iberia, the Spanish monarchy was essentially based on its power to co-ordinate civil society" (p. 101 / PDF 110); "there are few signs of deep penetration of a royal bureaucracy into local communities" (p. 138 / PDF 147); by the 1660s Castile "had no important infrastructure of permanent domestic military units" (p. 94 / PDF 103). The core's intermediaries held the tax administration and in the end "had gained the power to dismantle large parts of the Spanish fiscal-military state" (p. 126 / PDF 135).

**(v) The navy: was artillery the ratchet?** His most capital-intensive, most state-monopolised organisations are navies, and their origin is not a siege story: "Most of the earliest sailing navies were created on the European periphery: in Portugal, England, Denmark-Norway and Sweden. They were small forces created by naval-minded kings for protection selling and as instruments of dynastic ambition … Private trade and shipping interests were of little importance for the early growth of sailing navies" (p. 39 / PDF 48). And "the earliest major permanent armed forces in Europe were created for imperial defence (outside the metropolitan territory) in the Mediterranean" (p. 39). The state-owned decisive organisation appears first where no wall was broken.

**(vi) Broad taxation against credit and rents (spine §8(g)(4)).** His strongest states are his most represented: "the Swedish state could extract more resources from free peasants with parliamentary representation than contemporary European states could extract from unrepresented and often unfree peasants" (p. 194 / PDF 203); the Dutch "raised more resources per capita than any other seventeenth-century state" (p. 172 / PDF 181). His unbargained case is Spain: resources "increasingly used to pay interest on debts to those who extracted the resources, and the proportion of resources that were actually used for military purposes declined steadily" (p. 126 / PDF 135); in Naples "It was partly the state's own financial apparatus that had become a new aristocracy" (p. 125 / PDF 134).

**The book's best answer.** On (i) and (iii): Glete measures a different object. His yardstick — "the most visible sign of a fully fledged fiscal-military state is if the bulk of the armed forces, their organisational structure and the taxes required to support them are retained when the state is at peace" (p. 39 / PDF 48) — is a yardstick for armies. The manuscript's ratchet is fiscal and constitutional: what 1449–53 made irreversible was the assemblies' and magnates' power to reverse the settlement of 1439–46 (§8(a), 2 September). Glete concedes the permanent army of 14,000–17,000 and never examines the taille, the élus or the Estates; the Wars of Religion hollowed the crown's collection (Parrott's League collecting the taxes) without restoring the Estates' consent, which is the ratchet's object. Ch. 10 §III should say this against him by name. On Spain the answer is better: the reversal happened in a core whose fisc was never made permanent — the millones time-limited, city-collected, permanence "denied" — which is the Burgundy finding of Ch. 10 §VI (offices without an irreversible fisc) at imperial scale, and ruling (aa)'s "money collected, not money planned". Spain is evidence *for* the two-variable finding. That answer is good, but it costs a sentence the manuscript lacks: Castile subordinated its *military* intermediaries (orders, castles, private armies — Glete pp. 75–76 confirm all three) and not its *fiscal* ones (the Cortes cities), which is why it could field decisive force for a century and a half and could not sustain it — the narrowing ruled at (h). On (iv), therefore, the answer is partial and (n)'s wording is exposed: "subordinates the intermediaries of the core polity" is true of Castile's armed intermediaries and false of its fiscal ones, and Ch. 11 must say which. On (ii): his p. 65 is the ratchet in his own vocabulary, and his p. 18 concedes the dating — the decades in which heavy guns beat medieval walls "were – and this is important in an explanation – the same as those when state monopolies of violence became common" (p. 18 / PDF 27). The dispute is "window" against "lock"; §8(a) already forbids the gun as motor, and the quarrel is fought there with his *may* quoted. On (v): the navy is the same mechanism in a second element, and he says so — "the heavier types of ordnance … could only be carried by special warships of a size and construction that made them less economic as merchantmen" (p. 65 / PDF 74); artillery afloat made the warship non-dual-use as it made the wall obsolete, and Parrott's p. 290 concession is Glete's premise. But the naval case shows the state-owned decisive organisation arising first at sea and in the empire, so artillery-against-walls is one form of the capital-intensity ratchet, not its only one; the spine's "the ratchet" is its French form, and the Coda's method note should say so. On (vi): Glete's Spain is the case §8(g)(4) lacked — consolidation financed by silver, Church taxes and asiento credit rather than bargained taxation de-articulated within a generation into a rentier elite — and his Sweden is the other branch, absolutism made "with the consent of the peasants". Neither reaches a ruling; (vi) turns the ch12 memo's "dark branch" from conjecture into a case.

## 7. Verdict

Glete changes the book's register, not its direction: the mechanism survives him — his p. 65 states it in transaction-cost terms, his Spain confirms every one of Ch. 11's five moves and his n. 28 says of Tilly what Objection 6 says — but he is the authority for the very term Ch. 10 retrojects, he dates the fiscal-military state to 1560–1660 with France's early machine collapsing in 1562–98, and the chapter cannot use his word without meeting his clock by name. The single most important thing the author should know is that Glete's pioneer, Spain, is his one absolute reversal, and it reversed because the core's fisc was never made permanent while its armed intermediaries were subordinated — so his Spanish chapter at once threatens (n)'s "intermediaries of the core polity" and supplies the sentence that rescues it, and gives spine §8(g)(4) its historical case: consolidation financed by silver, Church taxes and contractor credit rather than bargained taxation de-articulated within a generation. Two wordings follow: Ch. 11 must say which intermediaries Castile subordinated, and the spine's "the ratchet" must be read as its French form, because Glete's navies show the state-owned decisive organisation arising first where no wall was broken.
