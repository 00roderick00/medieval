## 1. The book in two paragraphs

Krasner's subject is the gap between what rulers say sovereignty is and what they do. He separates four usages that "are not logically coupled, nor have they covaried in practice": "domestic sovereignty, referring to the organization of public authority within a state and to the level of effective control exercised by those holding authority; interdependence sovereignty, referring to the ability of public authorities to control transborder movements; international legal sovereignty, referring to the mutual recognition of states or other entities; and Westphalian sovereignty, referring to the exclusion of external actors from domestic authority configurations" (p. 9 / PDF p. 19). Beneath the four runs the distinction that matters most here: "Westphalian sovereignty and international legal sovereignty exclusively refer to issues of authority … Interdependence sovereignty exclusively refers to control … Domestic sovereignty is used in ways that refer to both authority and control" (p. 10 / PDF p. 20). The study concerns the two authority-sovereignties only; domestic sovereignty is "the single most important question for political analysis, but the organization of authority within a state and the level of control enjoyed by the state are not necessarily related to international legal or Westphalian sovereignty" (p. 12 / PDF p. 22).

The thesis is stated flat: "the most important empirical conclusion of the present study is that the principles associated with both Westphalian and international legal sovereignty have always been violated. Neither Westphalian nor international legal sovereignty has ever been a stable equilibrium from which rulers had no incentives to deviate. Rather, Westphalian and international legal sovereignty are best understood as examples of organized hypocrisy" (p. 24 / PDF p. 34). Rulers, not states, are the actors; "logics of consequences dominate the logics of appropriateness" (p. 6); the Westphalian model "is a well-understood cognitive script, one that is sometimes honored and sometimes not" (p. 69 / PDF p. 79). The evidence is four centuries of external actors ruling inside states — toleration clauses guaranteed by France and Sweden in 1648, minority treaties from Berlin to Dayton, the Ottoman Public Debt Administration, IMF conditionality, Soviet officers in the Polish security ministry — and a bestiary of recognised non-states (the Order of Malta, Andorra, the Dominions). His last line: "In a contested environment in which actors, including the rulers of states, embrace different norms, clubs can always be trump" (p. 238 / PDF p. 248).

## 2. What it CONFIRMS

**(a) The Westphalian myth — ch. 1 §VI's load-bearing paragraph, with an IR predecessor.** Manuscript (l. 715–22): "Osiander's demolition of the 'Westphalian myth' shows the 1648 treaties concerned overwhelmingly with the Holy Roman Empire's internal constitution — the sovereign-state reading is a nineteenth- and twentieth-century retrojection." Krasner, two years before Osiander's 2001 article and citing his 1994 book: "The norm of nonintervention in internal affairs had virtually nothing to do with the Peace of Westphalia, which was signed in 1648. It was not clearly articulated until the end of the eighteenth century" (p. 20 / PDF p. 30). From the Osnabrück articles (pp. 79–81): "Westphalia was a contractual arrangement in which the emperor invited French and Swedish oversight of constitutional practices in the empire in exchange for an end to the Thirty Years' War" (p. 81 / PDF p. 91); and "The Peace of Westphalia was a break point with the past but it is not the one understood by most students of international relations … Westphalia did mark the transition from Christendom to reason of state and balance of power … But it accomplished this by violating the principle of autonomy" (p. 82 / PDF p. 92). With it, ch. 1's claim that nested authority is not anomalous in the system's grammar: the powers "'resuscitated pre-Westphalian forms of divided sovereignty' such as protectorates and subordinate states" (p. 6 / PDF p. 16, quoting Strang).

**(b) Hollow sovereignty — ch. 5's title condition in Krasner's terms.** Manuscript (l. 2047–52): "the crown as a legal fiction disconnected from executive capacity — authority intact in law, in ceremony … and unable to raise a tax, relieve a fortress or discipline a magnate." Krasner: "A state can have international legal sovereignty, be recognized by other states, but have only the most limited domestic sovereignty either in the sense of an established structure of authority or the ability of its rulers to exercise control over what is going on within their own territory. In the 1990s some failed states in Africa, such as Somalia, served as unfortunate examples" (p. 4 / PDF p. 14). Ch. 5 §VI's fragile-state archipelago has its IR warrant here.

**(c) Functional versus formal sovereignty — ch. 4's promoted thread.** Manuscript (l. 1695–97): "functional sovereignty and formal sovereignty are separable, and the functional kind can run a great polity for generations without the formal kind ever arriving." Krasner: "A state such as Taiwan can have Westphalian sovereignty, but not international legal sovereignty" (p. 4 / PDF p. 14). Sforza's fifty years without investiture is domestic control without international legal sovereignty; the vocabulary exists and should be borrowed.

**(d) The fiscal-military state, from the credit side.** "Rulers in early modern Europe were compelled to borrow and sorely tempted to default" (p. 129 / PDF p. 139); and, via North–Weingast and Brewer, "The creation of the Bank of England, primarily as a device for providing transparent information about royal borrowing, made it possible for the British government to secure lower interest rates and contributed to Britain's ultimate military triumph over France" (p. 130 / PDF p. 140).

**(e) State capacity rising with flows — spine §7's pattern from outside the book.** "the level of government spending for developed countries has increased along with various measures of globalization since 1950" (p. 13 / PDF p. 23); "globalization and state activity have moved in tandem" (p. 223 / PDF p. 233).

## 3. What it CORRECTS

Nothing factual. One precision point, and it is not small.

**(i) "Exclusive" overclaims.** Manuscript ch. 1 §VI (l. 741–46): the plural order was "hammered into the exclusive territorial states we mistake for nature" and produced "the most exclusive form of political authority ever devised"; l. 742: "the exclusive sovereignties of 1700"; Introduction (l. 55): "the world before exclusive territorial sovereignty". Krasner pp. 20, 81 and 238 say the exclusion of external authority was never the practice of any period: "There has never been some ideal time during which all, or even most, political entities conformed with all of the characteristics that have been associated with sovereignty—territory, control, recognition, and autonomy" (p. 238 / PDF p. 248). The paragraph needs only that the state was *made*; "exclusive" is the adjective a Krasner-reading reviewer will strike. The fix is in §5.

**(ii) A period caution for ch. 10, matching the one entered against Scott.** Citing Tilly: "In the early modern era European rulers were highly dependent on international finance, much more dependent than would be the case for any developed states in the contemporary era. It was only in the nineteenth century that the major European governments developed sophisticated national systems of finance including revenue collection" (p. 221 / PDF p. 231). Ch. 10 claims permanence and collection, not sophistication; the distinction should be visible wherever 1439–46 is called a fiscal state.

## 4. What it ADDS

**(1) A vocabulary for what the platforms breach and what the state reasserts — the tasking's second question, answered yes.** In Krasner's grid, ch. 2's "new church" breaches *Westphalian* sovereignty only if it claims authority rather than control; and he supplies the test in a sentence about actors he did not have in mind: NGOs "like Amnesty International try to change the practices and policies of governments as does the Catholic Church. Such private actors are not violating Westphalian autonomy; they make no claim to authoritative decision making" (p. 119 / PDF p. 129). Ch. 2's burden is that the Oversight Board, the app-store tribunals and the terms-of-service canon *are* such claims over the subjects of territorial states. Stated so, the correction of Friedrichs becomes falsifiable: markets erode interdependence sovereignty (control of flows); churches breach Westphalian sovereignty (external authority inside the polity). What the consolidating state reasserts is then nameable too, and it is not Westphalian purity but domestic sovereignty in the control sense — "the ability of public authorities to exercise effective control within the borders of their own polity" (p. 4 / PDF p. 14) — plus the fiscal monopoly of decisive force, for which Krasner's grid has no cell and which is the book's contribution.

**(2) "Invitation" — the name for the ornamental route (spine §8(g)(3)).** Krasner's modalities of compromise are conventions, contracts, coercion and imposition (pp. 25–28); the first two are invitations: rulers "can extend invitations, sometimes inadvertent, that result in compromises of their own Westphalian sovereignty" (p. 20 / PDF p. 30), among other motives for "tying the hands of their successors" (p. 22 / PDF p. 32). The regularity that matters: "more powerful states are unlikely to lose their Westphalian sovereignty unless they invite external authority through conventions or contractual arrangements. Only weaker states have been the targets of intervention" (p. 29 / PDF p. 39). Spine §8(g) names the present indicator of the ornamental route as "absorption by alliance rather than by revocation". That is invitation in Krasner's exact sense: a strong state contracting an external power into its authority structure because its rulers are better off. Ch. 12 §IV and Appendix C's American sub-wager can score the form question as revocation (coercion: licences, off-switch, structural separation) against contract (the barons invited in), and the vocabulary sits in a canonical T2 text.

**(3) The Ottoman Public Debt Administration — an external actor running a fisc, worked.** "In 1881 the Ottoman Empire established the Council of the Public Debt controlled by foreign bondholders, which collected revenues and even engaged in development projects. By 1910 it had more employees than the Ministry of Finance" (p. 34 / PDF p. 44; p. 136 gives 1912); it "controlled more than a quarter of the empire's revenue" (p. 137 / PDF p. 147); in Egypt the Debt Commission took "direct control of 60 percent of Egypt's revenue" (p. 138 / PDF p. 148). Ch. 5's Ottoman comparator and ch. 12 §II's interstices both reach for a recognised sovereign whose fisc is run from outside; this is it, at T2.

**(4) His example of lock-in is the book's decisive layer.** On why international institutions never embed: "Diplomacy, contracting, coercion, and war are not like making computer chips—it does not get cheaper the more you fight and talk" (p. 68 / PDF p. 78; the chip example at p. 61). Spine §8(a)'s ratchet claims that decisive force *did* acquire increasing returns in the 1440s and does again now; Krasner grants that "Well-established domestic political institutions can be embedded … because they can be set in a hierarchical structure of authority" (p. 238 / PDF p. 248). The mechanism is a domestic embedding argument, and he has conceded the category.

## 5. What it CONTRADICTS

**At full strength.** The book's arc is a two-state model: a plural order "locked" (ch. 1 §VI) into exclusive territorial sovereignties by 1700, now returning to plurality; the ruling that the order was "locked" not "ended" keeps the lock as a real condition from which the pendulum swings back. Krasner denies the second state ever existed as practice. "Violations of Westphalian sovereignty have been almost routine in international politics even though observers have been blinded to their frequency by the assumption that the Westphalian model has been operative" (p. 28 / PDF p. 38). "The right, or ability, of the state apparatus to exercise full authority within its own territorial borders has never been consistently established in practice" (p. 51 / PDF p. 61). "Hence, perhaps half of the countries of Europe have never enjoyed Westphalian sovereignty for a single moment of their existence as international legal sovereigns" (p. 29 / PDF p. 39). If sovereignty was hypocritical from the start, what the neomedievalists "diagnosed" is a change in what analysts notice, not in the world — Krasner's verdict on the globalization literature: "these challenges are not new. Rulers have always operated in a transnational environment" (p. 223 / PDF p. 233). Worse for the pendulum, he extends the hypocrisy back through the book's own period: "Rulers operating in the European feudal system, the Chinese tributary state system, the Islamic system, or the Greek city-state system would like those in the sovereign state system, expound principles that they would also violate" (pp. 227–28 / PDF pp. 237–38). On this reading there was no lock to spring and nothing for a pendulum to leave. This reaches ch. 1 §VI's ruling, spine §3 (the pendulum, hold-on-balance) and CLAUDE.md §1 claim 1 (dispersal as transitional), and it touches spine §7's Bull test — replace versus reproduce — because Krasner holds the test malformed: "The international system is not like the game of chess. It does not have constitutive rules" (p. 229 / PDF p. 239), and the EU "might simply evolve into a conventional federal state. It might become embedded as a distinctly new institutional form" (p. 236 / PDF p. 246). It does not reach the DEFEND-tier mechanism directly, for the reason the answer makes plain.

**The book's best answer.** Krasner's own grid dissolves the objection, because the sovereignty he shows to be hypocritical and the sovereignty the book says was locked occupy different cells. His violated Westphalian sovereignty is the exclusion of *external* actors — other rulers, lenders, courts, IFIs — from a polity's authority structures. The book's lock is the subordination of *internal* intermediate authorities — lords, cities, orders, the Church's writ inside the realm — and the crown's monopoly of decisive force: domestic sovereignty in Krasner's control sense, which he says "is not necessarily related to international legal or Westphalian sovereignty" (p. 12). He never claims the internal consolidation was hypocritical; he brackets it — "I offer no explanation for the displacement of other institutional forms, such as the Holy Roman Empire, the Chinese tributary system, or the Hanseatic League by an international system in which states are the most prevalent organizational unit" (p. 5 / PDF p. 15) — and then concedes it as fact with a functional cause: "The evolution of states as a dominant institutional form … was not a random event but reflected functional advantages enjoyed by states over other structures such as feudalism, the Catholic Church, or city-states" (p. 68 / PDF p. 78, citing Tilly and Spruyt). His regularity that strong states lose autonomy only by invitation (p. 29) means the consolidators were in practice what the book says they became. And his globalization scepticism is dated: written before the platforms, it treats transnational actors as flows or as advocates without authority claims (p. 119); ch. 2's point is that the new universalisms claim authority, the one thing his 1999 evidence lacks. Honestly assessed: the answer is good against the mechanism and the "locked" ruling, provided the lock is restated as internal subordination and fiscal monopoly, never as exclusivity; it is weak wherever the manuscript says "exclusive", because there Krasner is simply right; and it leaves a residue to carry rather than answer — the consolidated state was, from 1648 onward, routinely penetrated by invitation, so Part III's absorption of the stack may itself run by invitation: spine §8(g)'s ornamental route, reached from the other side. The pendulum survives as a claim about who holds decisive force inside the polity; it does not survive as a claim about the purity of borders, and it should stop making one.

## 7. Verdict

Krasner changes the book's vocabulary and one adjective, not its direction: the mechanism, the "locked" ruling and the pendulum all survive him once the lock is restated as internal subordination and the fiscal monopoly of decisive force rather than as exclusive sovereignty, which he shows never existed in practice and which the manuscript claims in three places. The single most important thing: his four sovereignties give the book a canonical grid in which the platforms' breach (Westphalian, by authority claim), the state's reassertion (domestic, by control) and the form the settlement may take (invitation rather than intervention) each have a name — and "invitation", in his exact sense of strong states contracting away their own autonomy because their rulers are better off, is the term the ornamental-democracy ruling was reaching for.
