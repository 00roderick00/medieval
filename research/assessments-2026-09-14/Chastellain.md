## 1. The book in two paragraphs

Tome III is the Burgundian court chronicler's narrative of the years in which Philip the Good was at once the richest prince in Latin Christendom and a vassal under judicial pressure from Charles VII: the crusade vows and their financing, the Utrecht war, the dauphin Louis's flight into Burgundy in 1456 and the king's reaction, the Ghent reconciliation of 1458, and, as the volume's climax, the trial of Jean II duke of Alençon before the peers. Chastellain writes as a moralist of princes and a partisan of his master: of the king's summons to Montargis he says it "lui sembloit estre une manière d'argu et de despit, fait plus par malice que par nécessité" — seemed to him a kind of provocation and spite, done more from malice than necessity [TRANS. CLAUDE] (p. 417 / vol15 PDF 427); and his reading of the Alençon prosecution is that the king, "en faisant courre sentence sur le duc d'Alençon qui estoit son compère et son sang, pensoit et tendoit à donner fréeur au duc de Bourgongne lequel il maintenoit à son rebelle" — in letting sentence run on the duke of Alençon, his gossip and his blood, thought and aimed to put fear into the duke of Burgundy, whom he held to be his rebel [TRANS. CLAUDE] (p. 422 / PDF 425).

Its value to this project is not the chronicler's judgement but what he transcribes: the royal arrêt against Alençon "en la propre et mesme forme comme il la pronunca" — in the very form in which [the chancellor] pronounced it [TRANS. CLAUDE] (p. 479 / PDF 482) — a T1 crown document inside a T1 chronicle, with Kervyn's apparatus as a T2 retrieval ladder beneath it; and, in a single chapter, the century's own word for a state's dependence on an intermediary it has just punished.

## 2. What it CONFIRMS — the three claims verified at the page, and the citation audit

**(1) The Alençon scene — the review's paraphrase is ACCURATE in substance, with one precision.** The arrest: "fut pris dedens la ville de Paris, Jehan, duc d'Alençon et conte du Perche, par commandement du roy … car avoit conspiré avecques les Anglès, ce disoit-on, et tenu paroles de leur baillier aucunes de ses places en Normandie" — was taken in Paris, John duke of Alençon and count of Perche, by the king's command … for he had conspired with the English, it was said, and held talks to hand them some of his places in Normandy [TRANS. CLAUDE] (p. 100 / PDF 108; 1456). The sentence, pronounced at Vendôme on 10 October 1458 (Kervyn, p. 488 n.), declares him "crimineux de crisme de lèse-majesté" and strips him of "l'honneur et dignité de parrie de France" (p. 481 / PDF 484), condemns him "à recepvoir mort et estre exécuté par justice" and declares "tous et chascuns les biens dudit d'Alençon estre confisqués" (p. 482 / PDF 485). Then the king's pleasure: "que au regard de la personne de messire Jehan d'Alençon il plaist au roy que l'exécution d'icelle soit différée, et la diffère le roy jusques à son bon plaisir" — as regards the person of Sir John of Alençon, it pleases the king that the execution be deferred, and the king defers it until his good pleasure [TRANS. CLAUDE] (p. 484 / PDF 487). The children, who by right "dussent estre privés et déboutés de tous biens", are spared "en ramembrance des services des prédécesseurs dudit d'Alençon … à la chose publique du royaume" and at the petition of the duke of Brittany, the prisoner's uncle (pp. 485–486 / PDF 488–489). The operative clause: "le roi de sa grâce, en modérant la confiscation et forfaiture dessus déclarées, veut … que en tant qu'il touche les biens meubles qui furent audit d'Alençon, ils soient et demeurent à la femme et enfans d'icelui, **réservé au roy l'artillerie, harnois et autres habillemens de guerre**. Et au regard des terres, seigneuries et biens immeubles, le roy, en modérant comme dessus, retient à lui les villes, chastel, chastellenie et viconté d'Alençon, les ville, chastel, chastellenie de Donfront et aussi ceux de Verneuil … lesquels dès à présent le roy unist et adjoint et incorpore au patrimoine et au domaine de son royaume, et avecques ce le roy a retenu et retient à lui le surplus des chasteaux et chastellenies, terres, vicontés et seigneuries, rentes, revenues … qui furent du ducié d'Alençon … et tous autres droits et seigneuries qui font partie de la couronne et appanage de France où qu'ils soyent, réservé la conté du Perche" — the king of his grace, moderating the confiscation and forfeiture declared above, wills that as regards the movable goods that were the said Alençon's, they remain to his wife and children, **reserving to the king the artillery, harness and other equipment of war**; and as regards lands, the king retains the towns, castles, castellanies and vicomté of Alençon, Domfront and Verneuil, which from this moment he unites and incorporates into the patrimony and domain of the realm, and retains the whole residue of the duchy of Alençon and all rights that form part of the crown and appanage of France wherever they lie, save the county of Perche [TRANS. CLAUDE] (p. 486 / PDF 489). The family keeps Perche "pour en joyr à un seul fils … sans toutevoies aucune dignité ou prérogative de parrie" and the remaining non-appanage lands "soubs la main du roy, jusques à ce que eux et chacun de eux soient en eage" (p. 487 / PDF 490).

So: artillery retained — yes, in those words; fortifications retained and named — yes (Alençon, Domfront, Verneuil, incorporated into the domain); other family interests preserved — yes (movables, Perche, the patrimonial lands in wardship); execution postponed — yes, at pleasure. **The precision:** "certain fortifications" understates. The line the arrêt draws is between *appanage* and *patrimony*: the crown resumed the whole duchy of Alençon as a crown grant and left the family what was its own — with one exception, and that exception is the artillery, which was the family's own movable property and was taken anyway. That is the sentence ch11 §VII needs: of everything that was his, the one thing the crown would not leave a condemned peer's heirs was his guns. Kervyn's transcript of the *procès* shows why: Alençon had promised the English "qu'il leur aideroit de plasches, d'artillerie et de tout son povoir, et qu'il avoit assés d'artillerie pour combattre" — that he would aid them with places, artillery and all his power, and that he had artillery enough to fight (p. 480 n. / PDF 483; cf. p. 483 n., "de ses plaches, de son artillerie"). A magnate in 1456 still owned a train worth offering to an invader; the crown's first reservation in 1458 was of it. The chancellor's reply to Burgundy's plea for mercy states the doctrine: "par justice règnent les roys … [sans elle] les royaumes et seigneuries ne seroient que laronnières" — kingdoms and lordships would be nothing but dens of thieves [TRANS. CLAUDE] (p. 475 / PDF 478).

**(2) The Lombard lenders — the review's paraphrase HOLDS, and "public necessity" is Chastellain's phrase, not the reviewer's gloss.** Chapter LXI, "Comment le duc mist sa main sur les bancs des usuriers" (pp. 314–316 / PDF 321–323; Bruges, "sur la fin du mois de may", in the chronicle's 1457 sequence). The three Piedmontese tables fled by night with their depositors' money, dragged down by a Louvain banker who "par avoir trop embrassié de marchandises en Angleterre" had entangled them "d'une commune bourse" (p. 315). The duke inventoried the houses to restore what was owed, found that rich burgesses "avoient mis argent à usure avecques eux, ce que point ne devoient, par quoy le prince confisqua tout à luy, et en furent encore à grand amende" (p. 315), and had the ringleader taken out of Valenciennes' franchise because his case "trop touchoit au grief de la chose publique" (pp. 315–316). Then: "tous les bancs des usuriers des pays du duc rompirent, et y mist le duc sa main sus comme forfais et les composa, car tous contribuoient ensemble avecques les dessusdits en la faute commise. Par quoy tous les pays et les bonnes villes furent longuement sans avoir nuls Lombars pour prester, là où le peuple toutevoies, selon la nature de luy ancienne, se peut mal passer de eux. **Et par ainsi, le duc enfin estoit constraint de les y remettre par la nécessité publique**, et retournèrent partout comme devant" — all the usurers' banks in the duke's lands broke, and the duke laid his hand on them as forfeit and put them to composition, for all contributed together in the fault; whereby all the lands and good towns were long without any Lombards to lend, where the people nevertheless, by its ancient nature, can ill do without them. And so the duke in the end was constrained to restore them by public necessity, and they returned everywhere as before [TRANS. CLAUDE] (p. 316 / PDF 323). Two precisions for decision 3: the punishment was for a failure and a flight, not for lending as such, and it was fiscal in form (confiscation, *amende*, composition) — the state profited from the seizure before dependence forced the restoration; and the indispensable function was retail credit to "le peuple", not operational knowledge. The structure the review wants — coercive superiority without a substitute for the function — is exactly there, in the century's own word, *constraint*.

**(3) The royal-death predictions — FOUND, in tome III, but MISATTRIBUTED by the review.** Chapter LXXXVIII, "Comment le roy fut aussy durement malade" (pp. 444–450 / PDF 447–453). In the winter of 1457–58 Charles VII's leg was thought mortal: "couroit la voix par diverses terres que dedens terme préfix et par jugement d'astronomie il devoit mourir et ne passeroit point le mois de mars" — the word ran through divers lands that within a fixed term, by astrological judgement, he must die and would not pass the month of March [TRANS. CLAUDE] (p. 444). The dauphin was urged to approach the frontier to be crowned the sooner; the duke was thought to share the hope. "En quoy et dauphin et duc de Bourgongne furent trompés tous deux, car vesqui le roy encore longs jours depuis en haut règne … tous deux furent moqués en leur espoir et payés de samblable folie l'un comme l'autre" — in which both were deceived, for the king lived long days after in high reign; both were mocked in their hope and paid in the same folly [TRANS. CLAUDE] (p. 446). The astrologers "mirent terme préfix dedens lequel les influences de là sus démonstroient sa fin" (p. 447); Chastellain's verdict is on "cestes manières de gens qui présument des secrets de Dieu qui desvoient les princes et les deçoivent" (p. 449), and "Or n'estoit point mort le roy alors quant son fils y avoit l'espoir, et pareillement le duc ne mourut point aussi à ceste fois quant François le désiroient" (pp. 449–450). These are not Chastellain's predictions; they are the astrologers' and the princes', reported and mocked. **But the review's underlying point is in the volume, and better than stated**, because the chronicler's own forecasting is on the page too. Of the comet of 1456 he writes: "y juge-on guerre, tribulation et mort de hauts princes, lesquelles choses, posé que elles ne soient approuvées, ne tenues à nécessaires, sont toutevoies apparues droit-cy assez depuis et trouvées advenues … en mort de plusieurs hauts et grans princes, dont il sera fait mention en lieu dû" — one judges from them war, tribulation and the death of high princes, which things, granted they are not proven nor held necessary, have nevertheless appeared since and been found to come true … in the death of several high princes, of which mention will be made in due place [TRANS. CLAUDE] (p. 117 / PDF 125); and he cashes the forecast on Ladislaus of Hungary and John of Coimbra: "Sy ne fut mie sans mistère que la très-triste et sombre commette s'estoit monstrée en la saison devant" (p. 387 / vol15 PDF 397). Kervyn notes flatly, "Chastellain croyait aux influences des astres" (p. 165 n. / PDF 173). The same writer who scolds the dauphin's astrologers for a fixed term that failed reads a comet as fulfilled by whichever princes happened to die — the forecast surviving by changing its referent. Kervyn's apparatus adds Jean de Wesel's 1454 horoscope for the dauphin ("erit mutatio regum", pp. 446–447 n.; T2). The 1461 death belongs to later tomes not in corpus and is not needed.

## 3. What it CORRECTS

- **The review's attribution (decision 8).** "Chastellain's failed predictions of royal deaths" should read: the astrologers' and the princes' failed prediction of Charles VII's death in 1458, reported and moralised by Chastellain (pp. 444–450), set against Chastellain's own comet-prognostic (pp. 117, 387). Decision 8's passage can be written, but only with that attribution.
- **The review's "certain fortifications" (decision 1).** The crown retained the whole appanage duchy, three castellanies by name incorporated into the domain, Saint-Blanchay in Touraine and the Tours tolls (pp. 486–487), not "certain fortifications".
- **The manuscript's Burgundy timeline (ch10 §VI).** The draft says "Burgundy instituted a dedicated *recette de l'artillerie* in 1458" (manuscript line 6218). Nothing here contradicts it, but Chastellain places it inside a general 1457 reform after chancellor Rolin's fall — the new men "créèrent six souverains gouverneurs sur le fait des finances … mirent sus réformateurs sur tous les pays et sur toutes gens officiers … là où de tous lez on tiroit argent" (p. 335 / PDF 342) — which the draft should carry as the context of the office it cites.
- Nothing else. No date, name or sequence in the manuscript's Burgundian or Praguerie passages (ch07 draft: Alençon "retired to their lordships unpunished" in 1440) is contradicted; the 1440 impunity and the 1458 sentence are the two ends of the arc, and tome III supplies the second at T1.

## 4. What it ADDS

- **ch11 §VII (the barons lose and keep).** The arrêt clause, by name and in the crown's words. Ch07's Praguerie settlement (Alençon "unpunished", 1440) and the 1458 arrêt bracket the eighteen years in which the instrument changed from calibrated pardon to the lit de justice — and the thing reserved first is the artillery. Add Chastellain's gloss (p. 422, "donner fréeur au duc de Bourgongne"): the sentence on the lesser peer addressed to the greater.
- **ch10 §VIII and ch12 §IV (command, dependence, capture).** "Constraint de les y remettre par la nécessité publique" (p. 316) is the T1 anchor for the dependence term the draft already carries at manuscript lines 6677–6690, two centuries before the Stationers and the Arsenal.
- **ch10 §VI (Burgundy counter-case — the fisc not yet permanent).** Three T1 passages the draft lacks. (a) The Franche-Comté aide of 1454: the nobles "sont si francs que leur prince ne peut tailler leurs subgès sinon de leur consentement, et encore quant ils consentiront … sy en tourne le tiers ou une grande portion devers eux, qui redonde en leur bourse" — so free that their prince cannot tax their subjects save by their consent, and when they consent a third or a large portion turns back into their own purse [TRANS. CLAUDE] (p. 13 / PDF 21); the grant conditional on the crusade sailing, the sum blank in the manuscript; Kervyn's note gives the Dijon estates' 60,000 livres from Plancher IV, 286 (p. 10 n.; T2). (b) The 1457 reform, above. (c) The 1458 mobilisation: the duke asked the estates of Brabant for "point d'argent, ni finance, mais service corporel … de six sepmaines à leurs despens … les bonnes villes … avecques leur artillerie, et les gens d'esglise … en contribution de deniers pour payement des saudées, car touchoit la chose à nécessité publique" — not money but bodily service, six weeks at their own cost, the towns with their own artillery, the clergy paying the wages [TRANS. CLAUDE] (p. 451 / PDF 454); the nobles of his party "soustinrent de grans frais à ceste cause" (p. 424 / PDF 427). In 1458 the richest prince in the north defended himself by service and communal guns, not a taille-paid standing force: the draft's two-variable finding at T1, fourteen years before the ordinances it cites.
- **ch12 §IV (the core polity and the contracted periphery).** The Arras terms as Chastellain states them: in the Somme towns the duke had all services and jurisdictions "tels comme au roy appartenoient, réservé seulement la souveraineté" — save only sovereignty — and the king nonetheless cried the arrière-ban "mesme … ès villes engagiées sur Somme" (p. 423 / PDF 426); the duke's person, exempt under Arras from personal summons, was summoned "comme doien des pairs" regardless (p. 417 / vol15 PDF 427). Reserved sovereignty exercised as levy and summons inside a pledged periphery.
- **ch11 (absorption by presence).** The Dauphiné, 1456: the king at Lyon "pour mettre en son obéissance tout le pays du Dauphiné", the governor surrendering "considérant ledit pays non pouvoir résister à l'encontre de sa puissance" (pp. 225–226 / PDF 233–234).
- **Introduction or ch12 §VII (decision 8).** The prophecy passage with the corrected attribution, and Chastellain's own comet hedge — "posé que elles ne soient approuvées, ne tenues à nécessaires" (p. 117) — as the specimen of the softening CLAUDE.md §2 forbids.
- **Colour, footnote level.** Ghent 1458 met its prince in black, keys in hand, under the legend "Veni nobis, pacificus dominus; utere servitio nostro" (pp. 412–413 / PDF 417–418); the duke's nightly gun trials at IJsselstein, 1456 (p. 129 / PDF 137); the Ottoman train abandoned at Belgrade, "onze grosses bombardes … et encore bien deux cens autres" (p. 113 / PDF 121, Hunyadi's letter as relayed).
- **Retrieval ladder from Kervyn's notes:** Jean Chartier t. III pp. 80, 91; Duclercq III, 37; Escouchy chs. CXIX–CXXI; BN mss. 1278, 4930, 5738 (the *procès*); Plancher IV, 286; Kervyn's later tomes for Louis XI and for the 1467 spider ballade.

## 5. What it CONTRADICTS

At full strength:

1. **The Alençon settlement was judicial, negotiated and reversible, not an artillery-state act.** The instrument is the lit de justice, the charge lèse-majesté, the "modération" is obtained by Brittany's petition (p. 486), and Chastellain's frame is pity — "un si haut et si noble prince, de la vraye estraction des fleurs de lis" (p. 488 / PDF 491) — and the king's malice. Execution was deferred at pleasure; Chastellain says the duke "de cinc ans après … ne vuida de prison" (p. 100), which implies release under the next reign [the release itself is outside the corpus tomes — UNVERIFIED here]. A consolidation that a succession can undo is weaker than the itemised "transfers" of decision 1 suggest.
2. **The Lombards show extraction as motive, not only dependence as constraint.** The duke confiscated depositors' money, fined them, and put every bank in his lands to composition before necessity forced restoration (pp. 315–316). Any use of the chapter for decision 3 must carry the fiscal appetite with the dependence, or it flatters the state.
3. **The prophecy passage cuts against the forecaster who uses it.** The chronicler mocks a fixed-term forecast that failed and keeps his own comet-forecast alive by hedging its necessity and cashing it on whatever deaths followed. If the Introduction cites him as the cautionary case, it invites the same charge against Appendix C's indicators the moment one is re-dated.

**The book's best answer.** To (1): the arrêt's reservations are the mechanism's content whatever the frame — the crown named guns, walls and appanage, and the chronicler's pity does not alter what the document keeps; the reversibility is real and is the disanalogy to state (CLAUDE.md §6): a Tudor-style settlement is one a succession does *not* undo, and 1458 was not yet that. Good, if made honestly. To (2): extraction as a third motive strengthens §8(g)(4) — stack rents, not bargained taxation, finance the consolidating state. Good. To (3): procedural, not rhetorical — Appendix C's indicators are dated and directional and are not re-dated after the fact; say so in the sentence that cites him. Adequate. None reaches a spine §8 ruling or a DEFEND-tier claim; (1) disciplines decision 1 and ch11 §VII, (3) decision 8.

## 7. Verdict

Tome III changes the weight, not the direction: it converts two of the review's three "new scenes" from unverified paraphrase into T1 at the page — the Alençon arrêt more exactly than the review stated it, the Lombard restoration in the century's own phrase — and corrects the third's attribution, while verifying none of the manuscript's one existing Chastellain citation (the 1467 spider), which needs another tome. The single most important thing to know is the arrêt's own order of reservation: of a condemned peer's movables the crown left his widow and children everything "réservé au roy l'artillerie, harnois et autres habillemens de guerre" — the guns were the one private thing the state would not leave in private hands, and that sentence, in the crown's words as a contemporary transcribed them, is what ch11 §VII has been waiting for. Retrieve vol15 into use as the working copy; vol01 lacks five pages and should be so marked in the ledger.
