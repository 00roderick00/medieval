## 1. The book in two paragraphs

Schmitt's argument is that every legal order rests on a prior act of spatial ordering — appropriation, division, cultivation — and that international law is the history of such acts at planetary scale. "All subsequent regulations of a written or unwritten kind derive their power from the inner measure of an original, constitutive act of spatial ordering. This original act is nomos" (printed p. 78 / PDF p. 80). Land-appropriation "is the archetype of a constitutive legal process externally (vis-à-vis other peoples) and internally (for the ordering of land and property within a country)" (printed p. 47 / PDF p. 49). On this ground he reconstructs three orders: the medieval *respublica Christiana*, the interstate *jus publicum Europaeum* born of the land-appropriation of the New World, and the disordered universalism that followed its dissolution after 1890.

The achievement he defends is the second order's law of war: "In the epoch of interstate international law, which lasted from the 16th to the end of the 19th century, there was real progress, namely a limiting and bracketing of European wars... It arose solely from the emergence of a new spatial order — a balance of territorial states on the European continent in relation to the maritime British Empire and against the background of vast free spaces" (printed p. 140 / PDF p. 142). The book is written from inside the wreck: "The traditional Eurocentric order of international law is foundering today, as is the old nomos of the earth" (Foreword, printed p. 39 / PDF p. 41), and it ends by posing the question of the successor order — the passage pinned in §4 below, which is this book's own fork stated in 1950.

## 2. What it CONFIRMS

**The plural order was law-dense, not chaotic (Chapter 1 §II).** The manuscript: "the plurality was not chaos. It was law-dense... What the order lacked was not law but *hierarchy among laws*." Schmitt, at T2: "the European medieval order certainly was very anarchistic in terms of a smoothly functioning modern factory, but it was not nihilistic, despite all the wars and feuds, as long as it retained the fundamental unity of order and orientation" (printed p. 57 / PDF p. 59). His anarchy/nihilism distinction is the canonical legal-theory statement of ch01's claim.

**The state as episode, made under pressure out of something else (Chapter 1 §VI).** The manuscript: "the exclusive territorial state was *made*, under pressure, out of something else." Schmitt: "Only a completely different spatial order ended medieval international law in Europe. It arose with the centralized, spatially self-contained, continental European state that faced emperor and pope, as well as other, similarly organized neighboring states" (printed p. 66 / PDF p. 68). And the whole architecture of his book — three successive nomoi, the interstate order an "epoch" with a beginning and an end — carries the episode framing at canonical weight.

**Two universalisms above the lattice (Chapters 1–2).** The manuscript's Empire-and-Church duality, taken from Friedrichs, has its legal-order statement here: the medieval unity "rested on the distinction between potestas [power] and auctoritas [authority] as two distinct lines of order of the same encompassing unity" (printed p. 61 / PDF p. 63); wars among Christian princes "were bracketed wars... They were feuds in the sense of assertions of right... within the framework of one and the same total order" (printed p. 58 / PDF p. 60); and the order survived the emperor's impotence "as long as the auctoritas of the pope sufficed to issue mandates for missions and crusades" (printed p. 66 / PDF p. 68) — authority real, nowhere exclusive, which is ch01's substrate stated by the century's most influential jurist of spatial order.

**The hemispherical reading (Chapter 12 §IV).** The original behind Bratton's gloss (Bratton scan pp. 31–35 reads the Monroe Doctrine through Schmitt): "Ever since, the Monroe Doctrine and the Western Hemisphere have been linked together. They define the sphere of the special interests of the United States. They encompass a space far exceeding the boundaries of the state proper — a Großraum in the sense of international law" (printed p. 281 / PDF p. 285). The Panama Declaration of 1939 "extended Großraum thinking over the free sea" (printed p. 283 / PDF p. 287). Chapter 12 can now cite Schmitt directly rather than through Bratton.

## 3. What it CORRECTS

Nothing factual: no date, figure, name or sequence in the manuscript is wrong by this book's account. One interpretive pressure is worth recording. The manuscript (Chapter 1 §III, following Friedrichs) renders Empire and Church as "competing universalistic claims"; Schmitt insists the competition sat inside one order — the antitheses of emperor and pope "were not absolute, but rather diversi ordines... of the same encompassing unity" (printed p. 61 / PDF p. 63), and he dates the real division to the thirteenth-century *societas perfecta* doctrine ("Misfortune did not arise until the 13th century," same page). This does not correct Friedrichs — competition-within-unity is still competition, and Friedrichs's "held together by" already concedes the unity — but ch02's line that the two universalisms were "neither able to absorb the other" could carry a footnote acknowledging Schmitt's stronger reading: they were not trying to absorb each other, because both were orders of the same *respublica*.

## 4. What it ADDS

**(a) The 1950 statement of the book's own fork — the single most valuable pin.** Part V ch. 3 poses the successor question and answers with three possibilities: victory of one side in the Cold War antithesis — "The victor would be the world's sole sovereign. He would appropriate the whole earth — land, sea, and air — and would divide and manage it in accord with his plans and ideas"; second, continuation of the hegemonic balance, with America as "the greater island that could administer and guarantee the balance of the rest of the world"; and third: "A combination of several independent Großräume or blocs could constitute a balance, and thereby could precipitate a new order of the earth... The third possibility, an equilibrium of several independent Großräume, is rational, if the Großräume are differentiated meaningfully and are homogeneous internally" (printed pp. 354–55 / PDF pp. 358–59). The manuscript's parallel-settlements prediction is Schmitt's third possibility, chosen; Bratton's 2026 "Hemispherical Stacks" is its platform-side restatement; and Schmitt's complaint that "Most of those considering this frightful problem rush blindly toward a single sovereign of the world" (printed p. 355 / PDF p. 359) is a 1950 rebuke of the end-of-history residue the book positions against. This belongs in ch12 §IV or the coda, by name, with the disanalogy of §6 below attached.

**(b) Sovereignty emptied of content — the legal form of the manuscript's settlement, stated from the other side.** On the US intervention treaties (Cuba 1903, Panama 1903): the modern form of control's "first characteristic is renunciation of open territorial annexation of the controlled state... the controlled state's territory is absorbed into the spatial sphere of the controlling state... The external, emptied space of the controlled state's territorial sovereignty remains inviolate, but the material content of this sovereignty is changed by the guarantees of the controlling power's economic Großraum" (printed p. 252 / PDF p. 256). Title left, discretion severed — the American method Chapter 12 documents for firms, described by Schmitt in 1950 for client states. Strong candidate for ch12 §IV or ch11's operative-but-never-invested thread, one sentence with pin. His epigram for the fiscal register: the old maxim cujus regio, ejus religio had become "cujus regio, ejus economia" and, in the American case, "cujus economia, ejus regio" (printed pp. 256, 258 / PDF pp. 260, 262) — whose the economy, theirs the territory: the platform question in seven words of law-Latin.

**(c) The EU assessment's precedent.** "Whereas the Monroe Doctrine forbade any League influence in American affairs, the League's role in European affairs, for example with respect to plans for a European Union (1929–30)... was co-determined by these American member states" and "In 1930, when this shadow fell on Europe, it was sufficient to transform all plans for a European Union into empty discourse" (printed pp. 255, 258 / PDF pp. 259, 262). Europe-as-province-of-the-stronger-hemisphere — the manuscript's own conditional for the EU ("completes its century as the American bloc's canonical province") has a 1930 dress rehearsal, quotable in ch12 §IV.

**(d) Protection and obedience — a frame for the drone objection and the Russia case.** Air war, for Schmitt, is decisive destruction that founds nothing: with air bombardment "Not even the shadow of the relation between protection and obedience remains" (printed p. 320 / PDF p. 324); occupation, by contrast, is land war's "natural goal" and generates legal order (printed p. 317 / PDF p. 321). The manuscript's claim that destruction is not decisiveness — Russia's evidentiary role, the drones-as-longbows argument — gains a T2 theoretical statement: force that cannot convert into protection-obedience cannot consolidate. The stack the book prices *includes* the legibility layer, i.e. occupation by administration; that is why it is cannon and not merely bombardment.

**(e) The katechon (colour, one clause at most).** The empire as "the historical power to restrain the appearance of the Antichrist and the end of the present eon" (printed pp. 59–60 / PDF pp. 61–62) — the medieval universalism self-understood as holding back dissolution. Usable in ch02 only if Roderick wants it; it is the most-quoted and most-abused concept in the book's reception.

## 5. What it CONTRADICTS

**The rival cause, at full strength.** Schmitt's account of where the state and its bracketed war came from has no cannon, no fisc, no artillery in it — the word artillery occurs in his air-war analogies, not his state-formation account. The driver is spatial-legal: "It arose solely from the emergence of a new spatial order — a balance of territorial states on the European continent in relation to the maritime British Empire and against the background of vast free spaces" (printed p. 140 / PDF p. 142; emphasis on "solely" mine), plus detheologisation: "The first effective rationalization of the spatial form 'state'... was achieved by the detheologization of public life and the neutralization of the antitheses of creedal civil wars" (printed p. 140 / PDF p. 142). On his account the constitutive event of the modern order is the land-appropriation of the New World and the religious wars' overcoming — not the capital-intensification of decisive force. If that is right, the book's mechanism is at best a subordinate cause of the consolidation it claims to explain, and the DEFEND-tier claim (spine §6: cannon → fiscal-administrative discipline → consolidation) has a canonical rival standing against it.

**The book's best answer.** Three moves, and they are honest ones. First, Schmitt is answering a different question: his subject is the *external* legal form of the interstate order — its law of war, its soil statuses — not the internal contest that decided which unit survived. His own agents presuppose the book's mechanism: the states that could bracket war were those with "unified central governments and administrations, and well-defined borders" (printed p. 140 / PDF p. 142) — he never asks how they got them, which is precisely Part II's question. Second, his chronology helps rather than hurts: his bracketed-war epoch begins in the sixteenth century, *after* the 1440–1500 consolidation the book reconstructs; the artillery state is the precondition of his story, not a competitor to it. Third, "solely" overclaims even on his own terms — the New World's free spaces explain where European violence was exported, not why the exporters were centralised monarchies rather than leagues, cities or the Church. The answer is good, but only if the contradiction is stated: a chapter that cited Schmitt's geometry while suppressing his causal account would be rightly savaged. Ch10's critiques file should carry him as a named rival cause alongside Rogers and Parrott.

**A second, smaller cut.** Schmitt holds that modern means of destruction dissolve legal order rather than found it — with air war "All institutions and principles that formerly were possible for a law of war, i.e., for a bracketing of war, lose their meaning" (printed p. 319 / PDF p. 323). The manuscript claims the new decisive weapon compels consolidation. But the cut is inter-bloc, not intra-bloc: his own third possibility concedes that internally homogeneous Großräume can found order, and the manuscript predicts settlement inside blocs with durable disorder between them — which is Schmitt's picture, not its refutation.

## 6. Placement

**Chapter 12 §IV — T2, engaged BY NAME.** Two uses: (i) the Großraum passage (printed p. 281) as the original behind Bratton's Monroe-Doctrine gloss, one sentence; (ii) the three-possibilities fork (printed pp. 354–55) as the 1950 statement of the space the book's wager fills — Bratton declines to choose between Schmitt's first and third; this book chooses the third and supplies the mechanism Schmitt never had. The emptied-sovereignty and cujus-economia pins (printed pp. 252, 256–58) are footnote-grade in the same section. NO to ch06 and ch10 text; YES to ch10/critiques as the named rival cause per §5.

**Chapters 1–2 — footnote-grade supply.** The anarchy/nihilism distinction (printed p. 57), the state-as-episode close of the medieval chapter (printed p. 66), and the diversi-ordines precision (printed p. 61) support ch01 §§II/VI and ch02 §II without displacing Anderson, Friedrichs or the chapter spines.

**THE DISANALOGY THE BOOK MUST STATE (CLAUDE.md §6 form).** The bridge: the manuscript's four-bloc settlement resembles Schmitt's equilibrium of several independent Großräume. The stated disanalogy, twofold. *Provenance:* Großraum is not a neutral coinage. Schmitt built the doctrine inside the Third Reich — *Völkerrechtliche Großraumordnung mit Interventionsverbot für raumfremde Mächte* (1939), the "Großraum principle" read off the Monroe Doctrine for application "in other historical situations, and in different friend-enemy groupings" (Ulmen's introduction, printed pp. 23–25 / PDF pp. 25–27), with the German Reich as the intended ordering centre — and the postwar *Nomos* re-grounds the concept without confessing that lineage. The book cites the 1950 text with the 1939–41 provenance named, not laundered. *Substance:* Schmitt's Großraum is a spatial-legal ordering concept — a sphere defined by a political idea plus a prohibition on intervention by powers foreign to the space, with no mechanism for why blocs form where they do. The manuscript's blocs are fiscal-military consolidations, defined by the capacity to pay for and assemble the decisive stack, scored by falsifiable indicators with baselines (Appendix C); the mechanism, not the map, does the work. Answer to the project's question: the four-bloc prediction occupies the position of Schmitt's third possibility — a balance of several internally homogeneous blocs — but it is *not* a Großraum order in Schmitt's sense, because its units are constituted by fiscal-administrative capacity rather than by an ordering idea with an intervention ban, and because the book predicts the settlement and declines to advocate it. Geometry inherited; doctrine, provenance and politics not adopted. Stated once, in ch12 §IV or its endnote, this closes the reviewer's line before it opens.

## 7. Verdict

Schmitt changes the book's weight, not its direction: he supplies canonical T2 authority for the plural order, the state-as-episode, and — decisively — the original behind Bratton's hemispherical reading, at the price of one serious rival cause (the spatial-legal account of state formation, "solely," p. 140) that ch10's critiques must carry and answer. The single most important thing: the three-possibilities passage at printed pp. 354–55 is the book's own fork — world sovereign, hegemonic balance, or "an equilibrium of several independent Großräume... if the Großräume are differentiated meaningfully and are homogeneous internally" — stated in 1950, and the manuscript is, in effect, the mechanism-equipped argument for the third branch. Cite it by name, pin it, and attach the provenance disanalogy in §6 form, so no reviewer can say the book adopted Schmitt's politics with his geometry.
