# Assessment — David Stasavage, *States of Credit: Size, Power, and the Development of European Polities* (Princeton University Press, 2011)

*Corpus: corpus/retrieved/source-library/text-2026-09-14/Stasavage.txt — an EPUB conversion with "===== FILE: partNNNN.html =====" markers and NO printed pagination. Pins are to chapter, section heading and file marker; every quotation must be re-pinned to a printed page before it enters a chapter. Read in full. The text is clean (a publisher's file, not OCR), but tables other than 2.1–2.2, the figures and ch. 4's equations survive as captions only. The manuscript nowhere cites Stasavage; its credit vocabulary is confined to ch. 4 §VI (prestiti, Monte Vecchio), ch. 10 §VI and ch. 11 §III (juros). "Public debt", "Holland", "1688", "Genoa" and "Siena" do not occur.*

## 1. The book in two paragraphs

Stasavage asks why some European polities borrowed long-term early and cheaply and others could not: "I will argue that the presence of an intensive form of representation characterized by an assembly that could monitor and modify expenditures was critical in facilitating access to credit by European states. But the existence of an assembly that would function in this manner was itself dependent on two underlying conditions. First, in an era of high communications and travel costs this intensive form of political representation could be maintained only in polities of limited geographic scale" (ch. 1, 'Introduction', part0008_split_000). The second condition is who sat in it: "having a merchant oligarchy was the best recipe for obtaining access to credit" (same). On a 31-polity dataset, 1250–1750, a city-state without a debt had in any half-century "a 43% chance" of creating one, a territorial state "only a 5% chance", and city-states borrowed "two percentage points lower" (ch. 1, 'Broad Sample Evidence', part0008_split_005). Territorial assemblies could veto taxes; only city councils, meeting weekly or daily and stocked with bondholders, held a "control right" over spending — and once city-state status is controlled for, the effect of assemblies as such "disappears" (ch. 4, part0011_split_000).

His second argument is aimed at Tilly, Hintze, Bean and North: "large size had both costs and benefits when it came to war mobilization, and therefore the effect of changes in military technology on the distribution and organization of European states was more muted than is commonly believed… It was arguably their better access to credit that allowed many city-states to survive far longer than we would expect, given the conventional 'war and state formation' explanation" (ch. 1, 'Introduction', part0008_split_000). His Table 2.1 dates the first observed long-term loans: Arras 1241, Venice 1262, Siena 1290, Genoa 1340, Florence 1347, Cologne 1375 — against Castile 1489, France 1522, Holland 1522, Great Britain 1693 (ch. 2, 'When Did States First Borrow Long-Term?', part0009_split_002). "Prior to 1500, we cannot directly compare city-states and territorial states, because no territorial states had yet created long-term debts" (ch. 2, 'The Cost of Borrowing', part0009_split_003).

## 2. What it CONFIRMS

**(a) Ch. 4 §VI — the instruments arose "in a *republic*, out of commerce".** "During the thirteenth century Italian city-states converged on the use of forced loans as their primary financing instrument… the prestanza paid interest… an active secondary market developed" (ch. 2, 'When Did States First Borrow Long-Term?', part0009_split_002); the Venetian prestiti "traded at an average of 85% of par" through the early fourteenth century (ch. 2, n. 17, part0009_split_005). Caveat: his only source on Venetian institutions is Lane 1973 (Table 3.1), so on Venice the two works are one witness.

**(b) Ch. 4 §VI — "converting its propertied citizens into its creditors and thereby into stakeholders".** The manuscript's (c)-grade inference is his headline finding, with prosopography: in Dordrecht "34% of town magistrates and their family members were registered as public annuity purchasers" (ch. 3, 'Who Was Represented?', part0010_split_003). Upgradeable to consensus.

**(c) Ch. 4 §VI — the Hansa as "all network and no institution".** "a far-flung entity that, because of its geographic dispersion, faced difficulties in maintaining a central representative assembly… Philippe Dollinger (1971: 94) notes that the League's general assembly, the Hansetag, suffered from chronic absenteeism caused in part by 'the desire to avoid the cost of sending a representative'" (ch. 3, 'Origins of Representative Assemblies', part0010_split_001). A Dollinger pin at one remove; the [GAP: Dollinger] flags stay open.

**(d) Ch. 10 §III — non-consultation as the constitution.** "At the conclusion to the 1468 meeting, the assembled Estates actually stated that they could not meet as frequently as in the past" (ch. 7, 'National Institutions for Creditor Representation', part0014_split_004, on Blockmans 1998); and "the French crown spent 50,000 livres tournois indemnifying the deputies to the Estates General of 1484" (ch. 3, 'Origins', part0010_split_001, on Major 1955) — the assembly the crown had to pay to convene. He also reports Downing's thesis that post-1500 territorial states "established new bureaucratic mechanisms for raising revenue that bypassed existing assemblies" (same section); ch. 10 §III is its fifteenth-century instance.

**(e) Ch. 11 §III — Castile.** The one territorial state with a long-term debt before 1500 (ch. 2, n. 8); juros and asientos as "two pillars"; and the limit — "after the bankruptcy of 1607 when the Spanish Crown was forced to conclude a truce, which lasted twelve years (Ehrenberg 1928: 335)" (ch. 7, 'Castilian Public Credit', part0014_split_007). For ch. 12 §VII's "durable rent", his city-states end "as rentier republics" (ch. 1, 'Introduction') — a second T2 route to the rentier warning in ch. 12's memo (Revisions 10).

## 3. What it CORRECTS

**(i) Manuscript (ch. 4 §VI) says the Monte Vecchio of 1262 was "the first funded sovereign debt in European history".** Stasavage, on Epstein 1996b: "In 1259 a reform was implemented to consolidate Genoa's existing debt. Existing holders of debt were given shares in a new compera that paid a nominal rate of 8%. In addition, they also had the right to sell these shares, which created a secondary market" (ch. 6, 'Development of Public Credit in Genoa', part0013_split_007); Arras issued annuities from 1241. Lane's narrower claim survives ("the first European state to fund its debt so that interest was regularly paid from one fund to all bondholders equally"); write that, not "first funded sovereign debt".

**(ii) Manuscript (ch. 4 §VI) implies the Venetian form fathered modern public debt.** "it was actually the system of municipal debt developed in northern Europe that would set the model for long-term government borrowing in Europe until the end of the nineteenth century" (ch. 2, 'When Did States First Borrow Long-Term?', part0009_split_002) — the rente, not the prestito.

**(iii) Manuscript (ch. 4 §VI) says the instruments "emerge wherever scale, commerce and insecurity meet".** On his whole argument scale *prevented* them: "geographic scale often proved to be a fundamental obstacle" (ch. 1, 'Introduction'). Write "smallness of scale", or drop the word.

**(iv) Spine §8(a) says "No Estates General met again until 1484".** Stasavage records a meeting in 1468 (§2(d)). The manuscript's "first Estates General in decades" survives; the spine's sentence should be checked against Major 1960 before it is carried into text.

## 4. What it ADDS

**(1) The Estates General of 1576 — ch. 10 §III and ch. 11 §II.** A Third Estate deputy "placed a sign at the entrance to the meeting chamber that called for seizing the revenues dedicated to paying the rentes sur l'Hôtel de Ville", and the Estates, under Bodin, declared: "[i]f the affairs of the king are so desperate, he could make use of half of the rents constituted on both the cities and communities of the kingdom, he could tax the financiers who have lent to him, or he could sell off church lands" (ch. 7, 'National Institutions', part0014_split_004, citing Cauwès 1895: 456). A territorial assembly proposing default and the dissolution as the alternative to taxation — forty years after Henry VIII took the second option (ruling (k)).

**(2) The rentes sur l'Hôtel de Ville, 1522 — the scale limit as a case, for ch. 10 §VII and ch. 12 §IV.** The Paris bureau de ville was "a veritable creditors syndicate" (Schnapper, quoted ch. 7, 'Local Institutions', part0014_split_003); the system worked while rentes were backed by taxes "collected in or near Paris" and failed after 1551 when backed by revenues from "more distant destinations… even, in one case, Marseille" (ch. 7, 'Creation and Evolution', part0014_split_002). "The problem was not that France lacked the right institutions; it was that France was simply too big" (ch. 1, 'Case Study Evidence', part0008_split_007). The legibility limb from the creditor's side: credit reached as far as the lender could see.

**(3) Ornamental representation, defined at T2 — ch. 12 §IV/§VII, ruling §8(g).** The Cortes won consent, administration of the millones and audit powers, then "the individual Castilian cities gradually lost control over their representatives… As a consequence, the representatives failed to effectively use even those formal powers that they did have" (ch. 7, 'Control of Public Finances by the Cortes', part0014_split_008); after 1665 "the monarchy did away with the institution entirely" (ch. 7, 'Explaining the Demise', part0014_split_009). Generalised: "it may be possible to maintain the institution but without it serving its intended purpose… Compare, for example, the Cortes of Castile in the seventeenth century to the Ugandan Parliament in the twenty-first century" (ch. 8, 'Information, Commitment, and Democracy', part0015_split_002). That is §8(g)(2)'s "demonstrable separation", stated by a political scientist with a dataset; add him to the Crouch/Wolin ledger check. For ch. 11 §III, Charles V in 1520 "convened a meeting of the Cortes in the remote location of Santiago de Compostela" and then moved it "to the even more remote location of La Coruña" (same section) — scale weaponised by the executive.

**(4) 1688 as an information revolution — ch. 11 §VI, ruling (p).** "the invention of modern political party organizations gave constituents a new technology for monitoring their representatives… the development of a very active print news culture also certainly lowered costs of information acquisition. These changes helped ensure that the British Parliament did not follow the same course as the Cortes of Castile" (ch. 8, same section).

**(5) The defender's ledger — ch. 10 §VI and §VIII.** Ehrenberg: "The growth of the use of fire-arms had forced them to surround themselves with stronger fortifications, and their regular revenues, often very large, were never sufficient to produce the enormous sums needed. The credit of the cities therefore was accordingly their most powerful weapon in the struggle for their freedom" (ch. 2, 'Why Credit Was Important', part0009_split_001). Cologne against Charles the Bold at Neuss borrowed "more in two years than it had borrowed in the preceding half-century" (ch. 6, 'Development of Public Credit in Cologne', part0013_split_002). Appendix B: Ghent's war-year military spending "ranged from 25% in 1353–54 to 77% in 1345–46" (ch. 2, n. 4).

**(6) Ch. 7 — the debt-tax cleavage.** "merchants tended to own debt whereas members of the craft guilds bore a significant part of the tax burden necessary to service this debt" (ch. 1, 'Representative Assemblies', part0008_split_003); Cologne's 1481 rising (already in ch. 7) came of "a proposed increase in excise taxation" (ch. 6, part0013_split_004). Genoa, 1339: "One of the new regime's first acts was to burn the official list of the state's creditors" (ch. 6, part0013_split_008).

## 5. What it CONTRADICTS

**(i) Scale, credit and the bloc thesis — at full strength.** Spine §4 makes the cannon the possession of the few polities that "can tax at the scale it requires"; the four candidates are continental. Stasavage's five centuries of data say scale was the *disability*: no territorial state borrowed long-term before 1489, and when they did they paid more, the city-state advantage remaining "very sizeable through the seventeenth century" (ch. 4, 'Regression Results', part0011_split_007). His conclusion is aimed at this book's premise: "war actually had considerably more ambiguous effects on state formation in Europe than is commonly believed… Territorial states had an advantage in this new military environment because they could draw on larger populations for revenues and manpower. But city-states also had an advantage to the extent that they could support the political institutions necessary to raise abundant credit at low cost" (ch. 8, 'The Debate on War and State Formation', part0015_split_001). His Dutch case is Tilly's capital-intensive survivor with a mechanism — "a relatively small state like the Dutch Republic was able to prevail in a military struggle with a much larger enemy" (ch. 7, 'Public Credit in Holland', part0014_split_012) — and he will not grant the technology exogeneity, leaving open whether the military revolution was "attributable primarily to exogenous technological changes… or, alternatively… an exogenous increase in the intensity of inter-state competition" (ch. 2, n. 22). This reaches the DEFEND-tier mechanism (spine §6), the four-polity casting (§5) and the Depreter limb of the EU conditional (§8(b)).

**(ii) Taxation without assemblies — at full strength.** Ch. 10 §III finds that France consolidated by not consulting its Estates. Stasavage's model says an executive without an assembly holding a control right cannot borrow: "territorial rulers did not establish a long-term debt earlier because no one was willing to lend to them on such terms" (ch. 2, 'When Did States First Borrow Long-Term?', part0009_split_002). The crown of 1439–46 had, by his account, no funded debt for seventy-six years, and acquired one only through the one institution in the kingdom that resembled a city council, and only as far as that council could see; the fiscal-military state's "first full demonstration" was a tax state without the second leg of public finance, and its route — non-consultation — led to the default cycle of 1561–88. The territorial polities that mastered credit kept assemblies with control rights: "the only states in the sample to which this applied were Holland, Great Britain after 1688, and Württemberg" (ch. 3, 'Prerogatives', part0010_split_002). The dating is the same objection from the other side: "after this point [1500] territorial states moved to create debts in rapid succession… a general phenomenon" driven by "intensified warfare associated with the Habsburg attempt to establish supremacy" (ch. 2, same section) — Parker's century, not the book's hinge.

**(iii) Venice.** Ch. 10 §VI diagnoses Venice's limit as "almost no bureaucracy"; Stasavage's is population, and in the long run the oligarchy that made the credit — and the credit outlived the power: "Venice continued to borrow at very low interest rates into the eighteenth century, long after its period as an innovator in industrial production, or as a principal hub of commerce, had ended" (ch. 2, 'Economic Explanations', part0009_split_004). Credit, in his Venice, is decoupled from consolidation altogether.

**The book's best answer.** On (ii), the sharpest: the mechanism was never a credit mechanism, and ch. 10 §III must now say so rather than imply it. The chain runs weapon → *permanent taxation* → administration → subordination; ch. 10 §I locates the state "in the recurring transaction", and the transaction was the taille collected, not a bond sold. His own material supports the ordering: even Holland's revolution "was first and foremost a tax revolution… it was only after this point that borrowing became a major source of war finance" (ch. 7, 'Public Credit in Holland', on Fritschy). The tax base of 1439–46 is what the rentes of 1522 were sold against — precondition, not substitute. And the assemblies France dispensed with were, on his evidence, useless for credit anyway: a veto assembly did nothing for borrowing in his regressions, and the Estates of 1576 proposed default. Good on the mechanism; it costs one honest sentence — that the artillery state financed its train on tax and short-term merchant credit, and the funded debt came two generations later by a different route.

On (i), partly good, partly a concession. Good: his attrition data are the mechanism from the city's side — credit bought survival, not scale: Mainz "lost its status in 1462 as a free imperial city" in "internal conflict and fiscal crisis", Ghent was "forcibly absorbed by a territorial state" (ch. 2, 'When Did States First Borrow Long-Term?'); and the Tilly assessment's concession applies — the mechanism explains the first consolidators, not why every polity became like them. The concession, owed to ruling §8(g): a consolidator financing the stack by taxation without a control-right assembly, or by rents from the stack itself, is on his evidence a polity that can tax and cannot be trusted; the French and Castilian default cycles are the price. He adds a twist the book's tense can carry: "Today… geographic scale is no longer as much of an obstacle as it once was, but the underlying point remains; for representative government to function effectively individuals need access to information" (ch. 1, 'Introduction'). The legibility stack abolishes the distance that beat the bureau de ville and the Cortes — but the information it cheapens flows to the centre, not to the constituents. Whether the modern consolidator's assembly is the Estates of Holland or the Cortes of 1665 is what §8(g) leaves open; he supplies the vocabulary for scoring it. The bloc casting remains a wager, and his book makes it dearer.

On (iii): the "scale" correction and one sentence in ch. 10 §VI conceding that Venetian credit outlived Venetian power — the fiscal leg fully present, consolidating nothing — which strengthens the counter-case.

## 7. Verdict

Stasavage changes the register of ch. 10 §III and ch. 12 §IV, not the book's direction: the mechanism survives him because it is a tax mechanism and he writes about credit, but the manuscript must now say so in words, since his data show that the polities which could borrow were the smallest, that no territorial state borrowed long-term before 1489, and that France's fiscal-military state ran seventy-six years without a funded debt. The single most important thing the author should know is that the assemblies France dispensed with in the 1440s were, on his evidence, useless for credit — a veto assembly did nothing for borrowing, and the Estates of 1576 proposed default — so the ratchet cost the crown nothing it could have used; but the same evidence says scale without intensive representation buys taxation and not trust, and his Cortes of Castile — prerogatives kept, control lost, abolished by a weak king in 1665 — is the T2 portrait of the ornamental settlement ruling §8(g) now scores for the democratic bloc.
