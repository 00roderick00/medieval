## 1. The article in two paragraphs

Ruggie's subject is the ground the discipline walks on without looking at it. Beyond the end of the Cold War, a second remaking may be under way on "a deeper and more extended temporal plane," involving "a shift not in the play of power politics but of the stage on which that play is performed" (p. 139 / PDF 2); the field cannot see it because "We lack even an adequate vocabulary; and what we cannot describe, we cannot explain" (p. 144 / PDF 7). His remedy is to re-examine how the modern stage was built. "Politics is about rule," rule being "legitimate dominion over a spatial extension" (p. 148 / PDF 11); the modern form "has differentiated its subject collectivity into territorially defined, fixed, and mutually exclusive enclaves of legitimate dominion. As such, it appears to be unique in human history" (p. 151 / PDF 14). Medieval Europe is "the archetype of nonexclusive territorial rule," in which "authority was both personalized and parcelized within and across territorial formations and for which inclusive bases of legitimation prevailed" (pp. 149–50 / PDF 12–13) — Meinecke's "heteronomous shackles" against the modern "homonomous" order (pp. 150–51 / PDF 13–14).

The transition is explained by three domains "irreducible to one another: namely, material environments, strategic behavior, and social epistemology" (p. 152 / PDF 15), the third — above all single-point perspective, of which "The concept of sovereignty, then, was merely the doctrinal counterpart" (p. 159 / PDF 22) — having "a relative autonomy" (p. 160 / PDF 23). The modern state then devised a way to live with its own exclusivity: "What we might call an 'unbundling' of territoriality (of which the doctrine of extraterritoriality was the first and most enduring instantiation) over time has become a generic contrivance used by states to attenuate the paradox of absolute individuation" (p. 165 / PDF 28). That terrain "is the place wherein a rearticulation of international political space would be occurring today" (p. 171 / PDF 34), and the European Community "may constitute the first 'multiperspectival polity' to emerge since the advent of the modern era" (p. 172 / PDF 35) — "nothing less than the emergence of the first truly postmodern international political form" (p. 140 / PDF 3). The last line: "Unbundled territoriality is not located some place else; but it is becoming another place" (p. 174 / PDF 37).

## 2. What it CONFIRMS

**(a) The plural substrate (ch. 1 §II).** "authority was real, dense, law-governed — and nowhere exclusive" (l. 357–58) is Ruggie's p. 150 at T2.

**(b) The state as episode (ch. 1 §VI, l. 730–31).** The modern state "was invented by the early modern Europeans. Indeed, it was invented by them twice, once in the leading cities of the Italian Renaissance and once again in the kingdoms north of the Alps sometime thereafter" (p. 166 / PDF 29).

**(c) The decades — the highest-value confirm, from a writer with no fiscal-military thesis to serve.** "Let us return for a moment to the western European monarchies around the middle of the fifteenth century. Their future looked bleak" — Castile, Aragon, France in ruins, England "further torn by the deadly Wars of the Roses." Then: "The turn came suddenly. By the end of the century, strong centralized administration had 'almost completely transformed the political life of western and west-central Europe' … In France, moreover, a weak central monarchy ended up absorbing a stronger duchy of Burgundy in the process" (p. 161 / PDF 24). The book's Part II chronology and the Depreter case, stated by the IR canon. His explanation differs (§5A); the dating does not.

**(d) Consolidation as subordination (spine §8(h)(b)).** "The chief characteristic of the modern system of territorial rule is the consolidation of all parcelized and personalized authority into one public realm" (p. 151 / PDF 14), with Contamine's sentence, unused in the manuscript: "'The king's war' and 'the kingdom's war' must, in the end, be identical" (p. 151 / PDF 14, citing Contamine 1984: 169); and "Over time, however, the issue at stake increasingly became the terms of central rule, not the fact of it" (p. 162 / PDF 25).

**(e) The military driver acknowledged.** "the feudal cavalry was coming to be undermined by the longbow, pike, and crossbow and the feudal castle, subsequently, by gunpowder. … Generating revenue through taxation augmented the trend toward standing armies" (p. 153 / PDF 16). And the invitation the book answers: "no epochal thought has been expressed by any serious specialist in that field since 1957, when John Herz published his essay, 'Rise and Demise of the Territorial State' — and this despite the fact that changes in military technology and in the relations of force are widely acknowledged to have been driving factors of political transformation throughout human history" (p. 143 / PDF 6).

**(f) Fast, then stopped (spine §8(a)).** Transformation "came quickly by historical standards" and "amid crisis and disintegration of the previous order" (p. 166 / PDF 29); "Once the system of modern states was consolidated, however, the process of fundamental transformation ceased" (p. 167 / PDF 30) — the ratchet's irreversibility, from outside.

**(g) The losers' revenue drove the constitutional by-products (spine §8(g)(4)).** "monarchs did not set out to weaken their constitutional powers by selling offices or convening assemblies to raise taxes; they sought only to increase their revenues" (p. 166 / PDF 29); n. 125: "the goals of the losers — the insatiable quest for revenues on the part of rulers — not of the winners, drove the process that ultimately made possible the imposition of constitutional constraints." The converse of the stack-rents ruling, at T2: the by-products came from bargained revenue.

## 3. What it CORRECTS

**(i) The Anderson attribution (ch. 1 §II l. 359–63; n. 1-2) is wrong by Ruggie's own notes.** The manuscript gives one quotation to Anderson 1974: 37–38 — "patchwork of overlapping and incomplete rights of government… inextricably superimposed and tangled," "plural allegiances, asymmetrical suzerainties and anomalous enclaves abounded" — "canonised for the international-relations literature by Ruggie 1993." Ruggie's sentence (p. 149 / PDF 12) has two sources: n. 55 gives "patchwork of overlapping and incomplete rights of government" to Strayer and Munro, *The Middle Ages* (1959), p. 115; n. 56 gives "inextricably superimposed and tangled" and the "plural allegiances" clause to Anderson, pp. 37 and 37–38. Unless Anderson himself quotes Strayer and Munro at p. 37 — uncheckable here, the ISEG PDF not being in corpus — the first phrase is not his, and the sentence should carry both attributions. Kobrin and Zielonka both cite Ruggie 1983 for the composite, and Ruggie's n. 57 confirms the 1983 *World Politics* article did the canonising; "by Ruggie 1993" is imprecise. Cite 1983 for the canonisation, 1993: 149 nn. 55–56 for the reconstruction, and verify Anderson before commit.

**(ii) ch01/sources.md l. 44–46** — "heteronomy vs sovereignty; medieval parcelisation. Open PDF (ResearchGate)" — has no tier and no pins. Now IN REPO: T2; pp. 149–51, 153, 156, 161, 165, 171–72.

**(iii) A nuance for ch. 1 §II l. 410–13:** "no internal tendency was visibly carrying it toward the exclusive territorial state — a point on which the neomedieval theorists and this book agree." Ruggie holds that thirteenth- and fourteenth-century change was already undermining "the personalistic ties and the modes of reasoning on which feudal authority rested" (p. 155 / PDF 18), while "territorially defined, territorially fixed, and mutually exclusive state formations did not emerge at this point. It was not that simple" (p. 153 / PDF 16). Read "toward the exclusive territorial state *in particular*."

No date, figure or engagement in the manuscript is contradicted.

## 4. What it ADDS

**(1) The Pentagon's 1993 bafflement, ch. 12 §IV — the take-back's baseline.** "It is the world in which even the U.S. Pentagon is baffled by the problem of how to maintain the national identity of 'its' defense-industrial base" (p. 172 / PDF 35); n. 143: the Pentagon "is considering, among other options, a 'reconstitution' model," but "It has proved extraordinarily difficult, however, to decide whether what should be available for reconstitution should be defined by ownership, locale, commitment to the economy, nationality of researchers, or what have you." CHIPS, the Intel stake and the H200 cut are thirty-three years of that question.

**(2) Kapstein's satellite, ch. 12 §I.** "If the United States wanted to prevent the gathering or transmission of information by satellite, it could easily do so by shooting the satellite down" (p. 143 / PDF 6, quoting Kapstein). The NGA's imagery cut of March 2025 is the hypothetical performed; Ruggie's derision of it (§5B) makes the pairing sharper.

**(3) Unbundling as the state's own contrivance, ch. 2 and ch. 4.** Mattingly's paradox: the new states "found they could only communicate with one another by tolerating within themselves little islands of alien sovereignty" (p. 165 / PDF 28) — a name for the Kontore and the Steelyard, and a frame for GDPR Art. 3 and the DMA: the state reaching a non-territorial subject by unbundling its own territoriality.

**(4) The fairs, ch. 12 §II — the off-switch's limit stated in 1993.** "local lords at any time could have closed down a fair in their domain — much as states today can close down offshore markets or even shoot down communications satellites — though other lords in other places probably would have been only too pleased to provide alternative sites" (p. 155 / PDF 18). Jurisdictional arbitrage as the constraint on revocation; the bloc thesis is the answer — the alternative sites now lie inside three or four commercial worlds, and a fab is not a fair.

**(5) The mercenary taboo's genealogy, ch. 3/4.** "when the concept of state sovereignty expanded to become the concept of national sovereignty, the use of mercenaries in warfare declined and ultimately was eliminated altogether" (p. 163 / PDF 26, citing Thomson 1990); the manuscript does not cite Thomson.

**(6) Herz 1957, Appendix A** (p. 143 / PDF 6, n. 23): the predecessor who argued a weapon ended the territorial state's protective function — the nuclear ceiling, anticipated.

## 5. What it CONTRADICTS

**A. The anti-functionalist objection — at full strength.** Ruggie names the book's mechanism and rejects it. Of the claim that "a shift in the advantage to the offense in weapons technology allegedly drove the desire for larger and fiscally more capable political formations" (p. 156 / PDF 19, citing McNeill): "The theory that the modern state was functionally determined in this manner has at least two serious shortcomings. First, its retrodictive value is severely limited … the Italian city-states and the Hanse, in fact were viable political alternatives to the territorial state, fully able to levy taxes and raise armies, for the better part of two centuries. In social life, two centuries is no mere time lag. Second, there is a substantial logical and empirical gap between the existence of some functional pressure for political units to grow, and their blossoming specifically into a system of territorially defined, territorially fixed, and mutually exclusive state formations. To assert that the specificities of the modern state system also were functionally determined entails a claim of staggering historical and intellectual proportions" (p. 156 / PDF 19). And against any repeatable mechanism: "while there may be law-like generalizations in the medieval-to-modern transformation, there are none of it. Accordingly, understanding that transformation — and presumably any analogous shift that may be taking place today — requires an epistemological posture that is quite different from the imperious claims of most current bodies of international relations theory" (p. 169 / PDF 32). Reaches the DEFEND-tier mechanism (spine §6), §8(a), and CLAUDE.md §1 claim 2 ("a MECHANISM, not an analogy"; the "repeatable historical arc").

*The book's best answer.* Three limbs, good on the first two. First, Ruggie attacks a claim the book does not make — that weapons determined the *form*. The chain ends at subordination of intermediaries under a permanent fisc, and spine §8(g) has already ruled the constitutional form open; Ruggie's second shortcoming is the book's own position and should be conceded under his name. Second, his two centuries are the two centuries *before* the ratchet: his counter-examples run 1300–1500, the mechanism 1440–1500, and the book's evidence is the end of exactly those alternatives — the Peterhof closed in 1494 and the Hansa's silent disintegration (ch. 4), Venice unable "in the long run" to compete (Lane, ch. 10), Charles VIII's train (chs. 6, 10). Spruyt's selection event has no date in Ruggie; the book supplies it, and Ruggie's p. 161 concedes the decades. Third, his positive account — "legitimate power became fused with the provision of public order, steadily discrediting its deployment for primitive extraction and accumulation" (p. 161 / PDF 24) — is a legitimation layer the book can carry (chs. 7, 9), not a rival cause. Where the answer is weaker: the book has no account of why the *idea* of exclusive sovereignty formed, and should not pretend to one. Ruggie's "there are none of it" is the strongest theoretical form of the anti-determinism objection the outline assigns to the Swiss pikes; ch. 1's critiques.md and the Coda's method note should carry it.

**B. The off-switch is Kapstein's satellite.** Spine §8(c) rests the American verdict on "fundamentally the ability to turn their systems off." Ruggie, on the satellite sentence: "There is an extraordinarily impoverished mind-set at work here, one that is able to visualize long-term challenges to the system of states only in terms of entities that are institutionally substitutable for the state. … The theoretical or historical warrant for that premise has never been mooted, let alone defended" (p. 143 / PDF 6); and "In no sense could the medieval trade fairs have become substitutes for the institutions of feudal rule. Yet, the fairs contributed significantly to the demise of feudal authority relations" (p. 155 / PDF 18). Reaches §8(c) and the hold-on-balance platform-centralisation prediction.

*The book's best answer.* Ruggie's target is the use of the off-switch to deny change; the book uses it to describe how the state responds to a change it accepts in full — Part I is the concession Kapstein refused. The book agrees the platforms are not substitutes for the state (the universalism correction) and adds that a non-substitutable entity can still be *subordinated*, the off-switch being the instrument. The fairs cut both ways: the lord could close the fair but another lord would host it — which is why the mechanism predicts consolidation *inside blocs*, where the sites run out. Good, provided ch. 12 says it is answering Ruggie and not Kapstein.

**C. The EC will not become a state.** "There is no indication, however, that this reimagining will result in a federal state of Europe — which would merely replicate on a larger scale the typical modern political form" (p. 172 / PDF 35); the multiperspectival polity is stable and novel, not transitional. Reaches the bloc casting (wager tier) and the Depreter test as spine §5 applies it.

*The book's best answer.* Ch. 12 predicts no federal Europe either: "either the assertion-to-enforcement lag closes as the fisc arrives, or the Union completes its century as the American bloc's canonical province" (l. 7906–09). "Multiperspectival polity" is the Depreter failure mode named — apparatus without a centre — and Ruggie's "no indication" is the null hypothesis Appendix C scores against ReArm, SAFE and the own-resource proposals. The residue: Ruggie grounds the form's stability not in the state's decline but in the state's own unbundling contrivance, which is the neomedievalists' "dispersal is stable" error in its strongest form. The reply — unbundling was always the contrivance of states that had a fisc, and the Union is unbundling without one — is right but must be made.

**D. Bull's test is the wrong test.** Spine §7 has Bull supplying the test — "does the trend replace sovereignty, or reproduce it in new units?" Ruggie's p. 143 denies that substitutability is the question: transformation ran through fairs that substituted for nothing. *Best answer:* the test the book applies is subordination (§8(h)(b)); the "new units" limb is the book's, not Bull's, and §7 should say so.

**E. Not a return but a new place.** Ruggie nowhere names Bull or the new medievalism; his one present-to-medieval analogy is hedged — "somewhat in the manner of medieval rulers vis-à-vis cosmopolitan bodies of religion and law" (p. 173 / PDF 36). This cuts against Bull, Friedrichs and Cerny more than against a book that forbids resemblance. He is the literature's lexicographer, not a member.

## 7. Verdict

Ruggie changes the book's register in one place and its weight everywhere else: the mechanism survives him because his objection — the weapons thesis cannot explain the *form* — is now the spine's own ruling, and his dating of the turn to 1450–1500 is the best disinterested T2 warrant the manuscript has for its decades. The single most important thing: the manuscript's one citation of him hangs on a misattributed quotation, and its two most exposed devices — the off-switch and the EU-as-bloc — are the two things Ruggie named in 1993 as the impoverished mind-set and the polity that would never become a state; ch. 12 must answer him by name, and can, because the fairs and the Pentagon's baffled reconstitution model are his own evidence for the book's case.
