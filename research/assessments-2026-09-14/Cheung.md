## 1. The book in two paragraphs

Cheung's object is a type of state, and his claim is that Xi Jinping has built one. "The techno-security state is an innovation-centered, security-maximizing regime that prioritizes the building of technological, security, and defense capabilities to meet expansive national security requirements based on heightened threat perceptions and the powerful influence of domestic security coalitions" (pp. 2–3 / PDF pp. 17–18). China under Xi "is a stridently statist regime and is in the process of shifting from a defensive to an offensive posture. The United States, by contrast, is strongly anti-statist" (pp. 4–5 / PDF pp. 19–20). The Xi-era state was assembled through four lines of effort — innovation-driven development, a national security state, military-civil fusion (MCF), military strengthening — "under his close personal control through direct command of key institutions" (p. 12 / PDF p. 27), with a fifth, "economic securitization", added under American denial from 2018 (pp. 297–99 / PDF pp. 312–14).

The book's temper is deflationary in both directions. Against Western alarm it measures the thing and finds it modest: "the Chinese techno-security state in the twenty-first century is not the overwhelming colossus it is sometimes imagined to be" (p. 284 / PDF p. 299) — a core base of 3.4–3.6 per cent of GDP against an American 5.9 (pp. 277–80 / PDF pp. 292–95). Against Beijing's self-description it finds fusion barely begun and the edifice resting on one man: "This open-ended overconcentration of power and authority in Xi represents the gravest risk to the future prospects of the techno-security state as well as to China more generally" (p. 286 / PDF p. 301). His framework is bello-centric — Tilly is quoted for "war made the state" (p. 257 / PDF p. 272) — and his aphorism is the book's thought from the threat side: "If war is the mother of invention, then the threat of war is the patron saint of innovation" (p. 272 / PDF p. 287).

## 2. What it CONFIRMS

**(a) The Chinese church's authority is delegated, by statute (ch. 2 §III; C11-1).** Manuscript: the party-state "asserted — with fines, delistings and disappeared founders — that the platforms' authority was delegated, not owned". Cheung supplies the legal base at T2: the National Security Law of July 2015 enumerates "political security, homeland security, military security, economic security… scientific and technological security, information security" and more, and with "the National Intelligence Law (2017), Counterespionage Law (2014), Counterterrorism Law (2015), Cybersecurity Law (2016)… the Chinese NSS has unassailable legal authority to do anything it wants within its own borders and increasingly beyond" (pp. 63–64 / PDF pp. 78–79) — the extraterritorial reflex ch. 2 reads in PIPL Art. 3.

**(b) The summit is personal; the two-body exposure is real (ch. 9 §V, ch. 12 §IV, Coda §V).** "The leadership and organizational structure of the Chinese techno-security state are hyperconcentrated under Xi's authority; he wields absolute authority as the chairman of the Central National Security Commission, Central Military Commission, and Central Military-Civil Fusion Development Commission, which make up the triumvirate" (p. 284 / PDF p. 299); "His current deputies lack the political qualifications to take over in anything more than a short-term acting capacity" (pp. 285–86 / PDF pp. 300–01). Ch. 9's routinisation datum has its instance in the CNSC — "a personalistic symbol of Xi's command of the NSS" (p. 67 / PDF p. 82).

**(c) The foundry line sits inside the security perimeter (ch. 10 §VIII; C10-1).** The National Integrated Circuit Industry Investment Fund, October 2014, "RMB 139 Bn (US$22.6 Bn)… raised", stakeholders including China Tobacco, China Mobile and CETC (p. 130 / PDF p. 145), classed as an "MCF-lite" fund of the techno-security state (p. 127 / PDF p. 142) — the manuscript's Big Fund I figure carried from the Chinese side.

**(d) The self-reliance headline is a series (ch. 12 §II; context variable).** The 14th Plan (March 2021) gives "nine out of the sixty-four chapters" to national security and makes "science and technology independence and self-reliance… the strategic support for national development" (pp. 304–06 / PDF pp. 319–21).

**(e) The denial regime's Chinese reception (ch. 10 §VIII).** ZTE, May 2018, "likened to a 'Sputnik' wake-up moment" (p. 7 / PDF p. 22); Liu He, November 2020, on "the threat of having its neck choked (卡脖子, qiabozi)… the potential cutoff in exports by the United States of critical high-technology components such as semiconductors" (p. 301 / PDF p. 316); Xi, April 2020, on supply chains "independently controllable, secure and reliable" with "at least one alternative source" (p. 302 / PDF p. 317).

## 3. What it CORRECTS

**(i) "Fusion" is a Chinese term of art, and the manuscript uses it for something else.** Ch. 2, C4-1 and ch. 11 §VI call the Chinese absorption mode "fused ownership" / "fusion", meaning platforms ruled as departments of the party-state. In China, fusion is military-civil fusion (军民融合, junmin ronghe): a national strategy since March 2015, a party-constitutional priority since 2017, with its own commission (CMCFDC, January 2017) and outline (March 2018) (pp. 6–7 / PDF pp. 21–22; pp. 89, 94 / PDF pp. 104, 109). A China-literate reviewer will read the manuscript's "fusion" as MCF and then find Cheung rating MCF "early-stage" (Table C.5, p. 298 / PDF p. 313). Reserve "fusion" for MCF and rename the platform mode — "ruled as departments", "absorption by discipline".

**(ii) The Chinese fisc is domestic first.** The manuscript's Chinese fiscal instances are all external-facing. Cheung's Table 2.1 shows the internal-security budget overtaking defence: 2018 public security RMB 1,378.1bn (central 204.1, provincial 1,174) against defence RMB 1,128.0bn — 122.2 per cent (p. 73 / PDF p. 88). For a candidacy resting on the legibility stack, the fisc that pays for the gaze should be stated as the larger line.

**(iii) A date not to carry from him.** Cheung has the chip appropriation as "the 2021 US Innovation and Competition Act" (p. 291 / PDF p. 306); the manuscript's enacted CHIPS and Science Act (9 August 2022) is correct.

## 4. What it ADDS

**(1) Dated baselines for Appendix C.** *Institutional:* CNSC approved January 2014, first meeting April (p. 6 / PDF p. 21); NSL July 2015 (p. 63 / PDF p. 78); MCF a national strategy March 2015 (p. 89 / PDF p. 104); CMCFDC January 2017, twenty-six members, reporting to the Politburo Standing Committee (p. 101 / PDF p. 116); all thirty-one provincial MCF committees by end-2017 (p. 104 / PDF p. 119); PLA centennial target 2027 (p. 308 / PDF p. 323). *Fiscal:* defence growth averaging 9.1 per cent and public security 11.7 per cent, 2013–18 (p. 72 / PDF p. 87); core techno-security state 3.4–3.6 per cent of GDP (2019), expanded 5.5–5.7, against US 5.9 and 7.1 (pp. 277–80 / PDF pp. 292–95); official 2019 defence US$172.1bn plus off-budget R&D estimated at 15–20 per cent (p. 276 / PDF p. 291); Xinjiang security spending RMB 5.45bn (2007) to 57.95bn (2018) (p. 74 / PDF p. 89). *Market finance of the stack:* asset securitisation by the ten state defence conglomerates RMB 2.548tn (US$369.3bn) in 2012–20, RMB 283.1bn a year, AS ratio 56.9 per cent (p. 123 / PDF p. 138); eighty-three MCF guidance funds with RMB 872.3bn (US$126.2bn) raised by end-2019 (p. 127 / PDF p. 142); the two channels together "41 percent of the average official annual defense budget" (p. 278 / PDF p. 293); the National MCF Fund, December 2018, Ministry of Finance the largest holder at 14.3 per cent (pp. 132–33 / PDF pp. 147–48). *Private firms in the stack:* 1,189 civilian WERPL and 1,800 ECC licence-holders in 2018, roughly fifty with category-1 licences, "between 3 percent and 5 percent of the civilian licensed community" (p. 115 / PDF p. 130); the licence catalogue cut 62 per cent, 755 to 285 items, in 2018; "the approximately three thousand firms that have been signed up into the defense acquisition system represent a participation rate of just 2.3 percent" of 130,000 technology SMEs, "Huawei, Alibaba, and… DJI… likely to be within this recruited pool" (p. 116 / PDF p. 131); DJI at 70 per cent of the global consumer drone market (p. 135 / PDF p. 150). *Legibility:* the Big Intelligence System's "twenty-eight thousand analysts" at national, provincial and municipal level (p. 75 / PDF p. 90). *Seen from Beijing:* PRC entities 24.25 per cent of the Entity List by end-March 2021, 454 of 1,872 (p. 282 / PDF p. 297).

**(2) The licence as the Chinese Sforza thread (ch. 12 §III; ch. 4).** Private firms enter the acquisition system only through WERPL and ECC licences "jointly administered by SASTIND and the PLA armaments bureaucracy", while "The big state-owned defense firms have maintained an iron grip on licenses" at category 1 (pp. 114–15 / PDF pp. 129–30). Function delegated by licence, ownership retained: the Chinese settlement with its tech barons is a licensing settlement, closer to the American form than "fused" allows.

**(3) A measurement warning Appendix C must print.** "public reporting on the CMCFDC's activities stopped beginning in 2019" (p. 102 / PDF p. 117); "MCF was not mentioned in the 14th Five-Year Plan" and Xi now says "military-civil unity" (pp. 139–40 / PDF pp. 154–55); MCF initiatives "have been relabeled or are no longer transparent" (p. 307 / PDF p. 322). Every Chinese row scored from 2019 is scored against concealment.

**(4) The pendulum inside one polity (ch. 12 §IV).** A Maoist techno-security state built on "Two Bombs, One Satellite" (p. 143 / PDF p. 158); dispersal under Deng — "The techno-security state found itself in survival mode" (p. 10 / PDF p. 25); reconsolidation from the 1991 Gulf War, "which made clear to Chinese military chiefs that the PLA was chronically outdated and ill-prepared to fight a high-technology war" (p. 10 / PDF p. 25) — a Castillon watched on television.

**(5) The stack financed from securitised savings (spine §8(g) item 4).** Where §8(g) contemplates a state financed by stack rents, China shows the inverse: the state financing its stack from "new market-sourced funding streams for defense and dual-use projects" (p. 278 / PDF p. 293), under a regime in which "national security and political determinants usually take precedence over market considerations" (p. 120 / PDF p. 135). Tilly's bargain without representation, in the form of the securitised loan.

**(6) The American diagnosis from a China specialist (ch. 10 §VIII).** "The DoD at the beginning of the 2020s accounts for a mere 3.6 percent of global R&D outlays compared to 36 percent at its height in 1960"; the top five technology firms "spent ten times more annually on R&D than the top five US defense prime contractors" (pp. 290–91 / PDF pp. 305–06) — the frontier-lab anomaly with a series behind it.

## 5. What it CONTRADICTS

**At full strength.** Four cuts, in descending reach.

*First, the driver.* Cheung's Chinese consolidation was not compelled by the price of decisive force. His opening question is "Why did Xi make a hard turn toward national security when he assumed power despite the absence of any significant changes to the country's security environment?" (p. 51 / PDF p. 66), and his answer is political: "The concrete security environment that China faced in the early 2010s had not radically deteriorated, but the way its new leaders perceived the situation had significantly altered" (p. 56 / PDF p. 71). The motives are the Soviet collapse, colour revolutions, "an apparent attempt to thwart his rise to power by a cabal of senior figures in the Hu regime" (p. 58 / PDF p. 73), Document No. 9 (p. 60 / PDF p. 75). The NSS was built 2013–17 for regime security; the chokepoint entered in 2018. This reaches the DEFEND-tier mechanism (spine §6) as applied to the polity §5 ranks first: the consolidator consolidated before the price moved.

*Second, the fusion.* Against "5/5 in execution" and "the settlement substantially executed" (spine §5; C11-1), Cheung's verdict on the move that puts private capability into the decisive stack is the reverse: MCF's twenty years are "an underwhelming achievement" (p. 116 / PDF p. 131); "Hybridization has yet to make a significant impact on the Chinese techno-security state" (p. 295 / PDF p. 310); "the public sector will remain the dominant player with the private sector limited to a minor role" (p. 298 / PDF p. 313); "Without meaningful integration, a fragmented techno-security state will be severely handicapped" (p. 286 / PDF p. 301). The obstacles are "some of its most privileged, protected, and politically powerful constituencies whose opposition will be fierce and relentless even as they pay lip service to following top-level orders" (p. 95 / PDF p. 110); "two hot ends but a cold middle" (p. 97 / PDF p. 112); guidance funds with "plenty of corruption, rent seeking" (p. 125 / PDF p. 140); and the defence monopolies re-merging after the 1999 split — aviation 2008, nuclear and shipbuilding in the late 2010s, the two space corporations in 2020 — so that "State-owned monopolies have so far successfully limited meaningful implementation of these market-opening measures" (p. 172 / PDF p. 187). The security apparatus itself carries "deep-seated organizational fragmentation" (p. 78 / PDF p. 93), and the override is one man: "relying on Xi's personal intervention is risky because he has limited bandwidth" (pp. 80–81 / PDF pp. 95–96). This reaches spine §5 and the hold-on-balance ranking in §6.

*Third, the dependence.* "Techno-nationalist dependence: China seeks long-term technological self-reliance but is heavily dependent on foreign technology and know-how in the meantime" (Table C.5, p. 298 / PDF p. 313); "the US techno-security state would be far less negatively impacted by decoupling than its Chinese counterpart" (p. 296 / PDF p. 311). Miller's point (iii), from the institutional side.

*Fourth, the American model.* The United States won the last contest without absorbing its firms: "governed interdependence" through "incentives and rewards rather than through control and penalties" (p. 268 / PDF p. 283); the National Security Resources Board's fate "a clear signpost that the realm of the US NSS would not reach deep into the economic sphere" (p. 266 / PDF p. 281). Read straight, this denies spine §8(b)–(c).

**The book's best answer.** To the first: concede, then split. The mechanism claims what a polity must do to field decisive force, not why a consolidator consolidates; and Cheung's own periodisation gives the second phase in the book's terms — economic securitisation from December 2017, dual circulation 2020, the 14th Plan — driven by "the rapid tightening of US-led export controls" that "forced the Chinese authorities into concerted action to prevent technological 'strangulation'" (p. 306 / PDF p. 321). Say plainly that Xi's state was built for political security and re-tasked by the chokepoint; the mechanism explains the stack's direction from 2018, not the state's origin in 2013. Good, but it costs China its status as the clean instance. To the second: the manuscript's "5/5" rests on the 2020–23 platform discipline and the legibility instruments, which Cheung does not cover; his verdict concerns the defence-industrial stack, which the state never lost to private hands and never had to take back. The answer is that China's consolidation is layer-specific — complete at canon, subordination and legibility; inherited at foundry-munitions-orbit, where the state's own monopolies are the over-mighty subjects; unexecuted at the private-tech-to-defence seam, where MCF has recruited 2.3 per cent of firms under licence. A real answer, and it requires the ranking's qualifier to be rewritten in those words. To the third: ch. 10 §VIII's triad has its Chinese case for the [PART B — (x)] paragraph — command over domestic firms by statute, dependence on foreign chokepoints, and a form of capture the triad does not yet name: capture of the consolidator's policy by its own state-owned monopolies, the great officers of the household rather than any private baron. To the fourth: partial. Spine §8(c) already predicts absorption in hybrid form — "title left, discretion severed" — and "governed interdependence" is that form under another name; Cheung's own diagnosis (3.6 per cent; ten to one) shows the old hybrid no longer governs. What he withholds is the prediction: revitalise the hybrid, do not take the switch. Stage him as Bratton is staged — ally on the diagnosis, rival on the outcome.

## 7. Verdict

Cheung changes the weight of the Chinese case and, in one place, its register: he carries every instrument the manuscript names for China at T2 with dates and figures, and shows that the polity the spine ranks first consolidated its state for political security before the price of decisive force moved, has drawn a thirty-fifth of its technology firms into the defence stack under licence, and remains "heavily dependent on foreign technology and know-how in the meantime". The direction survives — command over domestic barons by statute, a legibility fisc larger than the defence fisc, the stack financed from securitised savings — but "the settlement substantially executed" must become "executed at canon and legibility, inherited at the defence stack, unexecuted at the seam between them, and dependent at the chokepoint", in those words. The single most important thing: the Chinese over-mighty subjects the mechanism has not subordinated are not Huawei or Alibaba but the state's own defence monopolies, and the book should say so before a reviewer who has read Cheung says it first.
