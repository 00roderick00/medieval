## 1. The article in two paragraphs

This is the peer-reviewed origin of the "weaponized interdependence" frame — the theory of which the 2023 trade book (*Underground Empire*) is the narrative extension. Against the liberal reading of globalisation as flat, reciprocal and pacifying, Farrell and Newman argue that global economic networks converge on hubs, and that hubs are power: "Asymmetric network structures create the potential for 'weaponized interdependence,' in which some states are able to leverage interdependent relations to coerce others. Specifically, states with political authority over the central nodes in the international networked structures through which money, goods, and information travel are uniquely positioned to impose costs on others. If they have appropriate domestic institutions, they can weaponize networks to gather information or choke off economic and information flows" (printed p. 45 / PDF p. 4). The compressed aphorism: "Focal points of cooperation have become sites of control" (printed p. 47 / PDF p. 6).

Two strategies follow. The panopticon effect: "The first weaponizes the ability to glean critical knowledge from information flows, which we label the 'panopticon effect.' … States that have physical access to or jurisdiction over hub nodes can use this influence to obtain information passing through the hubs. Because hubs are crucial intermediaries in decentralized communications structures, it becomes difficult — or even effectively impossible — for other actors to avoid these hubs while communicating" (printed p. 55 / PDF p. 14). The chokepoint effect: "The second channel works through what we label the 'chokepoint effect,' and involves privileged states' capacity to limit or penalize use of hubs by third parties (e.g., other states or private actors)" (printed pp. 55–56 / PDF pp. 14–15); "Because hubs offer extraordinary efficiency benefits, and because it is extremely difficult to circumvent them, states that can control hubs have considerable coercive power" (printed p. 56 / PDF p. 15). Two cases carry the plausibility probe to 2018: SWIFT (both effects exercised — TFTP surveillance; the 2012 disconnection of Iranian banks) and the internet backbone (panopticon exercised at scale via PRISM and upstream collection; chokepoint deliberately not exercised).

## 2. What it CONFIRMS

**The concentration thesis, from the network side, in a T2 journal.** Manuscript Chapter 10 §VIII currently carries the platform-side witness through Bratton alone ("few, ruinously expensive chokepoints — which is to say, the precondition of an off-switch"). Farrell and Newman state the same finding as peer-reviewed IR: "Contrary to Keohane and Nye's predictions, key global economic networks have converged toward 'hub and spoke' systems, with important consequences for power relations" (printed p. 49 / PDF p. 8); "networks are structures in the sociological sense of the term, which is to say that they shape what actors can or cannot do" (printed p. 49 / PDF p. 8).

**Chapter 1 §V's verdict on Slaughter.** The manuscript rules *A New World Order* "important, and wrong" — "The networks she identified coordinate; they do not command, tax, or compel." Farrell and Newman make the identical move by name: they quote Slaughter's claim that in the networked world "power is 'power with,' rather than 'power over'" (printed p. 49 / PDF p. 8, citing *The Chessboard and the Web*, p. 163) and answer with the SWIFT record: "The weaponization of SWIFT runs counter to the expectations of liberal accounts of globalization. It demonstrates how globalized networks can indeed be used to exercise 'power over'" (printed p. 70 / PDF p. 29). The manuscript's judgement now has a canonical scholarly ally that pre-dates it.

**The revocable patron layer of Chapter 12 §I.** The manuscript's March 2025 week ("the deep-strike permissions granted in November 2024 and revocable at will — all of it another polity's property") is a chokepoint exercise in Farrell and Newman's precise sense, and their Caytas quotation supplies the register: "disconnection from SWIFT access is, by any standard, the financial market equivalent of crossing the nuclear threshold, due to the vital importance of the embargoed services and near-complete lack of alternatives with comparable efficiency" (printed p. 67 / PDF p. 26 — Caytas quoted by the authors, so pin it as such).

**Legibility as governing capacity (Chapter 12 §III).** "Under the panopticon effect, states' direct surveillance abilities may be radically outstripped by their capacity to tap into the information-gathering and information-generating activities of networks of private actors" (printed p. 55 / PDF p. 14) — the theoretical statement of the manuscript's "uniform legibility is a governing *capacity* … the API builds by default." Hayden's line is the quotable exhibit: "This is a home game for us. Are we not going to take advantage that so much of it goes through Redmond, Washington?" (printed p. 72 / PDF p. 31).

**The three-and-a-half-bloc cast.** "This geographic skew effectively means that only the United States and a couple of other key states and statelike entities (most notably, the European Union [EU] and, increasingly, China) enjoy the benefits of weaponized interdependence, although others may still be able to play a disruptive role" (printed p. 57 / PDF p. 16). And the EU's specific defect matches the manuscript's Depreter finding (apparatus without a centre): "the EU has fragmented instruments of financial regulation, which means that it has not been able to exercise control over SWIFT, except when its member states have agreed unanimously on formal sanctions under prodding from the United States" (printed p. 75 / PDF p. 34).

## 3. What it CORRECTS

Nothing. The manuscript nowhere cites the article, and no manuscript figure, date or attribution conflicts with it. One near-item, filed under ADDS rather than CORRECTS: Chapter 10 §VIII's "chokepoints" is used as ordinary language via Bratton, where IR has since 2019 had it as Farrell and Newman's term of art; unattributed use adjacent to Bratton risks a reviewer's marginal note.

## 4. What it ADDS

**(1) The mapping onto the book's vocabulary — stated precisely, with its failure point.** The chokepoint effect maps onto the manuscript's *revocation* (the revocable authorisation, the patron's pause of Chapter 12 §I) and the panopticon effect onto the *legibility stack* (Chapter 12 §III). But the mapping fails at the axis of the coercion, and the failure is exactly the space the book occupies. Farrell and Newman's coercion is **horizontal** — state against state, exercised *through* a compliant private hub (SWIFT delisting Iranian banks under joint US–EU pressure; AT&T's "unique relationships" commandeered, printed p. 73 / PDF p. 32). The hub itself is never a rival: it is instrument, not over-mighty citizen. The manuscript's question is **vertical** — who owns the switch, state or platform; the contest of Chapter 12 §IV and spine §8(b)–(c). The article therefore theorises the *use* of the off-switch but not the *contest for its ownership*; and *licence* has no counterpart at all — their state compels and subpoenas, it never prices access as revenue. The manuscript's "licensing fee … collected as the settlement's popular revenue" is beyond their frame entirely.

**(2) The "institutions, not just topology" argument — ally, decisively.** Asked whether the article makes state capacity or network position the variable: it makes them jointly necessary, and capacity is the discriminating one. The decisive passages: "The central expectation of our argument is that states' variable ability to employ these forms of coercion will depend on the combination of the structure of the underlying network and the domestic institutions of the states attempting to use them. States that have jurisdictional control over network hubs and enjoy sufficient institutional capacity will be able to deploy both panopticon and chokepoint effects" (printed p. 58 / PDF p. 17); "States need both leverage over network hubs and appropriate institutions if they are to take advantage of the panopticon and chokepoint effects. States and jurisdictions that have potential leverage over network hubs, but do not have the appropriate institutions, cannot make good use of weaponized interdependence" (printed p. 75 / PDF p. 34). The internet case is the controlled demonstration: the United States holds maximal topology yet "has not directly leveraged its dominance to create chokepoints, both because it lacks the domestic institutional capacity, and because several administrations have believed that its strategic and business interests are better served by open networks" (printed p. 74 / PDF p. 33). Position without apparatus is impotent — which is the book's mechanism (administrative capacity as the gate on decisive force) confirmed at T2 in the modern material. Better still for spine §8(c): the SWIFT case is *regulation of the switch rather than seizure of the property* avant la lettre — SWIFT's title, membership and Belgian domicile untouched; its discretion over who connects, severed by state instrument.

**(3) What Chapter 12 (and 10) can now pin at T2 rather than assert.** SWIFT's centrality with figures: 1977, "22 countries … roughly 500 firms … approximately 3,000 messages" annually; by 2016, "more than 200 countries and some 11,000 financial institutions … more than 6.5 billion messages annually" (printed pp. 59–60 / PDF pp. 18–19). The 2012 Iran disconnection as the executed chokepoint, with Campos: "Disconnecting banks is an extraordinary and unprecedented step for SWIFT" (printed p. 68 / PDF p. 27). Backbone concentration: "Ninety-seven percent of intercontinental internet traffic travels across roughly 300 cables" (printed p. 62 / PDF p. 21); the 70-per-cent-through-Northern-Virginia estimate (printed p. 65 / PDF p. 24) is carried in the article from a *Washingtonian* piece — usable, but pin the underlying grade honestly. The Lambert–Tworek precedent (Britain's pre-1914 cable monopoly and Germany's wireless counter-build, printed p. 56 / PDF p. 15) gives Part III a documented earlier run of build-alternative-hubs.

**(4) The division of labour against the 2023 book.** The article does NOT contain: the executed Russia sanctions of 2022 (it has only the 2014–15 SWIFT *threats*, printed pp. 77–78 / PDF pp. 36–37); the semiconductor war (ZTE and Huawei appear only as closing asides — the compute-foundry half of Chapter 12's stack is absent; its hubs are payments and information only); dollar clearing, explicitly deferred — "As we discuss further in unpublished research, similar patterns prevail in other global networks such as the dollar clearing system and some globalized supply chains" (printed p. 74 / PDF p. 33); anything on cloud-era AI or compute; and any normative programme of restraint. The clean split: this article carries the *theory and definitions* at T2 (peer-reviewed, the field's citation of record); the 2023 book, when it enters the corpus, carries the *post-2019 evidentiary extension* (Russia 2022, the chip chokepoints, dollar clearing) at T3 serious trade. Cite the effects and scope conditions from here, never from the trade book.

## 5. What it CONTRADICTS

**First, at full strength: the state gets this power for free.** "The asymmetric networks that make up much of the structure of a globalized world were not constructed as tools of statecraft. … By building centralized networks, market actors inadvertently provide states, which are concerned with political as well as economic considerations, with the necessary levers to extend their influence across borders" (printed p. 54 / PDF p. 13). No taille, no permanent finance, no fiscal threshold: the most consequential coercive capability of the era, on this account, was built by private actors pursuing efficiency, and the state inherited it by jurisdictional accident. They add: our cases show these strategies "are not simply coterminous with United States market size or military power" (printed p. 46 / PDF p. 5). This cuts at the spine's DEFEND-tier claim (§6): "Power follows the ability to pay for decisive force."

**Second: the endgame is erosion, not settlement.** "The more that privileged states look to take advantage of their privilege, the more that other states and nonstate actors will take action that might potentially weaken or even undermine the interdependent features of the preexisting system" (printed p. 76 / PDF p. 35); "the United States may face the slow erosion of its ability to weaponize key economic networks, constraining its ability to project power globally" (printed p. 79 / PDF p. 38). Overuse dissipates the asset; no Tudor consolidation follows.

**The book's best answer.** To the first: the article's own institutional condition does half the work — the levers are free, but the hand is not: OFAC, the post-9/11 legal instruments, the NSA's built apparatus are state capacity, purchased and political, and the authors concede institutions "cannot costlessly be transformed to confront new international challenges" (printed pp. 57–58 / PDF pp. 16–17). The other half is scope: panopticon and chokepoint sanction and observe; they do not take and hold. The book's priced-force chain concerns the decisive military stack, which is emphatically not free — and the legibility stack's *cheapness relative to the compute stack* should be conceded openly as what the article really proves. The answer is good but partial: the article does document a major class of modern state power that required no new taxation, and the manuscript should not pretend otherwise. To the second: the contradiction is valence, not substance. What Farrell and Newman call erosion — targets and allies building rival hubs, China's post-ZTE chip drive (printed p. 77 / PDF p. 36), Putin's "cutting the branch they are sitting on" (printed p. 78 / PDF p. 37) — *is* bloc formation; their lock-in sentence ("States are locked into existing network structures only up to that point where the costs of remaining in them are lower than the benefits," printed p. 77 / PDF p. 36) is the mechanism of parallel settlement stated from the network side. Their 2019 endgame is the book's premise. That answer is good.

## 7. Verdict

This article changes the book's weight, not its direction: it converts several of Chapter 12's asserted contemporary claims — hub concentration, the revocable layer, legibility as capacity, the EU's capacity gap — into T2 peer-reviewed citations, and it hands Chapter 1 §V a canonical ally against Slaughter. The single most important thing to know is its scope-condition sentence — position over hubs is worthless without domestic institutional capacity (printed p. 58 / PDF p. 17) — which is the book's own mechanism restated in modern IR, at the price of one honest concession: the panopticon and chokepoint powers accrued to the state without new taxation, so the fiscal threshold must be defended on the military stack, not on the coercion channels. Engage it by name in Chapter 12 §IV, and split labour cleanly: theory from this article, post-2019 evidence from the 2023 book.
