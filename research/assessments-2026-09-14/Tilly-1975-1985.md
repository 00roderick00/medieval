## 1. The essays in two paragraphs

The 1975 chapter is a postscript on "theories of political development", and its thesis is a rule for using Europe at all. Having judged it "quite unlikely that the exact sequences of events we have lovingly reconstructed from the European record will ever occur again", Tilly locates relevance in "the chance that the relationships among variables—between the costliness of the armed forces and the extent of the extractive apparatus, for example—which held in European history will continue to hold in our own time, although the specific sequences and forms in which those relationships worked themselves out in Europe will not" (p. 601 / PDF 1). His European findings include "(7) the high cost of state-building; (8) the intimate connection between the conduct of war, the building of armies, the extension and regularization of taxes and the growth of the state apparatus" (p. 633 / PDF 33), and "the major forms of political participation which westerners now complacently refer to as 'modern' are for the most part unintended outcomes of the efforts of European state-makers to build their armies, keep taxes coming in… and fend off the threat of rebellion on the part of ordinary people" (p. 633).

The 1985 essay repacks this as the protection racket: "war making and state making - quintessential protection rackets with the advantage of legitimacy - qualify as our largest examples of organized crime" (Warning, para 1); "Someone who produces both the danger and, at a price, the shield against it is a racketeer" (Double-Edged Protection, para 2). "War making, extraction, and capital accumulation interacted to shape European state making" (Double-Edged Protection, para 9), and state violence does four things: "1. War making: Eliminating or neutralizing their own rivals outside the territories… 2. State making: Eliminating or neutralizing their rivals inside those territories 3. Protection: Eliminating or neutralizing the enemies of their clients 4. Extraction: Acquiring the means of carrying out the first three activities" (What Do States Do?, para 1). Rights are the residue of resistance: "When ordinary people resisted vigorously, authorities made concessions: guarantees of rights, representative institutions, courts of appeal" (How States Formed, para 1).

## 2. What they CONFIRM

**CLAUDE.md §2 and the Introduction — mechanism, not analogy.** The book's method is Tilly's third "application" (p. 601, above): relationships among variables travel, sequences do not. The strongest T2 licence the book has for Part III.

**Ch. 3 §I, ch. 5, ch. 9 — the over-mighty subject.** "Many lords who did not pretend to be kings, furthermore, successfully claimed the right to levy troops and maintain their own armed retainers. Without calling on some of those lords to bring their armies with them, no king could fight a war; yet the same armed lords constituted the king's rivals and opponents… For that reason, before the seventeenth century, regencies for child sovereigns reliably produced civil wars. For the same reason, disarming the great stood high on the agenda of every would-be state maker" (Violence and Government, para 4).

**Ch. 8 §VII, ch. 11 — Tudor subordination.** "Tudor demilitarization of the great lords entailed four complementary campaigns: eliminating their great personal bands of armed retainers, razing their fortresses, taming their habitual resort to violence for the settlement of disputes, and discouraging the cooperation of their dependents and tenants" (Violence and Government, para 6); the Percys and Dacres as the delicate case (para 7).

**Ch. 10 §I — "subordinating, not eliminating"** (manuscript l. 5785, pinned to Tilly 1990: 25). The 1985 form is blunter: "every European government before the French Revolution relied on indirect rule via local magnates" (Violence and Government, para 10), by "eliminating, subjugating, dividing, conquering, cajoling, buying… exemptions from taxation, creations of honorific offices, the establishment of claims on the national treasury" (para 11).

**Ch. 10 §III — cost drives apparatus.** "The more costly the activity, all other things being equal, the greater was the organizational residue" (What Do States Do?, para 5); "Extraction brought fiscal and accounting structures into being" (para 4); variations "in the expense of the particular kind of armed force adopted… resulted in the principal variations in the forms of European states" (Double-Edged Protection, para 12). The chain's second and third links are Tilly's sentences; only the first is not.

**Ch. 11 §V — Elton** on 1534/1540 and the Peacock–Wiseman "ratchet effect" (History Talks, para 10): the collision noted in Tilly.md §3 recurs here.

## 3. What they CORRECT

1. **ch10/critiques.md Objection 6 and Revisions 1** say Tilly "was deliberately technology-agnostic" and cite the 1990 book for the slogan. Neither survives: the slogan is not in the retrieved chapter (header), and Tilly was not agnostic — he adjudicated the artillery step by name and rejected it (§5(i)). The steelman must be rebuilt as an objection Tilly actually made.
2. **Spine §8(a)'s "not war-made-the-state in general"** understates the gap. Tilly's own disarmament story already contains artillery — as a quotation from Gerhard on the Empire after 1648: "Everywhere the razing of castles, the high cost of artillery, the attraction of court life, and the ensuing domestication of the nobility had its share in this development" (Violence and Government, para 8). The differentiation is not "Tilly omits the gun" but "Tilly dates it two centuries late and lets Gerhard carry it".
3. **The phrase "the rights that we now consider fundamental"** occurs in neither the 1985 text nor, on search, the 1990 sidecar. Do not attribute it. The 1985 wording is "guarantees of rights, representative institutions, courts of appeal" (How States Formed, para 1).

## 4. What they ADD

**Ch. 1 / Appendix A — Tilly as neomedievalist two years before Bull, with technology as the disperser.** "At the same time as the state system absorbs the entire world, the individual state may be losing part of its significance… toward the regional grouping and the compact of superstates above, toward the subnational region, ethnic population, or racial group below… Perhaps control of a contiguous territory was peculiarly advantageous to the land- and water-bound technologies of the European state-making eras, but an obstacle to full exploitation of technologies of flight, electric power and electronic information-handling… Perhaps, unknowing, we are writing obituaries for the state" (p. 638 / PDF 38); cf. "the multinational corporation is superseding the national state as a repository of power" (p. 630, reporting Johnson 1972). The book's inversion — information technology now re-concentrating — answers a claim the founder of bellicism made in 1975.

**Ch. 12 §VII and §8(g) — Lane's owners.** Tilly reports Lane's three owners of the protection-selling government — "The first model approximates a Jeffersonian democracy, the second a petty despotism, and the third a military junta" — and adds "a dominant class", under which "a subordinate government would tend to maximize monopoly profits - returns to the dominant class" (Protection as Business, paras 6–7). §8(g) item 3's "fusion of barons and government" has a T2 name: the fourth owner. And the 1975 baseline is that consolidation *suppressed* rights first — "the abridgements of political rights which occurred with the formation of national states" (p. 613 / PDF 13); "a widespread suppression of political rights and participation by the state-makers" (p. 625) — so the ornamental route is Tilly's default early state, not a deviation from it.

**Ch. 12, Appendix C — the externally-armed path, 1985 form.** "As the twentieth century wore on, however, it became increasingly common for one state to lend, give, or sell war-making means to another; in those cases, the recipient state could put a disproportionate effort into extraction, protection, and/or state making and yet survive" (How States Formed, para 2) — the origin of 1990 pp. 207–8. And Appendix C's precedent: theory "must proceed in an open-ended and prospective fashion… specifying what paths away from, say, traditional kingship are likely and what affects the probabilities" (p. 635 / PDF 35); the ten survival conditions (pp. 632–3).
## 5. What they CONTRADICT

**(i) Tilly considered the book's thesis and rejected it — Bean 1973.** He summarises Richard Bean: "the improvement of artillery in the fifteenth century (cannon made small medieval forts much more vulnerable to an organized force) shifted the curve of economies and diseconomies to make larger armies, standing armies, and centralized governments advantageous to their masters" (Protection as Business, para 11). Then: "Bean's summary does not stand up to historical scrutiny. As a matter of practice, the shift to infantry-backed artillery sieges of fortified cities occurred only during the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries. Artillery did improve during the fifteenth century, but the invention of new fortifications, especially the trace italienne, rapidly countered the advantage of artillery. The arrival of effective artillery came too late to have caused the increase in the viable size of states. (However, the increased cost of fortifications to defend against artillery did give an advantage to states enjoying larger fiscal bases.)" And: "The increasing decisiveness of naval warfare, which occurred simultaneously, could well have shifted the military advantage to small maritime powers such as the Dutch Republic" (History Talks, paras 1–2). This reaches spine §8(a) and the DEFEND-tier mechanism, and it is worse than Tilly.md §5(i): not neglect but a verdict.

**(ii) The fifteenth century is absent from both essays.** The 1985 argument grows from "the growth of the French state from 1600 onward" (Warning, para 7); the French series begin in 1515 and 1522 (History Talks, paras 7–8); disarmament is Richelieu's "in the 1620s" (Violence and Government, para 8). The 1975 "main rhythm" opens with "the first great national states… roughly 1500 to 1700" (p. 637 / PDF 37). No artillery, cannon, gunpowder, siege, fortification or "fifteenth" occurs in ch. 9.

**(iii) Forms are plural and set by balance, not by one mechanism.** 1975: forms follow "the class coalitions, past and present, supporting a particular state's government, and… the relationship of that state to the whole system of states" (p. 613); 1985: "the relative balance among war making, protection, extraction, and state making significantly affected the organization of the states that emerged" — Spain militarised, "Venice or Holland" oligarchic, the Papal States policed (How States Formed, para 2). The 1990 three paths are this matured; and since the two essays disagree with each other (coalitions v. activity-balance), the book should not cite "Tilly's mechanism" as one thing.

**(iv) Inadvertence.** "Power holders did not undertake those three momentous activities with the intention of creating national states" (Double-Edged Protection, para 9) — against ch. 10 §III's crown "definitive in intent".

**(v) Rights come from resistance, and the no-rights path is external supply, not domestic rent.** "European states built up their military apparatuses through sustained struggles with their subject populations… The agreements on protection constrained the rulers themselves, making them vulnerable to courts, to assemblies, to withdrawals of credit, services, and expertise. To a larger degree, states that have come into being recently… have acquired their military organization from outside, without the same internal forging of mutual constraints between rulers and ruled" (War as International Relations, paras 6–7). §8(g) item 4 ("financed by stack rents rather than by bargained broad taxation") extends Tilly to a case he did not state in 1985; 1990 pp. 207–8 (commodity exports, aid) is the nearer carrier.

**The book's best answer.** On (i): the rejection has three limbs and the manuscript holds the evidence against two. That artillery sieges of fortified cities "occurred only during the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries" is what ch. 6 exists to refute — Normandy and Guyenne, 1449–53, which Tilly does not name. The trace italienne limb is absorbed: the wall-cost response is the defensive face of the same price shock (Mangini and Petroff, ch10 critiques), and Tilly's own parenthesis on fortification costs advantaging "larger fiscal bases" is the mechanism in miniature. The third limb matters most: Bean's explanandum was the *geographic size* of states, and Tilly's refutation is aimed there ("render ambiguous the claim of a significant increase in geographic scale"). The book's explanandum is the closure of the sub-state path to decisive force and the permanence of the fisc — on which Tilly concedes: "After 1400 the European pursuit of larger, more permanent, and more costly varieties of military organization did, in fact, drive spectacular increases in princely budgets, taxes, and staffs" (History Talks, para 3). The answer is good, but obliges the book to cite Bean (*JEcH* 33, 1973) and say in ch. 10 §VII that it is not Bean's claim. The naval limb is Tilly.md §5(v) again and stays the weakest point: the book has not said whether the compute stack behaves like artillery or like the navy that sustained the Dutch. On (ii): as Tilly.md concluded — admissible under the 1990 p. 36 rule only if the book shows why 1439–46 held and the 1360s did not. On (iii): §8(b) and §8(g) already concede plural forms; say plainly that the mechanism is claimed for the consolidators and form is Tilly's territory. On (iv): co-evolution and the 1446 formula, as before. On (v): honest wording — Tilly supplies the direction (constraint follows struggle over the means of war) and the no-constraint case (means acquired without struggle); the inference that a stack-rented fisc is a means acquired without struggle is the book's, and Appendix C should score it as such.

## 7. Verdict

These essays do not change the book's direction — the 1975 "relationships among variables" sentence is the book's own method in the founder's words, and the 1985 four activities carry the chain's second and third links verbatim — but they change its register in ch. 10 §VII, because Tilly did not ignore the artillery step: he read it in Bean, rejected it on chronology, and the chapter must answer a verdict rather than fill a silence. The single most important thing the author should know is that the differentiation from Tilly holds only by the explanandum: Tilly refuted an artillery theory of state *size* while conceding that fortification costs advantaged larger fiscal bases and that after 1400 costlier military organisation drove budgets, taxes and staffs — so the book must cite Bean, say it is not Bean, and rest on 1449–53 and the closure of the sub-state path. On §8(g) item 4, 1985 gives the direction and the externally-armed exception but not the domestic-rent case; that extension is the book's and must be marked as such.
