## 1. The book in two paragraphs

Ertman asks why states formed *differently*, and his dependent variable is a two-by-two: absolutist or constitutional regime, patrimonial or bureaucratic infrastructure (Table 1, p. 10 / PDF 25 — Latin Europe patrimonial-absolutist; the German states bureaucratic-absolutist; Britain and Sweden bureaucratic-constitutional; Hungary and Poland patrimonial-constitutional). The book "argues that three factors – the organization of local government during the first few centuries after state formation; the timing of the onset of sustained geopolitical competition; and the independent influence of strong representative assemblies on administrative and financial institutions – can account for most of the variation in political regimes and state infrastructures found across the continent on the eve of the French Revolution" (p. 6 / PDF 21). Regime follows tenth-century local government; infrastructure follows timing.

The timing thesis is Gerschenkron's, and it carries the book's date: "states that expanded and differentiated their infrastructures before about 1450 (early statebuilders) often did so using methods and institutional arrangements that became increasingly outmoded and even dysfunctional as the centuries passed, but that proved very difficult to replace due to the power of vested interests", whereas late statebuilders "possessed the advantage of being able to adopt the latest techniques of administration and finance" (p. 27 / PDF 42). The cause is a labour market: "Prior to 1450, such personnel could exploit their very strong labor market position, owing to the scarcity of their skills, to promote institutional arrangements like proprietary officeholding and tax farming" (p. 27). War is accepted as motor and dismissed as explanation: "precisely because geomilitary competition and the resulting tendencies towards absolutism and the growth of the state were so ubiquitous, they cannot in themselves explain differences in the paths of development" (p. 317 / PDF 332; cf. p. 26 on the "nonsimultaneous" onset).

**Against Tilly, Spruyt and Hoffman.** Tilly's engine is war-and-extraction converging by scale; Spruyt's is institutional logic with war intervening; Hoffman's is a tournament in which technology is the output of spending. Ertman takes the bellicist consensus as read (p. 4 / PDF 19), cites Tilly and Spruyt (p. 3 n. 8: "Following Spruyt"), and attacks the one link none of them attacked: the administrative one. He concedes the fisc and the gun and denies that the capacity the fisc bought was the crown's. He is the only one of the four with a 1450 threshold, the only one with an England of 1453–1659, and the only one with venality.

## 2. What it CONFIRMS

**(a) Ch. 10 §III — the 1439 monopoly.** Manuscript: "1439 the claim to the monopoly of force". Ertman: "by an ordinance of 1439, the Estates outlawed the private recruitment of troops and seigneurial tailles [OCR 'tattles'], in effect creating a royal monopoly of both coercion and taxation" (pp. 86–87 / PDF 101–02).

**(b) Ch. 10 §III — the first, reversed, making, and the ratchet's object.** Henneman direct, which the manuscript carries only through Hoffman: "Charles V underscored this point by canceling the fouage on his deathbed in 1380, and the indirect taxes were abolished in 1381" (p. 73 / PDF 88). Then the second making: "In 1439, the same body approved a general taille ... for one year to support a standing army. As such an army remained in existence, in the form of the compagnies d'ordonnance, even after the defeat of the English in 1453, the taille continued to be collected" (p. 73). That is the ratchet in a rival's words, with the standing army as its object. And the demonstrated property: "Since the mid-15th century at the very latest, French monarchs had established their de facto right to alter the level of taxation without consulting either the Estates General or any other national representative body" (p. 96 / PDF 111).

**(c) Ch. 10 §III — "It was simply not consulted."** "Russell Major, drawing on the testimony of Philippe de Commynes, argues that it was hostility towards that body on the part of Charles VII's ministers that led the king after 1440 to abandon his policy of frequent convocations" (p. 92 / PDF 107); Tours 1484 extracted "a promise that they would be convoked again in two years' time. Yet two years later, the assembly was not recalled. Indeed, a full, working Estates General was not to meet again until 1560" (pp. 92–93).

**(d) Ch. 10 §I — subordinating, not eliminating (ruling (b)).** France's local landscape "remained, even after the incorporation of the great fiefs, riddled by seigneurial and ecclesiastical jurisdictions, a legacy of precocious state formation" (p. 92).

**(e) Ch. 11 §II — Henry VII: monopoly of force without a fisc.** "the power of the great magnates with their regional strongholds and private armies was broken during the Wars of the Roses and their aftermath" (p. 179 / PDF 194); "England's withdrawal from European politics after its defeat in France meant that even when order was restored after 1485, there was little need to call Parliament with any regularity" (p. 180 / PDF 195).

**(f) Ch. 10 §VI — the offices inherited by the neighbours.** Maximilian's Austrian reforms of the 1490s "drew on models familiar to him from the more advanced southern Netherlands", imitated by Saxony (1499), Bavaria (1501) "and thereafter by nearly every principality in the Empire" (p. 243 / PDF 258, citing Hartung's "französisch-burgundische Einfluss").

## 3. What it CORRECTS

**1. The Orléans taille, against the 2 September ruling's wording.** Spine §8(a): "Contamine carries no taille grant at Orléans"; ch. 10 §III holds the grant as "a tradition, held at tertiary strength ... [GAP: Wolfe; Henneman; Major]". Ertman p. 73, citing Henneman 1976: 309–10, carries a *one-year* grant. This neither restores the textbook permanent taille nor sustains the negative as phrased: granted once, for a year, never asked for again. "Collected forever, granted never quite at all" needs that rewording; [GAP: Henneman] is part-closed at T2; the ruling's "settlement of 1439 to 1446" is strengthened.

**2. The élus.** Ch. 10 §III: the crown "inherited from the 1350s a lattice of *élus*". Ertman: the élus "were initially named by the Estates General, but by 1360 had come under royal control" (p. 76 / PDF 91); the Estates of 1355 instituted "Estate supervision of army musters" and collection "by officials appointed by the Estates (the elus)" (p. 85 / PDF 100). The lattice was the Estates' instrument, captured — sharper, not weaker.

**3. Ch. 11 §I: "the completed fiscal-military state" of 1494.** The invasion force "numbered no more than 20,000 combatants, and was thus smaller than many of the armies assembled during the Hundred Years War" (p. 95 / PDF 110); the *compagnies* were the cavalry core of "between a quarter and a third" of the army; the infantry was Swiss and German by contract "right down to 1558, when they still supplied 21,000 men out of a total infantry contingent of about 40,000" (pp. 94–95). "Completed" overstates; "the settlement exporting itself" survives.

Nothing else in the chapters read is contradicted on fact.

## 4. What it ADDS

**(1) The venality limb — the largest gap in Part II.** The manuscript never uses "venal", "proprietary office" or "sale of office" for France ("tax-farming" appears once, for the Ottomans). Ertman: "the administration of 15th-century France, whether central or local, judicial or financial, was dotted with clans and dynasties of officials who treated their offices as their own property" (p. 81 / PDF 96); "It was primarily to these and other officials, rather than to bankers or large-scale tax farmers, that the French governments of the late middle ages turned for loans and advances. This use of 'inside' credit ... became even more significant during the Hundred Years War" (p. 76). To ch. 10 §III–IV, by name: the artillery state's revenue administration was proprietary from the start.

**(2) The Recess of 1653 (p. 246 / PDF 261)** — the comparator the Hoffman assessment asked for, with figures: "new direct taxes of 530,000 Thaler, to be levied over a period of six years by the government rather than the Estates. In return for this concession, Frederick William confirmed the nobility's tax-exempt status." The 1439–46 sequence run again, exemption bargain included.

**(3) Matyas Hunyadi as the anti-ratchet.** "During the 1470s and 1480s, he built up a professional army of 28,000 men supported by a nonproprietary bureaucracy staffed by university-educated Humanists. Yet after Matyas's death in 1490, the Hungarian Diet ... promptly dismantled this new instrument of royal power" (p. 31 / PDF 46); Hungary was "almost completely overrun" in the 1520s (p. 266 / PDF 281). A fifth counter-case for ch. 10 §VI — apparatus without an irreversible fisc, reversed by the assembly on the king's death.

**(4) The timing thesis as T2 warrant for spine §7's "late arrival is inside the pattern"** — with a twist. The latecomer builds a *cleaner* apparatus; the pioneers "were to pay a heavy price for their precocity" (p. 89 / PDF 104), and the German princes were "fully aware of the French system of *venalité des offices* and determined to avoid it" (p. 244 / PDF 259). For ch. 12 §V: if America is England's position, Ertman predicts the latecomer's form — licensing without proprietary capture — which is the spine's hold-on-balance prediction with a mechanism behind it.

**(5) Absolutism cannot borrow.** "the very nature of absolutism prevented them from complementing those bureaucracies with the kind of credit systems necessary to survive drawn-out conflicts" (p. 263 / PDF 278); Table 4 (p. 220 / PDF 235): British spending 24 per cent of national income in 1756–63 against France's 13–15. For ch. 12 §IV and ruling (g).

**(6) The EU is untypeable by his rule.** He excludes "the Swiss Confederation and the Dutch Republic, both of which were confederal entities in which sovereignty rested with the constituent territories ... rather than with the center" (p. 5 n. 13 / PDF 20). For ch. 12 §IV.

## 5. What it CONTRADICTS

**(i) The 1450 divide is the book's date with a different cause, and the cause excludes the gun.** "During the middle ages (prior to about 1450), resources like administrative and financial know-how and ready cash were in short supply ... the supply of trained personnel and short-term funds increased substantially after 1450, thanks to the growth of universities and financial markets" (p. 318 / PDF 333). His one artillery sentence is post-1494 and describes the bastion: "The perfecting of artillery capable of breaching castle walls and the development of new infantry tactics during the course of the 1400s had held out the possibility that conflicts might once again be decided by a single decisive battle ... But these hopes were dashed when a revolutionary new system of urban fortification built around the bastion was invented in early 16th-century Italy" (p. 95 / PDF 110, citing Parker 1988). No Castillon, no Bureau, no siege train; Burgundy in one clause; Venice excluded (p. 5) and selling offices (p. 123); the Ottomans only as pressure on Hungary. "Changes in military technology" appear once, as a reason the supply of expert personnel "expanded greatly in the period after 1450" (p. 27) — technology enlarging the labour market, not repricing force. This reaches spine §8(a) and the DEFEND-tier mechanism.

**(ii) France, the book's model consolidator, is Ertman's exemplar of the state that consolidated its magnates and lost its apparatus.** "the price that they paid for this precocity was a substantial loss of effective control to proprietary officeholders, tax farmers, and officeholder-financiers who viewed the state not only as an instrument of princely power but also as a source of income and social standing" (p. 28 / PDF 43). After 1494, "the intense military pressures faced by France ... inevitably led to a massive increase in the role played by private finance, proprietary officeholding, and military entrepreneurship within the French state apparatus" (p. 91 / PDF 106). The verdict on the chain: "While war and geopolitical pressures more generally may have fostered the expansion of states in this area in terms of absolute size, it certainly encouraged neither rationalization in the Weberian sense nor the creation of proto-modern institutions" (p. 154 / PDF 169). Worst for ch. 10 §VII's "template Europe copied for three centuries": the *compagnies d'ordonnance* became magnate patronage — "controlled and commanded by the local royal governor, almost always a member of a prominent magnate family. The governor was free to fill the ranks of the prestigious compagnies with representatives of the most important local clans and factions" (p. 94 / PDF 109), with "ominous consequences for France after 1559". CLAUDE.md §2's chain ends in administrative capacity subordinating the intermediaries; Ertman's ends in administrative capacity appropriated by a new intermediary stratum. Reaches §8(a) and the third link of the doctrine chain.

**(iii) England: the 1530s is "Deepening Patrimonialism", not the Tudor turn.** The section title is "Deepening Patrimonialism and Its Temporary Demise, 1453–1659" (p. 178 / PDF 193). "The creation during the 1530s by Henry VIII and his minister Thomas Cromwell of a whole series of new revenue boards staffed by nonproprietary officials did represent a serious challenge to the Exchequer, that bastion of proprietary officeholding. Yet by 1554 the most important of these bodies ... had all been incorporated into the Exchequer"; and the cause: "due to the absence of war and thus of the incentives it would have created for military effectiveness, none of these attempts was sustained" (p. 181 / PDF 196). "if one looks only at central government, England fares worse than Castile" (p. 181); "By the reign of Charles I, the English state had become a kind of parasite" (p. 184 / PDF 199). The English fiscal-military state is 1660–1714, made by Parliament sitting annually under war pressure (p. 187 / PDF 202). The dissolution of the monasteries does not appear in his England. Reaches ruling (k) of 16 September and §3's "standing taxation established" read at the dissolution.

**(iv) Form is determined, not open.** Tenth-century local government "helped determine the type of representative assembly and ultimately the kind of political regime (absolutist or constitutional) that would emerge centuries later" (pp. 24–25 / PDF 39–40). Ruling (g) says the constitutional form is open; Ertman says it is fixed by a variable the book does not carry.

**The book's best answer.** On (i): Ertman never asks the book's question. He states the ratchet's outcome — the taille "continued to be collected" because the army "remained in existence" (p. 73) — and gives no account of why the peace of 1453 did not do what the deathbed of 1380 did. His timing thesis is compatible with the hinge, and a 1450 divide reached from the personnel side with no artillery in view is independent corroboration that something changed at the date the book names. Good. On (ii): concede, and gain by it. "Administrative capacity" in CLAUDE.md §2 is capacity to assess, collect and pay at campaign tempo — the account books of §IV — not Weberian rationality, and the mechanism claims the subordination of *armed* intermediaries; Ertman's officiers were unarmed. But the concession has a cost: the settlement bred a new rent-taking stratum, and the officeholder-financier lending the crown its own revenue is the nearest fifteenth-century form to the platform that licenses its stack to the state and lends against the contract. Ch. 12's hybrid should expect it. Good on the mechanism; weak on "template" — the latecomers copied the offices and refused the venality (p. 244). On (iii): the weakest point, and it lands on a ruling. Three limbs: Ertman's "little large-scale warfare" elides the 1540s, which cost more than the dissolution yielded; his variable is proprietary office, so a permanent confiscation that ended a universalist rival is invisible to his test by construction; and (k) rests the turn on the canonical-fiscal move, not on boards. But the claim that *standing taxation* was established in England in the 1530s cannot be carried on Ertman or on anything now in the corpus — the dissolution was a capital receipt, spent within a decade, and the 1552 statute against traffic in offices (p. 182) shows Parliament, not the crown, still fighting the apparatus. Honest recommendation: (k) should say "fisc without permanence" — the dissolution as the irreversible confiscation that made the crown's universalism, the English ratchet as Brewer's and Ertman's 1690s — or ch. 11 §II must state the disanalogy by name. The GAP (Youings, Hoyle) is now load-bearing. On (iv): ruling (g) already answers, and Ertman is its best T2 warrant — his patrimonial absolutism separates the legitimating authority (the king *legibus solutus*, p. 93) from effective control of the apparatus, the ornamental form stated for the fifteenth century. Cite him for the split; decline the determinism, which his own Danish and Swedish "aberrant" cases (pp. 32–33, 316) qualify.

## 7. Verdict

Ertman changes the book's register in Chapters 10 and 11 and lands one contradiction on a ruling: his 1450 is the book's date reached by a different road, his France — the model consolidator — is his exemplar of the state that consolidated its magnates and surrendered its apparatus to an intermediary stratum the book has never named, and his England makes the 1530s a deepening of patrimonialism rather than a Tudor turn. The mechanism survives because he never asks why 1453 failed to reverse what 1380 reversed and because his own p. 73 states the ratchet's outcome with the army as its object; ruling (k) does not survive him unamended, and venality must enter ch. 10 §III as a stated defect of the settlement. The single most important thing: the fourth rival concedes the gun and the fisc and takes the third link, and the book's answer — that it claims collecting capacity and the subordination of armed intermediaries, not Weberian rationality — is good only once it is written down.
