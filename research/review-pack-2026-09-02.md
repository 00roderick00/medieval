## Decision one: what to say about 1439

**What you ruled on 9 August.** That finance and firepower built each other over two decades: the taille of 1439 and the standing companies of 1445 came before the decisive use of the guns in 1449 to 1453, but the artillery revolution of the 1420s and 1430s came before the taille, so the Estates voted for permanent taxation in a world where royal guns were already visibly paying off. Artillery was the ratchet.

**What the probe found.** Three things. First, Contamine's text of the 1439 ordonnance contains no grant of a tax at all. It is the troop-monopoly decree. Permanent taxation, as far as the sources in the project show, was a practice that hardened over the following years through annual decrees, a formula used in 1446 that granted money "until the conclusion of peace," and a consolidation in 1450, rather than a single vote in 1439. So the phrase "the taille of 1439," which the council used and which your ruling repeats, is currently a piece of period shorthand that the record in the project does not carry. The full text of the 1439 decree exists in a printed collection called the *Ordonnances des Roys de France*, volume thirteen, which is available free online and is now on the list of things to fetch. Second, Contamine dates the point at which the king's councillors regarded the new arrangements as effectively permanent to 1445 and 1446, which is before the guns broke the walls of Normandy in 1449. So if the ratchet is the guns, what the guns locked in was not the crown's intention, which was already fixed, but everyone else's acquiescence: no Estates General was summoned again until 1484, and when it finally met, the system survived it. Third, the co-evolution itself is well supported. The Bureau brothers had taken over the artillery by 1437, and in 1442 the crown was spending twice as much on the new artillery as on the old, all under an improvised financial regime with, in Contamine's words, "neither budget nor forecasts." The guns achieved superiority before predictable finance existed, and predictable finance then made that superiority permanent.

**What you have to decide.** Whether to keep the words "the taille of 1439" in your spine as accepted shorthand until the decree's text has been fetched and read, or to change the ruling now to something like "the settlement of 1439 to 1446," and to add one sentence saying whose ability to reverse the settlement the ratchet removed: the assemblies' and the magnates', not the king's. My own view is that the second option is stronger and costs nothing, because the refined version fits the evidence better and still supports everything you want to say. But the spine is your document, and this is a sentence only you should write.

## Decision two: how to state the Standard Oil proof case

**What you ruled on 9 August.** That the United States will consolidate control over its technology stack in its own traditional way: not by nationalising the platforms but by licensing, legal process and, above all, the ability to switch their systems off. You gave Standard Oil as the proof: when the Supreme Court broke it up in 1911, John D. Rockefeller became richer and less powerful, because the state did not take the oil, it destroyed the vertical integration that made oil sovereign. And you asked a question: after the breakup, who owned the power to turn off the energy?

**What the probe found.** It fetched and read the Supreme Court's 1911 opinion and decree. Your reading holds on the power side: the decree handed every share of the thirty-seven successor companies back to the existing shareholders, Rockefeller first among them, and forbade the seven named individuals from ever recombining them. Wealth was kept; unified command was extinguished. But the answer to your question is a surprise. In 1911 the power to turn off the energy passed to nobody. The decree removed the private switch and did not create a public one. The public switch was then built over the following three decades by a different set of laws: the Hepburn Act of 1906, which made pipelines common carriers under federal supervision and which came five years before the breakup; a Texas law of 1917 doing the same at state level; the wartime Trading with the Enemy Act of 1917, whose licensing power over transactions is the ancestor of every later economic control; the Texas Railroad Commission's orders from 1930 onward, under which a state agency set the rate at which every oil well in Texas could produce; and federal emergency and energy-policy legislation through the 1970s. So the proof case has a lag: the decoupling of wealth from power happened in one act in 1911, but the state acquired the off-switch over a generation, using regulation rather than antitrust. The probe notes that this lag mirrors what the first probe found about France: a settlement that hardened over years, not a single stroke. One further caution: the figure for Rockefeller's fortune tripling after the breakup is currently supported only by an article without footnotes, so it cannot be used until a proper biography, Chernow's *Titan* or Yergin's *The Prize*, is in the project.

**A sentence in the chapter that now conflicts with your ruling.** Chapter 12, which you have already reviewed, currently says the platforms are "not the American state's Standard Oil but its monasteries," and treats the antitrust instinct as nineteenth-century nostalgia. Your ruling says Standard Oil is the proof case. The two can be reconciled, because the chapter is attacking the nostalgic idea of trust-busting while your ruling reads 1911 as a consolidation of control, but the sentence as written contradicts the spine, and the machine will not touch a reviewed draft without your instruction.

**What you have to decide.** Two things. Whether the proof case, when it is written into chapter 12, is stated with the lag, which I recommend, since it is truer and it rhymes with the French story, or in the compressed form. And how the monasteries sentence is to be reconciled: dropped, qualified, or kept alongside a new passage explaining that the American method was regulation of the switch rather than seizure of the property.

## Decision three: the six proposed changes to chapter 6

**Background.** Chapter 6, "1450: The Year the Walls Fell," is the book's most complete chapter and the one that carries its central evidence. The Normandy council answered your objection that the speed of the reconquest must have come from artillery by agreeing with you at one level and not another. Its formulation, which five of eight models reached in nearly the same words, was that artillery set the clock speed of the collapse, while finance and politics determined that a collapse would happen at all. Most of the roughly one hundred strongholds never saw a gun; they surrendered under negotiated agreements that fixed a date on which the gates would open unless a royal relief army appeared. The French call this a *délai de secours*, a relief deadline. Artillery made those deadlines short, because everyone knew what the guns could do; then the destruction of the only English field army at Formigny in April 1450 meant no relief could ever come, and every deadline expired.

**What the council recommended and what the machine found.** Six changes, each assessed today against what the chapter already says. First, to separate two different kinds of speed, the speed of the campaign, which was a chain of surrenders with political and financial causes, from the speed of the individual sieges, which was genuinely the guns' doing and roughly ten times faster than before. Second, to make the relief deadline the chapter's central device. Third, to quote two English documents as the English state's own post-mortem: the Duke of Somerset's report to Parliament in 1449 that Normandy had no place supplied with ordnance or artillery of any kind, and William Worcester's *Boke of Noblesse*, which says in plain terms that lack of money to pay the soldiers was a cause of the loss, and to quote the French chronicler Blondel's line that it is not walls that protect cities but soldiers trained for war. Worcester's text has now been fetched and the key passages verified. Fourth, to present the Battle of Castillon, the book's opening scene, with two dissenting voices acknowledged: the historian Michael Howard's flat statement that it was in no sense a victory for field artillery, and the contemporary chronicler Thomas Basin's verdict that Talbot died of his own rashness. Fifth, to add the Gascony sequence of 1451 to 1453 as a controlled experiment: the same artillery train won Gascony by negotiation in 1451, lost it in a week in October 1452 when Bordeaux invited the English back, and had to win it again by fire in 1453, which shows that guns produce speed but not loyalty. The machine notes that Vale's 1969 study disputes the council's claim that a broken tax promise was the trigger for Bordeaux's revolt, so that detail needs care. Sixth, to add a caution that several of the French sources praising the artillery are one interdependent tradition rather than independent witnesses.

**What you have to decide.** A yes or a no on each of the six, recorded in the assessment file in the chapter 6 folder. The machine has stated for each what the chapter already contains and which supporting sources are in the project at page level and which are not.

# The Economist's transparency essay — placements against the spine

## What the essay gives the book

The essay's central caution — that attributing to weapons the advantages that belong to the systems which use them best has misled military minds before — is the book's sceptics-concede pattern restated by a defence editor in 2026 after eight years of watching the transparency revolution. Historians made the same error about cannon for a century; the artillery state's argument is that the institutions required to own guns, not the guns, did the work. The essay is a mainstream citation for the symmetry: same error, same correction, five hundred years apart.

## Six placements (proposals)

1. **The diffusion objection — capital-intensity migration.** The drone appears to invert the mechanism: $3,000 units on consumer-electronics supply chains, adopted by Hizbullah within months. But the essay locates decisive capacity in the connective tissue — satellite constellations, Maven-class targeting fed by frontier models, kill chains, encryptors procured urgently because data outran networks — all standing, peacetime, continuously funded commitments at bloc scale. Capital intensity migrated from the projectile to the seeing-and-deciding apparatus. The drone is this cycle's arquebus (cheap, diffused, tactically transformative, strategically subordinate); the sensor-targeting complex is the siege train. Diffusion at the tactical layer, consolidation at the systemic layer, simultaneously. Pair with the fifteenth-century handgun/arquebus diffusion material (Hussites; Burgundian ordonnance gunner retainers). Fits whichever chapter answers "modern technology fragments rather than consolidates" — likely ch10 §VIII or ch12; also the modern face of spine §8(a)'s ratchet. Tag as book's-own.

2. **Epic Fury as the living Depreter case.** Depreter's refinement (apparatus without a coherent political centre predicts failure; Burgundy 1477) has a 2026 instance: the most exquisite strike apparatus in history against Iran produced stalemate because the political centre could not specify what the apparatus was for. Necessary, never sufficient. Place beside Burgundy in ch10; it also answers the technological-determinism charge. Flag every Iran BDA figure [RE-CHECK AT PRESS]; CRS R48887 is the primary already in ch12.

3. **ch06 §VI — Talbot and the Belloc syndrome.** Talbot died from underestimating the apparatus; Belloc's Blood (the Maxim-gun couplet) and the knock-out-blow prophets (Freedman's genealogy; Bush 2003; the 2017 "cannot win"; 5,000 targets a day for a war without aims) from overestimating it. 4. **The sceptics-concede arc, compressed.** Biddle ("easy to exaggerate"), Owen (first-rate forces render FPVs irrelevant), Radakin (the Israeli stand-off model) each erode inside the essay — Biddle's own Fort Irwin observation, Gady's Lebanon evidence, Radakin's model tried at scale in Iran. 5. **The nuclear ceiling — a confessed limit for the conclusion.** The Taiwan material: attacking China's connective tissue means attacking systems that also control nuclear forces; the full transparency war between blocs may be unfightable; nobody knows where the red lines are, only that they exist. Put the question explicitly in ch12/coda. Consistent with spine §5's nuclear-overhang paragraph and GPT 5.6 Sol's "nuclear as scope condition" (model-council-synthesis.md).

## 1. What the act of 2 November 1439 actually was

**The verifiable core [T2 — Contamine].** Contamine calls the 2
November 1439 act "la grande « pragmatique sanction »" by which
Charles VII "revendiqu[a] **le droit exclusif de lever des troupes**"
(t1 OCR ll. 16798–16816, print ~pp. 266–67 [approx]). His one
verbatim quotation from the text is the captain-licensing clause: the
king "a ordonné que certain nombre de capitaines de gens d'armes et
de traict sera ordonné pour la conduicte de la guerre" (ch. X fn 2,
t1 ll. 19169–73; edition cited: *Ordonnances des Roys de France*, t.
XIII, pp. 306–313). The diagnosis presented to the Estates at Orléans
was Jean Jouvenel des Ursins's: "les seigneurs ont levé et mis sus
plusieurs gens de guerre… ce sont les rassemblements de troupes
opérées par les « seigneurs » — entendons les princes —, qui se
trouvent à la racine du mal" (t1 ll. 16086–94, ~pp. 256–58 [approx]).
The 1439–40 implementation failed under princely pressure ("sous la
pression des princes, au premier rang desquels le duc de Bourbon…
cette première tentative concertée et préparée de réforme militaire
échoua"), but the method was already defined: "faire dépendre du roi
seul, « sans moyen », tous les capitaines, toutes les troupes, de
leur assigner une garnison, de procéder à une sélection, de prévoir
un payement et un contrôle réguliers" (t1 ll. 16798–16816).

**The taille question — the probe's first structural finding.
Contamine is SILENT on a 1439 taille grant.** Nowhere in either tome
does he state that the Estates of Orléans granted the taille,
permanent or otherwise; "taille" and 1439 never co-occur as
grant-language in the monograph; the index has no entry for taille,
aides or gabelle. His 1439 is exclusively the troop-monopoly act.
The familiar textbook sentence — "the Estates of Orléans granted the
king a permanent taille" — is therefore carried in this repo only at
T3 (ch10 memo §2 cites Britannica-grade tertiary sources), and the
question of what the ordonnance's fiscal articles actually said
(including the clause structure on tailles — the tradition that art.
44 forbade seigneurial tailles and reserved the levy to the king)
**cannot currently be verified from anything in repo**:

  Roys de France* t. XIII, pp. 306–313, is OPEN on Gallica
  (ark:/12148/bpt6k108684h) but the site is bot-blocked — HUMAN PULL;
  entered on retrieval-master. Dependent claims: every article-level
  statement about the 1439 text, including any taille clause and the
  seigneurial-taille ban.]
- **[UNVERIFIED — RETRIEVAL: Henneman, *Royal Taxation in
  Fourteenth-Century France* (context: what the taille and aides were
  by 1439 and in what legal form they had been levied since the

  France* (the Estates' politics at Orléans and after); Wolfe, *The

## 2. The architecture: aides, taille, gabelle; élus, généraux, généralités

**Second structural finding: the machinery is NOT closable from
Contamine.** The monograph has no chapter on fiscal administration
and no index entries for the three taxes; "généraux des finances"
does not occur. What it attests is the machinery *in use* by the
army:

- **Élections as the assessment frame for military levies.**
  Francs-archers were chosen "dans le cadre de l'élection" (t1 l.
  21163); pioneers and carters were "taxées, dans le cadre financier
  des élections, à un certain nombre d'hommes et de chevaux" — for
  Guyenne 1453, and for Brittany 1489 "on eut recours jusqu'aux
  élections d'Orléans et de Bourges" (t1 ll. 18843–52, print ~pp.
  297–98). Repartition units listed once: "sénéchaussées, diocèses,
  élections, voire parties d'élections" (t1 l. 20296).
- **The three taxes as boundary-markers of privilege.** Franc-archer
  recruits "restaient soumises aux aides et à la gabelle…
  affranchies de la taille, de la taxe des gens d'armes et de
  l'imposition de guet et garde" (t1 ll. 21073–81); the 1480s debate
  on funding infantry from the gabelle foundered partly on nobles
  being "exempts de gabelle" (t1 ll. 21310–14).
- **Pays d'états in action, not in doctrine.** "Les trois états de
  Languedoc" granting aides (t1 ll. 15822–28); "les états du
  Rouergue" ordered to pay the lances monthly (t2 ll. 11980–82);
  "généralités dessus et deça les rivières de Seine et Yonne" once,
  in a Louis XI infantry scheme (t1 l. 21350).

The structure the ch10 memo sketches at §2 — élus and élections
(inherited from the 1350s) subordinated under four généralités (six
by 1484), receveurs below — remains **[UNVERIFIED — RETRIEVAL:
Wolfe; Henneman vol. 2 for the élus' fourteenth-century origins;
Major for pays d'élection vs pays d'états]**. Nothing in repo can
carry it above T3/T4.

## 3. The taille des lances

**Third finding: the PHRASE "taille des lances" does not occur in
Contamine 1972** (both tomes searched). The council synthesis and
the spine use the term; its scholarly carrier must be identified at
retrieval **[UNVERIFIED — RETRIEVAL: Wolfe / Henneman / the
secondary literature the term comes from — do not use the term in a
draft with attribution to Contamine]**. The SUBSTANCE — taille
receipts earmarked to pay the compagnies — is, however, one of the
monograph's best-documented points:

- **The predictable-receipts statement [T2, the probe's most
  load-bearing extract]:** "Si l'ordonnance du roi fut à cet égard
  privilégiée, c'est qu'on pouvait prévoir avec une exactitude
  suffisante, au début de chaque exercice, les recettes nécessaires
  à l'ordinaire des guerres, dont elle dépendait. En d'autres
  termes, **l'infrastructure financière de l'armée permanente fit
  preuve, à partir de 1445, d'une incontestable efficacité**" (t2
  ll. 2902–12, ch. XVI, print pp. 488–530 [~500–02 approx]). Before
  1444: appatis legalised, "ni budget, ni prévisions : l'événement
  immédiat et ses exigences commandaient" (t1 l. 15863).
- **District assignment of lances to fiscal circumscriptions** (ch.
  X fn 31, t1 ll. 19290–19335): "Lances logées (1446–1448)" per
  district — élection de Lyon 30, Basse Auvergne 120, Anjou et
  Maine 140, Poitou 190, Rouergue 80, etc.; 896 lances known for
  1446–49 (t1 ll. 17838–44). The fiscal weight of a province was a
  known quantity: ceding Normandy in 1465 would have cost Louis XI
  "une province qui fournissait la solde et le logement d'environ
  600 lances," so he "imposa au Dauphiné, jusqu'alors privilégié,
  le payement de cent lances fournies" (t1 ll. 17862–68, ~pp.
  282–83).
- **Payment in kind per district, instituted immediately after
  1445** (Annexe IX, print pp. 641–643, t2 ll. 11947–92): Senlis in
  grain/wine/meat per lance; Auvergne's "trois voies" (full
  victuals + 9 francs; part-kind + 21 francs; all-cash 31
  francs/month per lance); Rouergue fixed at 18 l. 7s. 10d.t., then
  from March 1446 "21 L.t. et… le reste — soit 10 Lt. — en vivres";
  the in-kind system quickly abandoned by the contribuables. A
  lance fournie "touchait 30 livres de gages par mois" (Reims 1468;
  t2 ll. 11919–23).

## 4. The ordonnance of 1445 and the compagnies' financing

- **The founding text is lost** (ch10 memo §0.1, confirmed): "la
  célèbre ordonnance perdue de 1445, qui énumérait les chefs de
  guerre retenus par le roi… définissait également la lance fournie
  ou garnie, composée de six hommes et de six chevaux" (t1 ll.
  17715–18); bracketed between the Nancy ordonnance of 9 January
  1445 (Tuetey) and that of **"Louppy-le-Château," 26 May 1445**
  (Cosneau, *Richemont*, app. LXXXIV, pp. 610–12), the phantom act
  placed "fin mars" (fn 4, t1 ll. 19178–83). **Spelling variance
  flag:** Contamine prints "Louppy-le-Château"; the ch10 memo has
  "Louppy-le-Châtel" throughout — check against Cosneau before any
  draft use.
- **Establishment:** narrative sources give 1,500 lances; "Thomas
  Basin précise même qu'elles étaient réparties en quinze compagnies
  d'égale dimension" (t1 ll. 17824–27); an unexecuted 1444 project
  of 13+1 companies/1,400 lances (t1 ll. 17834–38); the actual
  establishment rose "à environ 1 800 lances fournies" (t1 ll.
  17842–44); the 1461 Raguier account counts 1,729 lances under 34
  captains (t1 ll. 17853–56).
- **The reform's setting:** "Lorsqu'en 1445, mettant à profit les
  trêves conclues l'année précédente avec les Anglais, l'entourage
  politique, militaire et financier de Charles VII, réuni à Nancy…
  entreprit… la réforme de l'armée royale, il jeta les bases d'une
  organisation nouvelle qui, dans ses grandes lignes, resta
  inchangée au moins jusqu'au milieu du 16e siècle" (t1 ll.
  17680–84, print ~pp. 277–80). "À partir de 1445, le rôle du
  trésorier des guerres redevint essentiel" (t1 ll. 15984–85); one
  treasurer of wars paid the whole grande ordonnance until Louis XI
  doubled the office in 1476 (t2 ll. 2914–31).
- **The 1445 oath as the constitutional instrument:** captains swore
  to serve the king "envers tous et contre tous, sans nul excepter,
  **soit leur seigneur naturel ou autre quelconque**" (t1 ll.
  24777–79, ch. XIV) — dissolving the adveu system ("tous jours
  advouoient iceulx capitaines quelque adveu de ung des seigneurs de
  France," t1 ll. 16798–16816) that had wrecked the 1439–40 attempt.

## 5. The Praguerie, 1440 — the settlement's civil war

- **Its origins are AT the 1439 moment, in Contamine's own
  narrative:** in February–March 1439 Bourbon "incita secrètement
  les Ecorcheurs à désobéir aux ordres royaux… le duc de Bourbon ne
  se rendit pas à Meaux, mais s'arrêta à Orléans où il trouva
  Dunois, le maréchal de la Fayette, Jacques de Chabannes et
  d'autres seigneurs : **déjà la Praguerie commençait**" (t1 ll.
  16693–706, ~pp. 264–65 [approx]). The revolt was against
  precisely the licensing-and-payment programme — Contamine's
  judgment on the whole 1439–45 obstruction: the obstacle was
  "moins… le manque d'argent que… l'intervention des grands
  seigneurs. **Crise politique, plus encore qu'économique ou
  sociale**" (t1 ll. 16807–12).
- **The constitutional exchange [T2; already partially in memo
  Revisions 14, whose pin is corrected below]:** "Entre 1440 et
  1442, lors de la Praguerie," the princes (Bourbon, René d'Anjou,
  Alençon) asked the king to choose "capitaines convenables et
  suffisans, tant de ceulx du roy comme de mesdiz seigneurs…
  proposant ainsi un partage à l'amiable"; Charles VII answered
  "que « toute la guerre du royaume appartient au roy et a ses
  officiers, et non a autre, et n'est nul si grant audit royaume
  qui puisse ou doive mouvoir guerre ne tenir gens d'armes en
  icelluy sans l'auctorité, commission et mandement du roy »…
  **Mais il n'avait pu imposer ses vues. En 1445 au contraire, la
  monarchie sortait victorieuse de ce conflit**" (t1 ll. 24786–807,
  ch. XIV, print ~pp. 402–03; the adjacent Bourré note is
  index-confirmed print p. 405). **Pin correction:** ch10 memo
  Revisions 14 places this passage at "T1 ~pp. 267–68" — an
  artefact of the reprint's drifting inline markers; it sits in ch.
  XIV (print pp. 399–450).
- **Date-range flag:** Contamine writes "entre 1440 et 1442" —
  wider than the memo §2's "February–July 1440."
- **NOT in Contamine:** the revolt's military course, Richemont's
  campaign, the settlement at Cusset, the dauphin's role. The memo's
  narrative ("outgeneraled by Richemont, settled at Cusset on
  generous terms") keeps its T3 sourcing — **[UNVERIFIED —

## 6. "Jusqu'à la conclusion de la paix" — permanence made by practice

- **The dated act:** "un acte royal, en date du **25 avril 1446**,
  évoque la nécessité d'entretenir, **jusqu'à la conclusion de la
  paix**, 1 500 lances pour la Languedoïl et 500 pour la Languedoc,
  sans compter les troupes établies aux frontières de Normandie et
  de Guienne" (t1 ll. 17831–34, print ~p. 282; source *Hist.
  Languedoc* t. XII, col. 9–12).
- **Provisional in name, definitive in intent:** "pas un instant, il
  ne fut question… de supprimer l'ordonnance du roi ; si, au moment
  de sa création, **on avait tenu à faire croire aux contribuables
  que c'était là une mesure toute provisoire**, en fait… elle avait
  bien dès cette époque, dans l'esprit des conseillers de la
  monarchie, **un caractère quasi définitif**" (t1 ll. 17848–53,
  ~p. 282).
- **Contamine's general law of the mechanism:** "L'extraordinaire
  des guerres désigne des dépenses impromptues ou qu'on juge
  accidentelles. **Mais si l'accidentel se prolonge, si l'impromptu
  devient prévisible, on peut et on doit en prévoir le financement
  régulier et l'extraordinaire se mue alors en ordinaire**" (ch. X
  fn 1, t1 ll. 19161–67).
- **The pattern repeats:** peace of Arras 1482 "n'avait pas été
  suivie de la moindre diminution des effectifs de l'infanterie"
  (t1 ll. 18482–84); the contemporaries' grievance — "Ce qui fait
  problème, voire scandale… c'est que les tailles, les « aides
  ordonnées pour la guerre », ne soient pas entièrement consacrées
  à la sécurité du royaume" (t2 ll. 5264–67, Conclusion); the 1484
  Estates of Tours forced Charles VIII's infantry funding back to
  "la forme traditionnelle de levées temporaires" (t1 ll.
  18488–95) — the control experiment the memo §2 already carries.

## 1. What the Court actually ordered, 15 May 1911

**The standard [T1, verbatim].** The rule of reason: the Sherman Act
"prohibits all contracts and combination which amount to an
unreasonable or undue restraint of trade in interstate commerce"
(syllabus, 221 U.S. 1); the statute left the wrong "to be determined
by the light of reason, guided by the principles of law" (~221 U.S.
63–64).

**The decree affirmed [T1, the Court's recitation, ~221 U.S. 77–78].**
The circuit court "commanded the dissolution of the combination, and
therefore in effect, directed the transfer by the New Jersey
corporation back to the stockholders of the various subsidiary
corporations entitled to the same of the stock which had been turned
over to the New Jersey company in exchange for its stock." Decree § 5
"forbade the New Jersey corporation from in any form or manner
exercising any ownership or exerting any power directly or indirectly
in virtue of its apparent title to the stocks of the subsidiary
corporations"; § 6 enjoined the post-transfer owners "from in any way
conspiring or combining to violate the act… and prohibited all
agreements between the subsidiary corporations or other stockholders
in the future, tending to produce or bring about further violations
of the act." Thirty days was extended to "at least six months" (~221
U.S. 81); the court below retained jurisdiction "to compel compliance
in every respect."

**The structure severed [T1].** Decree § 4 (verbatim in the U.S.
Reports footnote, ~pp. 42–44) tabulates the holding company's ~37
subsidiaries — the state Standard Oils, Buckeye Pipe Line, National
Transit, Union Tank Line, Anglo-American, Waters-Pierce and the rest
— acquired for "more than $90,000,000" of SONJ stock; § 2 names "the
seven individual defendants" (John D. and William Rockefeller,
Rogers, Flagler, Archbold, Payne, Pratt) who "united with the
Standard Oil Company… to form and effectuate this combination."

---

## 2. What the decree did NOT do — the probe's first structural finding

- **No divestiture by the individuals.** Nothing ordered any
  shareholder to sell anything. The shares of the 37 successors went
  back "to the stockholders… entitled to the same" — i.e. pro rata
  to the holding company's owners, Rockefeller first among them.
  (The pro-rata mechanics appear in the opinion via the appellants'
  recitation, ~221 U.S. 10; the circuit court decree's own §§ 5–9
  verbatim text is in 173 F. 177 (C.C.E.D. Mo. 1909), NOT recited in
  full in the opinion — **[UNVERIFIED — RETRIEVAL: 173 F. 177, if a
  verbatim decree paragraph is ever load-bearing]**.) The Court
  construed § 6 "not as depriving the stockholders or the
  corporations… of the power to make normal and lawful contracts or
  agreements, but as restraining them from, by any device whatever,
  recreating directly or indirectly the illegal combination."
- **No operational severance of the physical system.** The Court's
  own illustration, verbatim: "take the pipe lines. By the effect of
  the transfer of the stock the pipe lines would come under the
  control of various corporations instead of being subjected to a
  uniform control. If various corporations owning the lines
  determined in the public interests to so combine as to make a
  continuous line, such agreement or combination would not be
  repugnant to the act…" — and the same for Union Tank Line's
  tank-car pooling. The decree severed the CONTROL STRUCTURE, not
  the infrastructure; coordinated carriage was expressly
  contemplated as lawful.
- **The blanket injunction was struck.** Decree § 7's bar on the
  defendants engaging in interstate commerce pending dissolution
  "should not have been awarded."

**Reading [BOOK'S ARGUMENT, resting on the verbatim record].** The
1911 instrument is precisely a control-rights operation: it
extinguished a private power of unified command over the energy
system (the holding company's ownership powers, § 5) and forbade its
reconstitution (§ 6), while leaving wealth (the shares), assets (the
pipes), and even coordination-in-carriage intact. Spine §8(c)'s
sentence — "the state did not take the oil; it destroyed the
vertical integration that made oil sovereign" — is supported at the
level of control, with one precision: what the decree destroyed was
unified DISCRETION over the whole system, and it transferred that
discretion to no one.

## 3. Where the off-switch went — the migration, dated from open primaries

The answer to spine §8(c)'s question ("after the breakup, who owned
the power to turn off the energy?") is: in 1911, NOBODY — the decree
vacated the private off-switch without creating a public one. The
public one was then built over three decades, by instruments mostly
independent of the antitrust line, one of which PRECEDES the breakup:

distinct forms, none of them created by the breakup itself: (i)
CARRIAGE control (Hepburn 1906; Texas 1917) — the chokepoint
neutralised by common-carrier status, begun before dissolution; (ii)
PRODUCTION control (proration 1930–35, federally enforced by
Connally) — a state commission acquiring exactly the discretion the
trust had lost: how much flows; (iii) TRANSACTIONAL control (TWEA →
EO 8832 → IEEPA/OFAC; EPCA's reserve) — the presidential
license-and-block apparatus. By 1941 the state could do to Japan what
Rockefeller could once do to a refinery district — and no successor
company could do it to anyone.

## 1. The railroads — ICC 1887, Hepburn 1906 [T1 IN CORPUS: 24 Stat. 379; 34 Stat. 584]

**Dependency.** Carrier discretion over rates and access — who ships,
at what price, which town lives. The statute's own targets: "any
special rate, rebate, drawback, or other device" (§2, 24 Stat.
379–80); "undue or unreasonable preference or advantage to any
particular person, company, firm, corporation, or locality" (§3, p.
380).

**Instrument.** Common carriage and rate regulation with a standing
commission — no structural act. §1: all charges "shall be reasonable
and just; and every unjust and unreasonable charge for such service
is prohibited and declared to be unlawful"; §11: "a Commission is
hereby created and established to be known as the Inter-State
Commerce Commission" (p. 383). Approved 4 February 1887. The 1887
text contains no divestiture, dissolution or forfeiture-of-assets
provision (searched; none). The 1887 ICC could prohibit and
investigate; the power to PRESCRIBE came with Hepburn (approved 29
June 1906), amended §15: the Commission "authorized and empowered…
to determine and prescribe what will be the just and reasonable rate
or rates… to be thereafter observed in such case as the maximum to
be charged" (34 Stat., §4), orders in force up to two years. Hepburn
also made oil pipelines common carriers (the off-switch probe's §3
anchor).

**The buried structural rule — the probe's first finding.** Hepburn
carries a genuine line-of-business separation INSIDE the regulatory
statute, the commodities clause: "From and after May first, nineteen
hundred and eight, it shall be unlawful for any railroad company to
transport… any article or commodity, other than timber and the
manufactured products thereof, manufactured, mined, or produced by
it, or under its authority, or which it may own in whole, or in
part, or in which it may have any interest direct or indirect,"
excepting articles for its own carrier use. The carrier may not
carry its own goods — a self-preferencing ban in structural form,
enacted five years before the Standard Oil dissolution.

**Severed / left.** Severed: unilateral tariff discretion, the
rebate, the preference, and (1908) carriage-of-own-commodities.
Left: ownership of the roads, private operation, the revenues.
**Fortune:** not addressed by these statutes; no in-repo T2 speaks
to the railroad fortunes — [UNVERIFIED — RETRIEVAL], not needed.

**Sequence.** Regulation IS the whole case: the series opens with
twenty-four years of carriage regulation before any structural
dissolution anywhere. The lag finding's baseline.

## 3. The Money Trust, 1913–14 [Clayton and FTC Acts T1 IN CORPUS; the Federal Reserve Act NOT — [UNVERIFIED — RETRIEVAL]]

**Dependency.** Coordinated control of credit and corporate
governance through interlocking directorates and a private liquidity
syndicate. The in-corpus T1 evidence for how Congress framed it is
the instrument itself: Clayton §8 bans the shared-governance device
directly.

**Instrument — three legs, none structural.** (i) A public
substitute institution: the Federal Reserve Act (Pub. L. 63-43, 38
Stat. 251, 1913) — its text is NOT in corpus; the claim that it
displaced the private liquidity function is [UNVERIFIED — RETRIEVAL:
FRASER NARA scan, URL in retrieval-master]. (ii) The interlock ban,
verbatim (Clayton Act, approved 15 October 1914): "from and after
two years from the date of the approval of this Act no person shall
at the same time be a director or other officer or employee of more
than one bank, banking association or trust company… [with]
deposits, capital, surplus, and undivided profits aggregating more
than $5,000,000"; and "no person at the same time shall be a
director in any two or more corporations, any one of which has
capital, surplus, and undivided profits aggregating more than
$1,000,000… if such corporations are or shall have been
theretofore, by virtue of their business and location of operation,
competitors" (§8, 38 Stat. 732–33). Alongside it §7: "no corporation
engaged in commerce shall acquire, directly or indirectly, the whole
or any part of the stock or other share capital of another
corporation engaged also in commerce, where the effect of such
acquisition may be to substantially lessen competition" (38 Stat.
731–32) — the stock-acquisition gate DuPont–GM would run through
forty years later. (iii) A standing commission: "unfair methods of
competition in commerce are hereby declared unlawful," the FTC
"empowered and directed to prevent" them by complaint, hearing and
cease-and-desist order (FTC Act §5, 38 Stat. 719–20, approved 26
September 1914). Remedies across both statutes are injunctive and
prospective; nothing transfers property.

**Severed / left.** Severed: the lawful basis of governance-network
coordination — the seat on the rival's board; prospectively, the
stock route to combination. Left: the banks, the partnerships, the
capital. **Fortune:** no in-repo source — [UNVERIFIED — RETRIEVAL].

**Sequence.** No structural act, ever: the republic answered a
control NETWORK with a public institution, a governance rule and a
standing commission. Regulation not preceding structure but
replacing it.

## 4. Glass–Steagall, 1933 [T1 IN CORPUS: Pub. L. 73-66, 48 Stat. 162, FRASER scan]

**Dependency.** The universal bank: "government-supported deposits,
lending relationships, securities promotion, and speculative risk
under one roof" is the playbook's gloss; the statute's own device is
the definition of incompatible combinations.

**Instrument — the line-of-business firebreak, verbatim.** Approved
16 June 1933. §16: dealing in investment securities "limited to
purchasing and selling such securities without recourse, solely upon
the order, and for the account of, customers, and in no case for its
own account, and the association shall not underwrite any issue of
securities" (with US/state/municipal exemptions; effective one year
after approval). §20: "no member bank shall be affiliated… with any
corporation… engaged principally in the issue, flotation,
underwriting, public sale, or distribution… of stocks, bonds,
debentures, notes, or other securities" (after one year; $1,000/day
penalty; forfeiture of charter or Fed membership after six months'
warning). §21: any organisation "engaged in the business of issuing,
underwriting, selling, or distributing… securities" barred from
"the business of receiving deposits" (after one year; criminal
penalty; binds EVERYONE, not just member banks). §32: officer and
director interlocks between member banks and securities firms banned
from 1 January 1934, "unless… there is a permit therefor issued by
the Federal Reserve Board."

**Severed / left.** The extraction's conclusion, verified against
every operative section: "No provision transfers any property or
requires sale to the state… the statute's entire enforcement
apparatus is… definitional restriction… civil per-diem penalties…
forfeiture of federal charter/Fed membership… criminal fine…
prohibition subject to a permit. The state acquires nothing."
Owners choose sides within the one-year windows; the statute never
uses "divest." Severed: the combination. Left: the equity and
either business. **Fortune:** not addressed at T1; [UNVERIFIED —
RETRIEVAL].

**Sequence.** The structural statute operates THROUGH the
pre-existing regulatory membrane: its sanctions are forfeitures of
membership in a system built in 1913, and its escape valve is a
regulator's permit. Supervision precedes; the firebreak is bolted
onto it.

## 8. Paramount, 1948 [OPINION T1 IN CORPUS: 334 U.S. 131, U.S. Reports scan]

**Dependency.** The first-run exhibition bottleneck plus copyright
licensing practices. The Court's figures (at 167): the five majors
held 3,137 of 18,076 theatres (~17%), but "In the 92 cities of the
country with populations over 100,000 at least 70 per cent of all
the first-run theatres are affiliated with one or more of the five
majors… In 38 of those cities there are no independent first-run
theatres." "The main contest is over the cream of the exhibition
business — that of the first-run theatres" (166–67).

**Instrument.** Sherman §§1–2. Practices condemned and enjoined:
horizontal and vertical price-fixing of admission prices (141–44),
unreasonable clearances, pooling agreements ("Clearer restraints of
trade are difficult to imagine," 149–50), joint ownership between
exhibitor-defendants dissolved (149–51), formula deals and master
agreements ("The pooling of the purchasing power of an entire
circuit in bidding for films is a misuse of monopoly power,"
154–55), block-booking ("All we hold to be illegal is a refusal to
license one or more copyrights unless another copyright is
accepted," 159). Vertical integration itself was held NOT per se
illegal (173–74). The Court struck the district court's
competitive-bidding remedy ("involves the judiciary so deeply in
the daily operation of this nation-wide business… it should not be
undertaken," 162) and remanded divestiture under the fruits
standard: the court's "function includes undoing what the
conspiracy achieved… the fruits of the conspiracy which are denied
the independents must also be denied the five majors" (171–72).

**Severed / left — the probe's second finding.** BY THIS TEXT:
practices severed, no assets. The opinion orders no divestiture; the
only consent decree it mentions is the pre-trial decree of 20
November 1940 (142 n.3); the theatre divestitures rest entirely on
the 1948–49 decrees, which are NOT in corpus — [UNVERIFIED —
RETRIEVAL: the Paramount consent decrees]. At this text's close the
majors keep production (monopoly in production "eliminated as an
issue," 166), distribution and the theatres; even the
anti-expansion injunction was vacated for a "fresh start" (175).
**Fortune:** not addressed; [UNVERIFIED — RETRIEVAL].

**Sequence.** A behavioural consent decree (1940) precedes; no
sector regulator ever existed for exhibition; the structural act
post-dates the opinion. Judicial line only — the one verified case
where carriage-style regulation played no part.

## 9. Alcoa, 1945 [OPINION T1 IN CORPUS: 148 F.2d 416, CourtListener text]

**Dependency.** Over ninety per cent of virgin ingot — the primary
input on which all fabricators depended ("They were its market,"
*431) — maintained by pre-emptive capacity expansion.

**Instrument.** Sherman §2, liability from structure plus intent to
hold it. The percentages (at *424): "That percentage is enough to
constitute a monopoly; it is doubtful whether sixty or sixty-four
percent would be enough; and certainly thirty-three per cent is
not." The exclusion holding (at *430–31): "It was not inevitable
that it should always anticipate increases in the demand for ingot
and be prepared to supply them… we can think of no more effective
exclusion than progressively to embrace each new opportunity as it
opened, and to face every newcomer with new capacity already geared
into a great organization." The concession that bounds it (at
*429–30): "monopoly may have been thrust upon it… The successful
competitor, having been urged to compete, must not be turned upon
when he wins."

**Severed / left — the probe's third finding.** BY THIS TEXT:
nothing structural. Hand expressly deferred dissolution:
"Dissolution is not a penalty but a remedy; if the industry will not
need it for its protection, it will be a disservice to break up an
aggregation which has for so long demonstrated its efficiency"
(*446). Relief actually granted: injunctions against the price
squeeze and the 1936 cartel; the prayer that common shareholders of
Alcoa and Aluminium Ltd divest one or the other was DENIED (*447).
The whole of Alcoa stayed in its owners' hands. **Fortune:**
untouched by this text, definitionally.

**Sequence — the series' unique row.** The remedy Hand waited on was
the STATE'S OWN CAPACITY: the Surplus Property Act disposal of the
government's war-built aluminium plants would set "a plan or design
for the industry as a whole," and only if that failed would
dissolution arise — "then, but then only" (*446–47). Public
capacity-building as the structural remedy's substitute: the row in
the pattern where the republic answered a private bottleneck by
having built plant of its own.

## 10. DuPont–GM, 1957 [OPINION T1 IN CORPUS: 353 U.S. 586, U.S. Reports scan]

**Dependency.** A strategic equity link converting ownership into
preferred access: du Pont's 23% of GM (1917–19) and "du Pont's
commanding position as General Motors' supplier of automotive
finishes and fabrics… promoted by its stock interest and… not
gained solely on competitive merit" (605); the stock "purposely
employed… to pry open the General Motors market" (606); Raskob's
1917 Treasurer's Report quoted at 601–02 ("will undoubtedly secure
for us the entire Fabrikoid, Pyralin, paint and varnish business").

**Instrument.** Clayton §7 (pre-1950 text), reaching a
thirty-year-old acquisition: "the Government may proceed at any time
that an acquisition may be said with reasonable probability to
contain a threat" (597–98); "any acquisition by one corporation of
all or any part of the stock of another corporation, competitor or
not, is within the reach of the section" (592).

**Severed / left — the probe's fourth finding.** BY THIS TEXT:
nothing yet. The opinion reverses the dismissal and remands "for a
determination, after further hearing, of the equitable relief
necessary and appropriate" (607–08); the divestiture belongs to the
later remedy proceedings (the 1961 decision — its citation, 366
U.S., appears in corpus only as quoted in *Microsoft*, 253 F.3d 34:
divestiture "has traditionally been the remedy for Sherman Act
violations whose heart is intercorporate combination and control").
The remedy decision itself is NOT in corpus — [UNVERIFIED —
RETRIEVAL]. **Fortune:** not addressed; the instrument targets the
LINK, not the holdings' value — but that reading completes only
with the 1961 text.

**Sequence.** The gate was legislated in 1914 (Clayton §7, in
corpus); the acquisition predates it (1917–19 — a §7 applied to a
pre-existing position through the "any time the acquisition
threatens to ripen" doctrine); suit 1949; liability 1957; structure
later still. The longest regulation-to-structure lag in the series
— four decades.

## 11. AT&T, 1956 and 1982 [MFJ OPINION T1 IN CORPUS: 552 F. Supp. 131; the 1956 decree known only through the MFJ's recitation]

**Dependency.** "The principal means by which AT&T has maintained
monopoly power in telecommunications has been its control of the
Operating Companies with their strategic bottleneck position" (*171)
— every rival's calls originated and terminated on AT&T's local
exchanges.

**Instrument — two stages.** (i) 1956 (as this opinion describes it;
the decree itself NOT in corpus): "an injunction was issued which
precluded AT&T from engaging in any business other than the
provision of common carrier communications services; precluded
Western Electric from manufacturing equipment other than that used
by the Bell System; and required the defendants to license their
patents to all applicants upon the payment of appropriate
royalties" (*137–38); non-exclusive licences for "all existing and
future Bell System patents" (§X, at *178). (ii) 1982: divestiture
of the twenty-two Operating Companies; MFJ §II(D) quarantining the
divested BOCs ("no BOC shall… provide interexchange
telecommunications services or information services; manufacture or
provide telecommunications products or customer premises
equipment…; or provide any other product or service… that is not a
natural monopoly service actually regulated by tariff"); §II(A)
equal access "on an unbundled, tariffed basis, that is equal in
type, quality, and price to that provided to AT&T and its
affiliates."

**Severed / left.** Severed: the bottleneck itself — and by spin-off
to the owners, in the decree's own words (§I(A)(4)): divestiture by
"a spin-off of stock of the separated BOCs to the shareholders of
AT&T," the decree declining to fix the number of successor
entities. Left: Long Lines, Western Electric, Bell Laboratories,
and freedom from the 1956 confinement — "AT&T would be free to
compete in all facets of the marketplace" (*170–71). **Fortune:**
the wealth-preserving mechanism is IN the decree at T1 — the
shareholders receive the successors' stock, exactly the Standard
Oil design. (Subsequent value: not addressed; not needed.)

**Sequence — the lag thesis's fullest confirmation, in the court's
own words.** Rate regulation from the Communications Act framework;
the 1949 suit; the 1956 confinement-and-licensing decree; then (at
*170): "The antitrust suit brought by the Department of Justice in
1949 ended in 1956 with a consent decree which imposed injunctive
relief that was patently inadequate… Future regulatory and
injunctive remedies are unlikely to be more successful than were
similar efforts in the past" — and the FCC's own former Common
Carrier Bureau chiefs testifying "that the Commission is not and
never has been capable of effective enforcement of the laws
governing AT&T's behavior" (*168). Regulation preceded, was tried
for decades, failed on the record, and structure followed. Greene
also supplies the book's lineage sentence, quoting Senator Sherman
(21 Cong. Rec. 2457 (1890)): "If we will not endure a king as a
political power we should not endure a king over the production,
transportation, and sale of any of the necessaries of life" — the
over-mighty citizen, in the instrument's own legislative record.

**Playbook checks.** (i) "The 1984 reorganization created seven
Regional Bell Operating Companies" — the SEVEN was AT&T's
reorganisation plan under the decree, not a decree term: §I(A)(4)
expressly provides "nothing in this Modification of Final Judgment
shall require or prohibit the consolidation of the ownership of the
BOCs into any particular number of entities"; the opinion records
AT&T's plan for seven (n.41; *201). (ii) The playbook's "confined
AT&T largely to regulated communications and government work": the
"government work" element is NOT in this opinion's summary of the
1956 decree — [UNVERIFIED — RETRIEVAL: the 1956 decree text] before
any draft carries it.

## 13. Microsoft, 2001–02 [T1 IN CORPUS: 253 F.3d 34; Final Judgment, D.D.C., 12 Nov 2002]

**Dependency.** The OEM distribution channel and the middleware/API
layer protecting the operating-system monopoly (>95% share; the
applications barrier to entry — liability "in its entirety" on
monopoly power, 253 F.3d at *51).

**Instrument.** Litigated Sherman §2 monopoly-maintenance liability,
affirmed (*46); the district court's two-way BREAKUP VACATED for
three stated reasons — no evidentiary hearing, inadequate reasons,
"we have drastically altered the scope of Microsoft's liability"
(*107) — with the structural-remedy caution ("divestiture is a
remedy that is imposed only with great caution," *80) and the tempo
warning (*49): "six years seems like an eternity in the computer
industry. By the time a court can assess liability, firms,
products, and the marketplace are likely to have changed
dramatically." Then the 2002 consent Final Judgment, conduct only:
§III.E compulsory communications-protocol licensing "on reasonable
and non-discriminatory terms"; §III.D API disclosure; §III.H
end-user/OEM freedom to remove Microsoft middleware and designate
rivals' defaults; §§III.A/F anti-retaliation; §III.G
exclusive-dealing ban; §IV.B a three-expert Technical Committee; §V
a five-year term. The extraction confirms: "no divestiture,
break-up, spin-off, or structural reorganisation provision anywhere
in the Final Judgment."

**Severed / left.** Severed: the closed interface and the
distribution chokehold — temporarily (five years) and by conduct
rule, not by ownership change. Left: Windows whole, the company
intact, the fortune untouched. **Fortune:** not addressed at T1;
[UNVERIFIED — RETRIEVAL] — though the decree's non-structural form
makes the inference trivial, the column stays honest.

**Sequence — the counter-lag case.** Structure was attempted FIRST
(the 2000 breakup order) and abandoned at the threshold; conduct
remedies replaced it. Where AT&T's court reached structure after
regulation demonstrably failed, Microsoft's court retreated from
structure to conduct because the market's tempo outran the
litigation's. The one verified case where the republic blinked at
the structural act — load-bearing for the Parrott engagement
(critiques.md, this date).

### The passages as they now read (located by the terms the note names — the draft carries no in-text marker)

The developed castle of high-medieval Europe was optimised against the
two threats its builders knew: the man on the ladder and the stone-
throwing engine. Against both, height was the governing virtue — a tall
wall defeated escalade and out-ranged the trebuchet's flat trajectories —
and height imposed thinness, because a wall both tall and thick was
ruinously expensive in stone and labour. "Tall and thin" was therefore
not a defect but a design, and for three centuries it worked. The
besieger's realistic options against a competently held first-rate
fortress reduced, in practice, to one: starvation. As English
Heritage's survey of siege practice puts it, "medieval sieges were
settled more often by treachery, starvation, or disease than by
breached walls" [GAP: T3 attribution; upgrade the siege-survey
authority to scholarly level before print]; the type-case, Kenilworth
in 1266, took roughly six months of blockade.
Investment on that timescale was itself a fiscal and political feat —
the besieging army had to be fed, paid and held together longer than the
garrison's stores lasted, while disease worked on the camp and relief
worked on the calendar.



Harfleur was where the English war in Normandy had begun. In August and
September 1415 Henry V besieged it for some five weeks — by the usual
account the first major English siege in which gunpowder artillery
played a serious part, and hard-won at that; the
town's resistance cost him a sick army and shaped the desperate march
that ended, by accident, at Agincourt. When the English themselves had
to retake Harfleur in 1440, the operation took somewhere between three
and six months, depending on which chronicler one credits (Rogers 1993:
fn 103). In December 1449 the Bureau train arrived before the same
town, emplaced sixteen large bombards, and battered it for seventeen
days, whereupon the garrison made terms; the Somerset list dates the
surrender "the first day of the year," 1 January 1450 (Rogers 1993:
265–66, citing Chartier ii. 178–79; Stevenson 1864: II.ii). Five weeks
against the town's stores and disease in 1415; a season or more in
1440; seventeen days of bombardment in 1449. Same walls. The
seventeen-day figure, note, stands without any storming
counter-factual: the town fell to the guns and the treaty, and to
nothing else. (An earlier state of this draft placed a failed French
escalade here on the Berry Herald's authority; first-hand reading of
Escouchy has since shown that scene — Talbot in person, banner beside
him, driving the attackers from the wall — to belong to Rouen's
repelled assault of 16 October, where this draft now records it; the
reattribution is logged in the evidence file, and the census point
survives at Rouen: storming was tried, failed, and the city fell by
composition.)



The second says: Normandy fell politically — the gates were opened from
inside. Curry again: "many surrenders were forced on the English by the
local populations… the fall of the major places led to a domino effect"
(Curry 2010: 187). Substantially conceded — and absorbed, because the
reputational mechanism *requires* a transmission belt. A threat
collapses resistance only if the threatened believe it, and the belief
ran through Norman townsmen who could do the arithmetic as well as any
captain: why endure a bombardment, and risk the sack that the law of
war licensed after a breach, for a garrison that could not hold once
the guns arrived? The politics and the ballistics were not rival
causes; the politics was the medium through which the ballistics
propagated. And the campaign itself supplies the discriminating test.
If popular disaffection were the sufficient cause, the reconquest
should have stalled where the population was loyal. In 1451 the train
moved into Gascony — English for three centuries, and, in Rogers's
words, "despite the deeply ingrained pro-English sympathies of the
inhabitants," all of Guienne fell rapidly (Rogers 1993: 266). Fronsac,
"la clef de la Guyenne," the strongest English fortress in the duchy,
surrendered by composition on 15 June 1451 without an assault; Blaye
went in five days; Bordeaux itself treated on 12 June and received
Dunois's army — Jean Bureau riding in the entry — on 30 June, seven
thousand three hundred and twenty French troops in the procession
(Vale 1969, citing Escouchy i. 356–58). The texture of the Gascon
capitulations is worth a moment, because it shows the composition
system operating as a market whose prices everyone now knew. Blaye
and Bourg — "the keys to Bordeaux," in the contemporary judgement
Vale reports — "surrendered cravenly to Dunois," and the terms their
defenders extracted included pensions from Charles VII for, among
others, Gadifer Shorthose, the *English* mayor of Bordeaux, with his
sub-mayor and three jurats (Vale 1969). Bayonne, the second city of
the English south-west, held two weeks in August. The first Bordeaux
settlement itself was studiedly generous — privileges confirmed, the
city spared the taxes of the French interior, judicial and minting
concessions in the tradition's account [GAP: the 1451 terms are
carried at tertiary level; upgrade to Vale's *English Gascony* when
retrieved] — because in 1451 the crown was buying a province's
acquiescence, and knew the difference between a price worth paying
and a precedent worth setting. Loyal populations, hostile
politics, same cascade — faster, if anything. And the counter-
experiment ran the following year: in October 1452 Talbot appeared
before Bordeaux with no siege train at all, the city rose for its old
allegiance, and a gate was opened to him on 20 October. Politics
alone, it turns out, could open gates. What politics alone could not
do — as 1453 would demonstrate — was hold walls against the train.
The two Bordeaux reversals, twenty months apart, are as close to a
controlled experiment as the fifteenth century offers: the political
variable operating without the train in 1452, the train operating
against the political variable in 1449–51 and 1453. Each fell to the
other's weapon exactly once.



The defenders "afusterrent leur artillerie au lez devers dont véoient
venir ledit Talbot" — trained their guns on the side from which they
saw him coming [TRANS. CLAUDE] (Escouchy, ii. 39). The Anglo-Gascon
army attacked the park frontally, banners forward, trumpets sounding,
the men crying "Talbot! Talbot! Saint-George!" — a feudal host doing
the bravest thing it knew how to do, into massed artillery firing from
prepared positions at close range. It was destroyed in an afternoon.
The assault was not
instantly hopeless — Pollard's reconstruction has the attackers
establish themselves "for a moment on the ramparts," where the
standard-bearer fell planting the standard — and then the
counter-attack broke them. Talbot's horse was brought down; he was
killed on the ground, his skull, in Pollard's account, smashed by an
axe — the weapon known, the hand anonymous (Pollard, ch. 1, p. 1);
his son Lord Lisle died with him. The town of Castillon
surrendered two days later under the continuing fire. What died on the
field is easy to name and easier to over-name: not chivalry, which had
been dying of other causes for a century, but the assumption that
valour could still, on its own, carry a decision. The last great
captain of the old
war charged the first great instrument of the new one, on the advice
of a false report and against the advice of a true one, and the
instrument did not care.



The chapter's answer is to concede the premise and keep the
conclusion. The infantry revolution was real, and it was
constitutionally *dispersive* — which is exactly why Part I's medieval
order looked as it did. Disciplined infantry could be raised by
anybody with a valley or a town: the Swiss cantons raised it, the
English indentured retinue packaged it, the Italian *condottieri*
companies sold it on the open market — this is Chapters 3 and 4's
material, and it is precisely the military underpinning of the
dispersal the book began with. A capability any subject can raise
does not concentrate sovereignty. The infantry revolution is
therefore not a rival explanation of the consolidation; it is part of
what the consolidation had to overcome — the problem to which the
artillery revolution was the answer. The distinction that carries the
argument is affordability, and the 1442 accounts have already given
it a number: a first-rate siege train cost more than a government's
entire traditional arsenal, and the cost recurred — powder, wages,
carriage, foundry — in a way no campaign-season improvisation could
meet. Infantry was a capability; the train was an *establishment*. And
here the objection folds into testimony, because Rogers himself
supplies the constitutional consequence: the central governments of
large states could afford siege trains; "their subjects and smaller
neighbors, in general, could not" — and the governments that acquired
them suppressed regional powers, raised taxes, and expanded the trains
further, in a cycle he describes and this book merely names (Rogers
1993). The disagreement that remains is a ranking of revolutions —
which movement of the symphony matters most — and the chapter declines
the contest, because it does not need to win it. It needs only the
narrower claim its sternest witness has already granted: that the
artillery revolution was the step which put the price of decisive
force above what any subject could pay. (One apparent counter-example
— the magnificent Burgundian train of Charles the Bold — is deferred
to Chapter 10, where it belongs; the short answer is that Burgundy
was not a subject but a rival consolidator, and that its train did
not save it.)



What the tempo teaches is the chapter's bequest to the rest of the
book. The reconquest looked, to those inside it, like weather — a
cascade with no author. But the cascade had a cause with a budget
line. The train that collapsed the time value of fortification was
not deployable by great magnates, because great magnates could not
afford it; it presupposed the *taille* levied on a kingdom and a
salaried technical corps to serve it — fiscal and administrative
capacities that had existed before the guns and had not, before the
guns, been decisive. Artillery made them decisive. The contemporaries
saw the coupling plainly enough: Charles VII, in DeVries's summary of
the reconquest's finance, "increased the royal budget to procure a
larger number of new guns, and he added heavier new taxes to his
kingdom's inhabitants to pay for this increase" (DeVries 1998: 132) —
guns requiring taxes, taxes requiring the administration to assess
and collect them, the administration protected by the guns. Rogers
describes the same figure as a cycle: governments that acquired
artillery suppressed regional powers, raised revenue from the
territory thereby subdued, and spent the revenue expanding the train
and the army that marched with it (Rogers 1993). A flywheel of this
kind, once spinning, does not stop at a frontier, and Chapter 10
will follow it into the institutions it built. That inversion —
the moment at which the sinews of administration became the sinews of
war, rather than the reverse — is Chapter 10's subject, and the
reader now holds its premise: after 1450, sovereignty had a minimum
price, denominated in guns, wages and taxes, and the only entities
that could pay it were the ones we would shortly begin calling
states.

## E1. coda/draft.md

# Coda — A Note on Method and Sources

*DRAFT (first draft, 2026-08-02) — synthesised from the completed and
reviewed chapters per the brief (no new research); drafted last, on
Roderick's instruction, with the authorised disclosure sentence and the
falsifiability-narrowing delta table as method material. ~4,000-word
target.*

---

A book that predicts deserves to have its machinery inspected, and
this note exists so that the inspection need not be forensic. It
states what kind of claim the book makes, how the evidence was
tiered, what rules governed the comparisons, what the book does not
claim, and where the argument's founder was right and is respectfully
departed from.

## I. Mechanism under conditions, not determinism

The book's causal claim has a precise logical form, and everything
turns on it: a *mechanism under conditions*, not a law of history.
The mechanism — capital-intensification of decisive force compelling
permanent finance, permanent finance compelling administrative
capacity, administrative capacity suppressing intermediate
authorities — operated once, in the documented case Part II
reconstructs. The book's wager is that its enabling conditions have
recurred: a decisive capability whose price sits above what any
subject can pay; polities with latent fiscal-administrative capacity
to mobilise; and exogenous pressure sufficient to force the
mobilisation. Where the conditions fail, the mechanism idles — the
book's own counter-cases are the demonstration, and they were chosen
for that work: the Swiss won the battles and built no state, because
pike squares compelled no institutions; the Empire asserted the
monopoly of justice without the fiscal substrate and enforced it a
century late; Burgundy built the apparatus and lost the continuous
political centre, and the apparatus passed to its neighbours; England
held the gun monopoly without the fisc and lost both. One mechanism,
several failure modes, one completed run — that is the shape of a
conditional claim, and it is why the predictions of Part III are
conditional too: *if* the stacks continue to concentrate, *if* the
blocs sustain the fiscal effort, *then* the settlement pattern
follows. History does not repeat on command. Prices, however, are
facts, and the book's core empirical assertion — that the price of
decisive capability has moved beyond subordinate reach — is checkable
at any time against public budgets.

## II. The comparative register, stated as rule

No single-polity argument appears anywhere in the book; the rule was
doctrine before a word was drafted, and it is auditable. Every
English-leaning chapter carries at least two continental comparators:
Henry VI is read beside Charles VI and the Ottoman succession crises;
Cade beside the Praguerie, the Ciompi legacy and the German risings;
the Pastons beside the *Fehde* and the vendetta; the artillery state
is French, with Burgundy and the Swiss as counter-cases and England
demoted to the control experiment; the new monarchies run four
polities with the Ottomans included precisely because the older "New
Monarchy" literature excluded them. Appendix B tabulates the register
chapter by chapter. The rule's purpose is not decorative breadth. It
is the book's defence against its own convenience: a mechanism that
only worked in one kingdom would be a national history wearing a
theory; a mechanism whose failure modes appear exactly where its
conditions fail is a mechanism.

## III. The three-tier sourcing discipline

The book runs on three distinct bodies of evidence, and they were
never allowed to vouch for one another.

The *neomedieval spine* — Bull, Friedrichs, Cerny, Kobrin, Slaughter,
Kaplan, Williams, McFate — was read as a literature being extended,
and quoted from its own texts wherever they were in hand, with every
second-hand quotation named as such — and retired when the primary
arrived: Bull's caution, carried through named intermediaries for
the whole research phase with one word flagged as varying by
printing, was re-quoted direct from the fourth edition when the
book reached the corpus, and the variant proved to be two adjacent
sentences on one page. The *medieval historiography* was held to the hardest
rule in the book: no gated work cited as if consulted; every claim
graded by tier; omission preferred to invention. Where the research
corrected the project's own working assumptions, the corrections are
in the text: the "sixty fortified places" of the early outline
became Rogers's sourced one hundred strongpoints with the
surrender-not-siege caveat that improved the argument; the Bureau
brothers were demoted from inventors to institutionalisers, which
strengthened the thesis; Formigny was downgraded from decision to
provocation; the drone-share figures of the last chapters are kept
as three separate measurements because that is what they are. And
the *contemporary record* was held to primary documents — statutes,
regulations, court filings, proxy statements, budget lines, the
institutions' own annual reports — with commentary as framing,
never as load-bearing support; every live episode is drafted as
dated events, attributed, and flagged for re-verification at press.
Two special disciplines ran across all three tiers. Foreign-language
sources are quoted verbatim in the original, with translations
either the scholarly edition's or marked as the pipeline's own for
checking. And a recurring evidentiary pattern was promoted, once
noticed, into the book's method: at every contested joint of the
medieval mechanism, the argument rests on the *sceptics'* own
concessions — Rogers's spiral, DeVries's conforming France and
Burgundy, Curry's state-only threshold, Cerny's fifteenth-century
attribution — so that what remains in dispute with the specialists
is ranking, tempo and sequence, never direction.

One disclosure belongs in this note as a matter of the same
discipline. The research and drafting pipeline for this book used
AI models, including Anthropic's; the Anthropic–Pentagon episode
discussed in Chapters 3 and 11 entered the book on documentary
criteria — government releases and docket entries — and carries the
standard live-event flag, like every other contemporary episode.
The reader should weigh the book's treatment of that episode with
the disclosure in view; the sourcing rules above were built so that
nothing in the treatment depends on trust.

## IV. The wager's narrowing — a delta table

The falsifiability passage in the Introduction is not the wager the
project began with; it is the wager the research left standing. The
narrowing was always in one direction — each condition tightened,
none loosened — and the method essay is the place to show the
deltas, because the direction of revision is itself evidence of how
the book was built.

| # | The outline's original condition | The narrowed condition as published | What forced the narrowing |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Cheap distributed autonomy defeats concentrated capital | A full-stack power defeated by cheap distributed systems *fielded without a patron's intelligence, connectivity and interceptor layers behind them* | The Ukraine record: the March 2025 suspension week showed the cheap layer aiming through a patron's decisive layer (ch12) |
| 2 | Privacy-preserving systems become the default | Bloc-internal fiscal and reporting reach *falls in fact*; the offshore untaxed share *rises again*; plural privacy-default stacks displace bloc stacks *as the operative default* | The legibility record: the offshore stock persists while the untaxed fraction collapsed threefold — the honest baseline required a sharper falsifier (ch08) |
| 3 | Platforms consolidate independence | An actor with decisive-relevant capability and a majority-commercial fiscal base escapes the state's instruments *and resolves its disputes with states in fora states do not control* | The legal history: plural fora proved parasitic on state law; forum-migration is the discriminating test (chs. 8, 11) |
| 4 | No identifiable Tudor moves | The five moves decomposed into scoreable forms across all four candidates, *plus* the Depreter limb: canon alone consolidating a bloc would falsify the mechanism even while producing a settlement look-alike | The comparative work (ch11) and the Burgundian accounts (ch10): apparatus without a continuous centre fails — so the EU case must be able to cut against the book |

A wager that tightens itself under research is behaving as a wager
should; a wager that loosens is becoming unfalsifiable. The reader
is invited to hold the book to the published version and to none
softer.

## V. What the book does not claim

Four disclaimers, stated flatly because each has been earned by a
chapter. The book does not predict specific events — no dates for
settlements, no named winners of elections or wars; its unit of
prediction is the pattern and its horizon is fifteen years of named
indicators. It does not claim any polity's success: the American
candidacy is called probable and half-conscious, the Chinese
substantially executed but exposed at the summit the book's own
two-bodies chapter diagnoses, the European conditional on a fiscal
substrate that does not yet exist, the Indian possible; all four
could fail, and condition four of the wager scores exactly that. It
does not claim the settlement is desirable: the Tudor turn, where
it ran, ran through bonds, expulsions, dissolutions and a harvest
of heads, and its modern forms — whatever their legality — will
concentrate power in ways the book's readers may reasonably dread;
the book predicts, and declines to bless. And it does not claim
that history repeats: it claims that a mechanism has recurred, and
it stakes the claim on measurements.

## VI. The debt to Bull, and the precise disagreement

The debt first, because it is total: without the tenth chapter of
*The Anarchical Society* there is no literature for this book to
extend, and without Bull's own scepticism there is no model for how
to hold a bold hypothesis honestly. Bull did the two things this
book has tried to imitate — he specified the alternative to the
states system with enough precision to test, and he then declined
to claim it on the evidence of his decade. The disagreement is
correspondingly precise. It is not about the diagnosis: on the
evidence assembled in Part I, Bull's five trends have matured past
his own threshold, and the book's first chapter argues that his
caution, right in 1977, would be wrong now. It is about the
*stability* of the condition the diagnosis names. Bull entertained
the new medievalism as a possible successor system — a durable
alternative form of universal political organisation. The book's
history says otherwise: the first medieval order was durable for
centuries precisely because no actor could pay the price of ending
it, and it ended within two generations of that price becoming
payable. A dispersal is not a settlement; it is what a settlement
looks like before the bill for decisive force arrives. Where Bull
saw a possible destination, this book sees a waypoint with a
well-documented exit — and it has tried to honour him in the only
way that matters, by saying exactly what evidence would prove the
dispersal stable after all, and publishing the list.

# Bratton and the book — how *The Stack* (2015) and "Planetary Computation's Next Phase" (2026) interact with the thesis

## 1. The council misdescribed Bratton, and the correction helps the book

The Model Council Synthesis (research/model-council-synthesis.md) states that Bratton's rival prediction is "that sovereignty migrates to the stack rather than the state reabsorbing it." The book does not say that. It disclaims it twice in almost identical words: "The implication is not another prophecy of the declining state withering away into the realm of pure network, but to the contrary: the state's ongoing redefinition is now undertaken in relation to network geographies that it can neither contain nor be contained by" [scan p.22; repeated scan p.144]. His stated position is mutual and unresolved: "As Cloud platforms absorb state functions and states realize Cloud platform topologies and methods, The Stack does not necessarily privilege either in favor of the other" [scan p.304]; "It is less that Cloud platforms ultimately replace states than that two domains become dramatically less distinct from one another, interlacing and folding up in new ways, producing emergent institutional forms not reducible to the direct combination of the two" [scan p.150]; "states respond forcefully, sometimes by attempting to directly compete with these kinds of sovereignty and sometimes by absorbing them as their own new norms" [scan p.145]; "the state pushback against today's version of planetary-scale computation should not be seen as extrinsic, or even contradictory, to the logics of Stack geopolitics" [scan p.165]; "The state takes on the armature of a machine, because the machine, The Stack, has already taken on the roles and register of the state" [scan p.44].

Consequence: Bratton is a hybrid-settlement theorist a decade before the council recommended one. Spine §8(b) — the settlement stated per bloc as a hybrid — is common ground with him, not a concession. Ch12 should cite him as an ally on the *form* of the outcome and a rival on its *cause* and *direction*.

## 2. Where he hands the book its own argument

**The Middle-Ages frame is his.** The Cloud layer opens with Kojève (1945): "a decisive turning point in history, comparable to the one that took place at the end of the Middle Ages… the progressive elimination of 'feudal' political formations… to the benefit of kingdoms… At present, it is these nation-States which, irresistibly, are gradually giving way to political formations which transgress national borders and which could be designated with the term 'Empires'… The modern State is only truly a State if it is an Empire" [scan p.141]. The Introduction lists "medieval returns" among the symptoms of the present "overbordering" [scan p.19]. The consolidation-at-bloc-scale arc is the epigraph he chose.

**The taille and the off-switch, stated from the platform side.** "The practical locus of governmental control is over the capacity to structure, police, and, especially, tax flows: flows of people, money, data, energy. Through the control of the interfaces of input and output, be that a port city, a great firewall, or a superjurisdictional seizure, the taxation of flows is both the means by which states execute the right to exact capital and the result of sovereignty" [scan p.164–165]. This is the revocation metric (GPT 5.6 Sol) and the playbook's "power switch test" before either existed; it should be quoted in ch12 as the point of contact.

**China as the state that keeps the riverbed.** The Sino-Google War is "a conflict… over the predominance of two different modes of sovereignty… a state that would dominate and determine the network sovereignty of information and energy flows versus a platform that would… exceed, in deed if not letter, the last-instance sovereignty of the state" [scan p.143]; Fang Binxing: "Water has no nationality, but riverbeds are sovereign territories" [scan p.143]. Spine §5's "China closest to current Tudor norms" has its 2015 witness.

**Capital intensity and concentration, without the conclusion.** Cloud economics "predicated on efficiencies of centralization" produce "the rapid centripetal centralization of information toward a small number of global Cloud platforms capable of supporting and leveraging service infrastructures at such a massive scale" [scan p.146]; the platforms' future as "quasi-independent geopolitical domains may be determined to a significant degree by their ability to deploy their own competitively effective, end-to-end cyberinfrastructures" [scan p.148]; data centres under mountains beside dams, the energy appetite of the next billions of users and objects, oceanic data centres [scan pp.144–146]. He documents the concentration the book's mechanism requires — few chokepoints, so an off-switch is possible — and stops at "sovereignty is not just made about infrastructural lines; it is made by infrastructural lines" [scan p.41]. The book asks who can pay for the lines.

**The state as stack-builder.** "Even the US Department of Defense are Stack theorists, having made their own version of a Stack model of planetary-scale computation in semisecrecy" [scan p.284; also p.347].

**Cartography and citizenship.** Google Maps shifting the Nicaragua–Costa Rica line (2010) and Google recognising Palestine ahead of the UN [scan p.148]; platforms "assumed and absorbed several core political functions of the modern state" [scan p.148]; the challenge to "the state's monopoly on legitimate violence… but also its monopoly on legitimate citizenships" [scan p.150]; "Clouds become de facto states" [scan p.149]. For ch02 (the New Church) this is the sharpest contemporary statement of a universalist institution assuming sovereign functions.

## 3. The 2026 piece: Bratton has moved toward the book

"Planetary Computation's Next Phase" (5 Feb 2026) adds four things. (a) The four blocs, named: "The term I coined for this fragmentation is Hemispherical Stacks: a China Stack, a US Stack, an India Stack, an EU Stack, and so forth" — the book's four polities, India included, from the opposite direction. (b) Geopolitics and computation are one thing: "the multipolarization of geopolitics over the last decade and the multipolarization of planetary computation over the same period not only track one another; they are the same thing. To build a Stack is to build a society and vice versa, a truth that drives the new adversarial dynamics of world affairs over the competitive access to foundational Stack capacities." (c) Re-subordination observed: "an increasing encapsulation of global platforms within the expanded borders of their host hemisphere: service withdrawals, app bans, deeper, direct connections with the state… caused by a fundamental shift in the basic relations between data and sovereignty"; AI "is already identified as a primary domain of state security." (d) The Earth layer as contest: "competition between China and the US for access to the minerals and compounds necessary for Stack construction has moved from an obscure policy discourse to the front pages"; platforms "secure as much electricity as possible." Against all of this he keeps the hybrid: private enterprises "may operate more independently," and the machinations are "uncertain."

So by 2026 Bratton describes the dispersal (functional governance "infused into the operations of those technical systems at the expense of legal and legislative institutions") and the encapsulation within blocs with "direct connections with the state" in the same paragraph — the neomedieval condition and the beginning of its reversal — and still supplies no mechanism that decides between them. That is the book's opening.

## 4. The genuine disagreements, to engage rather than smooth

1. **No teleology versus a compelled outcome.** "The Stack suggests no teleology or necessary outcome, and the specter of Cloud feudalism becoming the normal twenty-first-century macroeconomics is a real possibility" [scan p.165]; "Who knows? They may get what they want, but they may not want what they get" [scan p.293]. His Cloud feudalism (enclaves, exit over voice, Srinivasan's "Ultimate Exit," Hanson's trillionaires — "psychotic, incestuous autocracies" [scan p.306]) is the book's dispersal seen from inside the platforms; his Kojèvian Empire is the book's consolidation. He declines to choose. The book's claim against him is not that he is wrong about the present but that the price of decisive force decides the choice — and spine §7's tense (compelled, not accomplished) is exactly the register in which to say so.
