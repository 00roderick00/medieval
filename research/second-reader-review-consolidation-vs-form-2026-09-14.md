# Second-reader review, 14 September 2026 — consolidation of power v. the constitutional form it produces (verbatim)

The central intellectual change I recommend is to distinguish the consolidation of power from the constitutional form that consolidation produces.

Your pendulum remains useful: dispersed authority generates pressures towards renewed coordination and command. But a movement towards consolidation need not restore a previous arrangement. It can incorporate the institutions, technologies and expectations accumulated during the period of dispersal.

That gives the book a stronger proposition: the medieval analogy helps explain why independent powers become subordinate; it does not settle who ultimately controls the resulting state.

The corpus suggests the following priorities for the original draft.

1. Specify precisely what the tech barons lose—and what they retain.
The strongest version of the argument concerns the transfer of particular powers: the ability to refuse strategic demands, control access to essential infrastructure, determine succession, withhold operational knowledge or set binding rules independently.
Their wealth, social standing and influence may survive. Indeed, they might become wealthier under a settlement that guarantees contracts and protects incumbents.
Chastellain provides an excellent new historical scene: the disposition of Alençon's interests allows the crown to retain artillery and certain fortifications while preserving other family interests and postponing execution. The specificity matters. The crown acquires instruments of independent action without erasing the entire magnate. See Œuvres, actual tome III, printed pp. 484–487.
This should sharpen Chapters 11–12. "Consolidating the barons" becomes a concrete change in their discretion, rather than an ambiguous prediction of nationalisation.

2. Use composite monarchy to give the American prediction its own historical foundation.
Spain already appears in the original. It should do more than confirm another version of the same sequence.
Elliott's account shows formidable power organised around a fiscal core alongside persistent differences in laws, jurisdictions, representative institutions and obligations. Those differences could endure for generations. They were part of the settlement itself.
This offers a better way to imagine American consolidation: strong federal command in selected strategic domains, private ownership, state-level resistance, judicial boundaries and substantial differences between sectors.
The comparison is institutional, not a claim that America is Spain. It allows you to predict consolidation through an enduring plural arrangement, rather than treating every American complication as a delay before a more uniform destination.

3. Make indispensable intermediaries central to the mechanism.
The most valuable new episode may be Chastellain's account of the Lombard lenders. The duke punishes them but eventually restores them because their services are publicly necessary. Coercive superiority does not supply an immediate replacement for their function. See tome III, printed pp. 315–316.
That is a powerful historical foundation for the relationship between government and technology firms. A government might compel cooperation while remaining dependent on a company's engineers, infrastructure and accumulated knowledge.
The resulting bargain could be durable. It could also leave different kinds of power on different sides: the state holds legal coercion; the firm retains operational indispensability.
Chapters 10 and 12 should distinguish command, dependence and capture. They can coexist, but they are not interchangeable.

4. Deepen Talbot by making his competence part of the tragedy.
The original already contains a substantial, qualified Talbot argument. We should preserve it rather than behave as though all its nuance arrived with my draft.
Barker's account of Talbot's artillery at Dieppe in 1442–43 is particularly useful. Talbot knew guns and employed them. His predicament becomes more interesting when it concerns the limits of an experienced commander's methods against an opponent's sustained capacity to finance, supply and replace forces.
The hypothesis that he sought to strike the gun position remains worth developing. The alleged Caen plot to destroy French artillery shows that attacking the train was an intelligible strategy; it does not prove Talbot's intentions at Castillon. That distinction should stay visible.
His narrative can carry a deeper question: what does an intelligent participant do when the system that made him effective no longer provides sufficient means to prevail? That is much richer than a story about failing to recognise a new invention.

5. Let "the walls and the minds of the besieged" become a recurring problem of knowledge.
The quotation already appears in the original. Its prominence and interpretation should grow:
"The walls and the minds of the besieged"
Moenia et clausorum animos
Blondel's passage concerns Harfleur in December 1449, not Talbot at Castillon: De reductione Normanniae, in Stevenson's Narratives, printed p. 152.
A wall embodies an expectation about how the world works: endurance buys time; relief may arrive; the attacker may exhaust his resources; familiar bargaining terms will remain available. When the material balance changes, those expectations may outlive their foundations.
The same applies to constitutional expectations. People can continue looking towards familiar offices for powers that have gradually migrated elsewhere.
But the passage should also discipline the author. People who reject our prediction are not therefore mentally besieged. Some defenders understood their changed circumstances and negotiated intelligently. The book must distinguish inherited assumptions from rational disagreement.

6. Strengthen the causal explanation by allowing the historical cases to remain untidy.
The original's references to "controlled" siege comparisons overstate what the evidence can establish. Relief prospects, food, allegiance, disease, payments and political negotiations vary alongside artillery.
The better argument concerns an organised system that can make continued resistance unaffordable. Hall's emphasis on management and logistics helps here; so do cases where impressive artillery fails because other requirements are missing.
Two related corrections matter: distinguish the cost of a weapon from the cost of maintaining an establishment, and distinguish planned expenditure from money actually collected and spent.
These changes would make the book more robust because the mechanism becomes capable of explaining both success and failure.

7. Give constitutional bargaining and popular agency more weight.
The original sometimes moves too quickly from institutional difficulty to institutional exhaustion. Its treatment of regency needs to distinguish Henry VI's successful minority arrangements from the different problems created by an adult king's incapacity and uncertain recovery.
Similarly, Cade, towns, assemblies, creditors and organised workers should have consequences for the settlement. Their role cannot end with resistance that the central mechanism eventually overrides.
This is essential to your new prediction. If democratic control can become ornamental, the book must also explain how citizens might prevent that outcome. The historical actors provide the means: bargaining over revenue, withholding cooperation, organising collectively and insisting on enforceable conditions.

8. Make the forecast more demanding without surrendering its direction.
The original already has a substantial prediction apparatus. It needs better scoring, not replacement with generic scenarios.
Assess the United States, China, Europe and India separately. Distinguish enacted authority from demonstrated compliance, compliance from operating capacity, and operating capacity from democratic accountability. A failed American prediction should remain a failure even if consolidation proceeds elsewhere.
The original phrase that objections "can only refine" the thesis should go. So should any rule that requires the whole forecast to fail everywhere before confidence changes.
Chastellain's failed predictions of royal deaths offer an unusually good historical passage here: forecasts survive by changing their meaning after events disappoint them. That could become a pointed reflection on how this book intends to avoid doing the same.

Your idea about ornamental democracy could give these revisions their destination. It also returns the book to the question in your own intellectual spine: where power lies, and who holds it.

There is a historical distinction to preserve. Tudor consolidation strengthened monarchy. The later survival of monarchy as a predominantly symbolic institution belongs to another, much longer constitutional transformation. We should connect those processes analytically rather than compress them into a single sequence.

Bagehot gives you the appropriate bridge through his distinction between the constitution's "dignified" and "efficient" parts: institutions that sustain allegiance and institutions through which government actually operates. He did not regard the former as useless decoration; their influence helped sustain the whole arrangement. [The English Constitution](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/4351/pg4351-images.html).

Your extension would ask whether popular sovereignty could gradually occupy that dignified position.

Elections, inaugurations, congressional hearings, constitutional language and public argument could remain central to national identity. They could retain real influence and produce consequential changes. Yet the capacity to determine some of the state's most important commitments might become concentrated elsewhere.

That is more plausible—and more unsettling—than a scenario in which every election suddenly becomes meaningless.

Imagine a future administration elected to alter a major national infrastructure programme. It possesses a genuine mandate and lawful authority. But implementation depends on a handful of suppliers, contracts extending beyond electoral terms, specialist knowledge the government cannot independently reproduce and security arrangements that restrict scrutiny. The administration can modify presentation, distribution and some conditions. Changing the underlying system requires an institutional capacity that successive governments have allowed to disappear.

That is an illustrative scenario, not a description established by the present evidence. Its analytical value is that it identifies what would need to happen.

Several mechanisms deserve separate investigation:

* Delegation becomes difficult to reverse. Elected institutions retain formal authority while losing the practical ability to exercise it independently.
* Political choices become embedded in technical systems. Decisions about eligibility, identity, access and risk are made through standards and procurement before most citizens encounter them as political questions.
* Responsiveness substitutes for participation. Systems become better at predicting preferences and satisfying selected demands while citizens have less influence over the purposes being pursued.
* The fiscal bargain changes. If governments can finance important functions through concentrated revenues or rents, their dependence on broad popular bargaining might weaken.
* Crisis arrangements become permanent. Institutions built for exceptional coordination accumulate ordinary governing responsibilities.

The fiscal question is already present in the original research. The Tilly intake explicitly raises consolidation financed without the bargaining that can generate representation and rights. That unresolved point should move into the main argument. It offers a causal explanation for the constitutional outcome, rather than simply a dark description of it. [Chapter 12 research memo (line 778)](ch12/memo.md:778).

The American form could therefore combine intense electoral competition with restricted control over particular strategic systems. It might remain highly responsive to public anger, sensitive to courts and divided across jurisdictions. Its consolidation would be selective. Its democratic substance would also vary by domain.

"Ornamental" should mean a demonstrable separation between legitimating authority and effective control. It should not become a synonym for bureaucracy, unpopular decisions or imperfect democracy. Evidence against the scenario would include elections that redirect strategic commitments, legislatures that enforce meaningful conditions, courts whose remedies change operations, and public institutions capable of replacing essential suppliers.

Democracy and liberalism also need separate treatment. Electoral influence might diminish while substantial legal protections survive. Alternatively, competitive elections might continue while rights and independent scrutiny deteriorate. Those are different trajectories.

This argument has predecessors that deserve direct engagement. Crouch's account of post-democracy explicitly considers democratic forms surviving while substantive political influence concentrates among elites. Wolin examines a hybrid of corporate and state power. Your contribution would be to explain a particular historical mechanism through which such a settlement might emerge: the state subordinates independent technological powers while popular control over the consolidated apparatus fails to keep pace. [Crouch, Coping with Post-Democracy](https://fabians.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2012/07/Post-Democracy.pdf), [Wolin, Democracy Incorporated](https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctvc77b3h).

Lepore belongs in this discussion because she distinguishes technologies that assist government from systems presumed to replace government by consent. Her interviews also emphasise the possibility of democratic resistance. Our engagement with her new book remains based on those primary interviews and public materials, rather than a full reading of the book; that is a specific remaining research task. [Lepore interview](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/jill-lepore-on-why-silicon-valley-gets-science-fiction-wrong/).

The question your book could put alongside hers is whether an artificial state might emerge through the expansion of public authority over private technology, as well as through private technology's displacement of public authority. A stronger state does not by itself answer the question of consent.

I would concentrate the largest revisions in Chapter 9, on constitutional appearance and effective power; Chapter 11, on composite and selective consolidation; and Chapter 12, on the possible settlements. The other chapters need targeted additions and corrections, particularly the new Chastellain scenes, Talbot's artillery experience and the fiscal mechanism.

Before another substantial prose pass: for each proposed change, record the existing claim, the new evidence, its consequence for the argument and the decision required. Preserve the original's historical texture and your voice. Let the new evidence change the ending sufficiently that the barons' loss of independence and the people's acquisition of power are no longer assumed to be the same event.
