# Assessment — Chris Miller, *Chip War: The Fight for the World's Most Critical Technology* (Scribner, 2022)

**Text read:** the whole book (front matter, introduction, chs. 1–54, conclusion; notes and index consulted for pins). **Pinning caveat:** `/home/claude/books/Miller.txt` is an ebook-derived conversion, not a scan of the print edition: each form-feed "page" is a whole chapter (PDF p. 22 = Introduction, PDF p. 26 = ch. 1, … PDF p. 102 = Conclusion), and printed page numbers survive only as four stray inline markers (164, 169, 216, 289) and in the index. Printed pages below are therefore taken from the index or interpolated from those markers and are marked "≈" where interpolated. Text quality is good; there is light letter-spacing damage throughout ("Armc hair", "ch emical") but no garbled passages. **Date caveat:** the book went to press in mid-2022 and does not contain the 7 October 2022 BIS rule, the CHIPS awards, or the 2023 Japan–Netherlands alignment; it is the pre-history of those, not their record. Tier: T3 (serious trade synthesis, interview- and archive-based).

## 1. The book in two paragraphs

Miller's argument is that computing power, not steel or oil, is the currency of great-power competition, and that its production has concentrated — deliberately, by state and corporate decision — into a handful of irreplaceable firms and one island. "World War II was decided by steel and aluminum, and followed shortly thereafter by the Cold War, which was defined by atomic weapons. The rivalry between the United States and China may well be determined by computing power" (PDF p. 22, Introduction / printed p. xix). The concentration is stated as fact and as vulnerability: "our production of computing power depends fundamentally on a series of choke points: tools, chemicals, and software that often are produced by a handful of companies—and sometimes only by one" (PDF p. 23 / printed p. xxiv–xxv). The Dutch firm ASML "builds 100 percent of the world's extreme ultraviolet lithography machines" and "OPEC's 40 percent share of world oil production looks unimpressive by comparison" (PDF p. 23 / printed p. xxv).

The history he tells runs from the Pentagon as first customer (Minuteman, Apollo) through the civilian market that funded Moore's Law, the Soviet "copy it" failure, Perry's offset strategy and its vindication over Iraq in 1991, Japan's DRAM assault and America's resurgence via offshoring, the foundry model and TSMC, the EUV gamble, and China's state-directed "assault" on the industry — ending with the American discovery, in the Jinhua and Huawei cases of 2018–20, that the choke points could be weaponised: "The U.S., it turns out, has escalation dominance when it comes to severing supply chains" (PDF p. 96, ch. 51 / printed p. ≈317). His verdict on globalisation is the sentence the manuscript most needs: "'globalization' of chip fabrication hadn't occurred; 'Taiwanization' had. Technology hadn't diffused. It was monopolized by a handful of irreplaceable companies" (PDF p. 92, ch. 49 / printed p. 297–98).

## 2. What it CONFIRMS

- **Ch. 10 §VIII, "exactly one supplier on earth, no EUV system ever having shipped to China."** Miller: "Locked out of the research at the U.S. national labs, Nikon and Canon decided not to build their own EUV tools, leaving ASML as the world's only producer" (PDF p. 65, ch. 32 / printed p. 188); and "After discussions with the United States, the Netherlands decided not to approve the sale of ASML's EUV machines to Chinese firms" (PDF p. 96, ch. 51 / printed p. 317). Carries the claim at T3.

- **Ch. 10 §VIII and C10-2, the leading-node count.** Miller supplies the firm-level series behind the polity count: GlobalFoundries' 2018 exit — "The number of companies capable of fabricating leading-edge logic chips fell from four to three" (PDF p. 76, ch. 40 / printed p. 234) — and then "As the decade ended, only two companies could manufacture the most cutting-edge processors, TSMC and Samsung" (PDF p. 77, ch. 41 / printed p. 240). Apple's processors "can only be made in Taiwan" (PDF p. 74, ch. 38 / printed p. ≈224).

- **Ch. 10 §VIII, the export-control regime as "the negative image of the same state programme."** Miller on the May 2020 foreign-direct-product rule: Huawei "was simply cut off from the world's entire chipmaking infrastructure, except for chips that the U.S. Commerce Department deigned to give it a special license to buy" (PDF p. 96, ch. 51 / printed p. 316). The book's own sentence — "administrative power to deny a rival's" foundry layer — is Miller's Huawei chapter in one clause.

- **Ch. 12 §IV, "regulation of the switch rather than seizure of the property"; spine §8(c), "the ability to turn their systems off."** Miller shows the off-switch exercised against a foreign over-mighty subject: after the Jinhua ban, "Within months, production at Jinhua ground to a halt. China's most advanced DRAM firm was destroyed" (PDF p. 94, ch. 50 / printed p. 310); and the mechanism named — "It's far easier to control choke points in the chipmaking process when so many essential steps require tools, materials, or software produced by just a handful of firms" (PDF p. 95, ch. 51 / printed p. 315). Confirms that the switch runs through licence, not title.

- **Ch. 6 / ch. 10 §VIII / C6-3, capitulation-without-siege as procurement policy.** TSMC's chairman on the Huawei rule: he "promised not only to abide by the letter of the law but also its spirit" (PDF p. 96, ch. 51 / printed p. 316); and both leading-edge foundries sit "in countries that rely on the U.S. military for their security" (printed p. 315). The alignment of Taiwan, Korea and the Netherlands is composition with the polity that can pay, exactly as ch. 6 predicts.

- **Ch. 12 §IV, the electrotech corollary (spine §8(d)): "control of the supply is control of the people's weapon too."** Miller on Russia: its "drones shot down over Ukraine are full of foreign microelectronics"; Russian factories are "deploying chips intended for dishwashers into missile systems, according to U.S. intelligence"; a Javelin relies "on over 200 semiconductors" (PDF p. 101, ch. 54 / printed p. 342–43).

- **Ch. 12 §V, Russia as the demonstration case.** "It had the world's largest nuclear arsenal. But its semiconductor production couldn't keep up… and the military consequences were disastrous" (PDF p. 59, ch. 28 / printed p. 159).

## 3. What it CORRECTS

Nothing in the manuscript is factually wrong by Miller's account, but two statements are imprecise:

1. **Manuscript (Chapter 10 §VIII) dates the American denial regime to "the October 2022 BIS rule and its expansions."** Miller (published before that rule) shows the regime was built 2016–20: the ZTE denial order and plea (2016–17, re-imposed April 2018), Jinhua's entity-listing (October 2018), the Huawei entity-listing (May 2019) and the foreign-direct-product extension of May 2020 (PDF pp. 92–96, chs. 49–51 / printed pp. 299–317). The 2022 rule generalised a switch already thrown twice; the passage should say so, because the earlier cases are the ones with an audited *effect* (Jinhua destroyed; Huawei "forced to divest part of its smartphone business and its server business," printed p. 316).

2. **Manuscript (ch. 10 §VIII; C10-2) treats "one EUV supplier on earth" as a single-polity chokepoint.** Miller: "ASML itself only produced 15 percent of an EUV tool's components"; the light source is Cymer of San Diego, the laser Trumpf, the mirrors Zeiss; "ASML's EUV tools weren't really Dutch" (PDF p. 75, ch. 39 / printed pp. 228–30). This is not a contradiction — it *strengthens* the American revocation claim (the FDPR bites because of the Cymer content) — but the sentence should read "one integrator, with American-held nodes inside it," or C10-2's baseline will be scored against the wrong object.

## 4. What it ADDS

- **The 1442 test at firm level (ch. 10 §VIII).** Miller's GlobalFoundries passage is the modern budget document: a 7nm process "had already cost $1.5 billion in development and would have required a comparable amount"; "Building cutting-edge processors was too expensive for everyone except the world's biggest chipmakers. Even the deep pockets of the Persian Gulf royals who owned GlobalFoundries weren't deep enough" (PDF p. 76, ch. 40 / printed p. 234). A sovereign wealth fund priced out of the decisive layer is the cleanest contemporary analogue to a magnate priced out of a siege train.

- **The state cannot buy this gun (ch. 10 §VIII disanalogy; Appendix C C10-1).** "Today, even the Pentagon's $700 billion budget isn't big enough to afford facilities for building cutting-edge chips for defense purposes on U.S. soil… A facility to fabricate the most advanced logic chips costs twice as much as an aircraft carrier but will only be cutting-edge for a couple of years" (PDF p. 102, Conclusion / printed p. ≈346–47). The federal share of the chip market: "Now it's around 2 percent… As a buyer of chips, Apple CEO Tim Cook has more influence on the industry than any Pentagon official today" (PDF p. 89, ch. 48 / printed p. 289). The NSA "used to have a chip fab at its headquarters… too expensive to keep upgrading" (printed p. 289–90). This is the Krejsa–Benich "civilian-scale reserve" point stated a decade earlier and with figures; it should replace the WOTR citation as the disanalogy's primary T3 support.

- **TSMC as a state project raised by fiscal summons (ch. 10; ch. 11 fiscal absorption).** "The Taiwanese government provided 48 percent of the startup capital"; wealthy families "were 'asked' by the government to invest"; the prime minister reminded one refuser that "The government has been very good to you for the last twenty years"; "From day one, TSMC wasn't really a private business: it was a project of the Taiwanese state" (PDF p. 62, ch. 29 / printed pp. 167–68). A forced loan in all but name — memorable primary-source material for the fiscal chapter.

- **The 1980s off-switch threat by an ally (ch. 3/ch. 5 over-mighty subjects; ch. 12 §IV).** Ishihara, 1989: "If Japanese semiconductors are not used, this accuracy cannot be assured," and Japan "has nearly a 100 percent share of these 1-megabit semiconductors" (PDF p. 49, ch. 20 / printed pp. 111–12); Harold Brown's "High Tech Is Foreign Policy" (printed p. 113). The switch was recognised as sovereign leverage a generation before Huawei.

- **Perry's offset as cost-imposition (ch. 6/ch. 10 mechanism; ch. 12 nuclear paragraph).** Perry calculated Moscow "would need five to ten years and $30 to $50 billion" to defend against 3,000 American cruise missiles (PDF p. 41, ch. 14 / printed p. 76); and, the precedent for the manuscript's entanglement point, "Soviet defense planners feared American conventionally armed cruise missiles and stealth bombers could disable Soviet command and control over their nuclear forces" (PDF p. 57, ch. 26 / printed pp. 146–47).

- **The Chinese statement of the off-switch (ch. 12 §IV China paragraph).** Xi, 2016: "if an internet enterprise critically relies on the outside world for core components, the 'vital gate' of the supply chain is grasped in the hands of others" (PDF p. 80, ch. 42 / printed p. 244–45); and the militarised industrial policy: "compose shock brigades and special forces to storm the passes" (PDF p. 81, ch. 43 / printed p. 247). Also the aggregate dependence figures: China 6 per cent of the supply chain, 1 per cent of tools, US 39 per cent (printed p. 250).

- **Diffusion objection, contemporary form (ch. 10 §VIII; C10-2 direction).** "Countries and governments can often find ways around choke points, though doing so is time-consuming and expensive… Technological shifts can erode the efficacy of choke points, too" (PDF p. 98, ch. 53 / printed p. 327–28); China's fabrication share "from 15 percent… to 24 percent of global capacity by 2030" at lagging nodes (PDF p. 97, ch. 52 / printed p. 325); RISC-V moved to Switzerland as "geopolitically neutral" (printed p. 324). Use as the honest counter-weight.

- **Two lines for ch. 12.** Gelsinger: "God decided where the oil reserves are, we get to decide where the fabs are" (PDF p. 99, ch. 53 / printed p. 333). For the diffusion argument: "the democratization of authorship coincided with a monopolization of the digital printing press" (PDF p. 62, ch. 29 / printed p. 169).

## 5. What it CONTRADICTS

Stated at full strength:

**(i) The decisive layer was built by private markets, not by the fiscal state, and state attempts to build it mostly failed.** Miller's whole spine is that "Only consumer markets had the volume to fund the vast R&D programs that Moore's Law required" (PDF p. 41, ch. 14 / printed p. 78); that the winning motive was "I… WANT… TO… GET… RICH" (PDF p. 31, ch. 6 / printed p. 34); that "Government efforts were effective not when they tried to resuscitate failing firms, but when they capitalized on pre-existing American strengths" (PDF p. 55, ch. 24 / printed p. 140); that Sematech's lithography rescue "was an abject failure" (same page); that the Soviet sector "functioned like a defense contractor—secretive, top-down, oriented toward military systems" and "never caught up" (PDF p. 35, ch. 8 / printed p. 44); and that China's state-led drive produced Tsinghua Unigroup's default and the HSMC fraud (PDF p. 97, ch. 52 / printed pp. 319–21). The polity most capable of paying — China, "tens of billions" in subsidy, a promised "$150 billion industrial policy" — has not obtained the layer; the polity that obtained it — Taiwan — is not a great power. This reaches the DEFEND-tier claim (spine §6): "Power follows the ability to pay for decisive force."

**(ii) The decisive layer sits outside all four candidate blocs.** Fabrication is "Taiwanized"; lithography is Dutch–German–Californian; memory is Korean. If settlements are "executed inside civilisational blocs," the artillery is in the interstices.

**(iii) China as "furthest along" is weakest exactly here.** Miller's China is the polity with 5/5 Tudor moves on the manuscript's scorecard and the least control over the one layer he calls decisive.

**The book's best answer.** To (i): the manuscript already concedes the purchase/summons disanalogy in ch. 10 §VIII, and spine §8(c) already relocates the state's decisive act from ownership to the switch. Miller himself supplies the reconciliation — the Pentagon *was* the founding customer ("defense dollars still bought 72 percent of all integrated circuits" in 1965, printed p. 31), TSMC was state-founded, and the control that matters today is administrative denial, which worked. Read "ability to pay" as "ability to command the price," and the mechanism survives: the American state did not out-spend China; it out-controlled it, through licences over privately built chokepoints. A *good* answer for the American case and for §8(c), but it honestly changes the weight of the fiscal link: in the foundry layer the 1442 test is passed by firms and failed by treasuries, and the state's contribution is the veto, not the account book. The manuscript should say so rather than let the taille analogy carry more than Miller allows. To (ii): strong — Taiwan, Korea and the Netherlands are inside the American bloc by composition (they "rely on the U.S. military for their security"), and their compliance with the Huawei rule is ch. 6's capitulation-without-siege in real time; the interstitial fab is evidence *for* the bloc geometry, provided the book states that bloc membership is procured, not chosen. To (iii): weaker. The manuscript can say China's settlement is executed at the legibility stack and not yet at the compute-foundry stack — but that concedes "furthest along" is layer-specific, and it should be written as such (spine §6, held on balance, not defended).

None of this touches §8(a), (b), (e) or (f). It bears on §8(d) as below.

## 6. Placement

- **Ch. 10 §VIII — T3 evidence, engaged BY NAME.** Miller is the standard trade account of the concentration the section describes and should carry the EUV, TSMC, GlobalFoundries and "Taiwanization" sentences at footnote level, and be named in the text once for the "Pentagon cannot afford a fab / 2 per cent" disanalogy, which is stronger from him than from Krejsa–Benich.
- **Ch. 12 §IV (American paragraph; China paragraph) — T3 evidence and ally for §8(c).** The Jinhua and Huawei cases enter as the exercised off-switch, by name; Xi's "vital gate" and "storm the passes" as the Chinese side. Note in text that the 2022 rule is the generalisation of these.
- **Ch. 12 §V (nuclear/entanglement paragraph) — footnote.** Ogarkov and the Soviet C2 fear as the 1980s precedent.
- **Appendix C, C10-2 baseline — cite for the firm count (4→3→2) and for the "not infinitely durable" direction caveat; C10-1 — cite the 2 per cent federal share as the counter-baseline.**
- **Ch. 5 / ch. 3 — footnote only:** Ishihara 1989 as the over-mighty ally's threatened switch. **Ch. 6 §VI — footnote:** Perry's cost-imposition arithmetic as attacking the process, not the weapon.
- **Rival in one place:** the private-markets thesis (§5(i)) belongs in ch. 10's critiques, answered there.

**On spine §8(d).** Miller *supports* the ruling: computing power is his master variable throughout ("Whichever country can produce more 1s and 0s will have a serious military advantage," PDF p. 88, ch. 48 / printed p. 283–84); energy appears only as an input cost (Micron's hydro, data-centre power bills); and his oil comparison runs the manuscript's way — "Unlike oil, which can be bought from many countries," compute depends on chokepoints (printed p. xxiv), and for China chips are "as important as hydrocarbons… Unlike oil, though, the supply of chips is monopolized by China's geopolitical rivals" (printed p. 245). He *complicates* it only in the sense of §5(i): the compute-led stack is not state-led, and the ruling's "energy as input" framing should not be read as implying the state buys the outputs. Watts are the powder; but the gun was cast by Fairchild and TSMC, and the state's hand is on the touch-hole, not the foundry ledger.

## 7. Verdict

Miller changes the manuscript's weight, not its direction: he carries every concentration sentence in ch. 10 §VIII and ch. 12 at T3, confirms §8(d) by making compute the master variable and oil the wrong analogy, and gives the off-switch its exercised cases. He alters register in one place — the fiscal link must be restated for the foundry layer as command over a privately built chokepoint rather than purchase, because the treasuries that could pay did not get the gun and the firm that got it could not pay for an army. The single most important thing for the author: the decisive layer lives in Taiwan, Veldhoven and San Diego, held by firms and denied by licence — the artillery state of this century taxes the switch, not the train, and the book should say so before a reviewer says it first.
