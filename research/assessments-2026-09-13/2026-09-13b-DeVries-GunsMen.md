# Assessment — Kelly DeVries, *Guns and Men in Medieval Europe, 1200–1500: Studies in Military History and Technology* (Variorum Collected Studies CS747; Ashgate 2002, Routledge reprint)

**Text read:** the whole volume (introduction; essays I–XVIII; index), from books2/DeVries-GunsMen.txt (370 PDF pages). Variorum keeps each article's original pagination, so pins below give the essay number, the printed page of the original journal/collection, and the PDF page. Essay XVI is *War in History* 5 (1998) 127–45, i.e. the item already in corpus as "DeVries 1998"; every existing manuscript pin to it remains valid at the same page numbers.

**OCR condition:** body text legible throughout; footnotes badly garbled in essays XVI, XVIII and the index. Essay XI has systematic digit corruption ("3" read as "5": Gavere "1455" for 1453, Lagny "1451" for 1431), so its dates were cross-checked against essays XII–XIII. Essay III has two-column text interleaved.

**A premise of the tasking is wrong.** This volume contains no Hussite article; the only occurrences are a German book title in a footnote (essay XV, PDF p. 307) and "the King of Bohemia" as a Luxemburg claimant in 1443 (XVI p. 137 / PDF p. 322). The ch10 §VIII gap ([the Hussite prehistory of the cheap hand-gun… GAP: not yet in corpus]) is **not closed** and must stay open. The book supplies a different, well-pinned prehistory of the cheap hand-gun (§4). The likelier home for the Hussite material, on DeVries's repeated citation, is Bert S. Hall, *Weapons and Warfare in Renaissance Europe* (1997) — a retrieval suggestion, not something this book asserts.

## 1. The book in two paragraphs

DeVries's collected articles are held together by a single declared conviction, set out in the introduction: "I came to understand that technology did not determine warfare, neither at the end of the Middle Ages, with gunpowder weapons, nor at any time" (Introduction p. x / PDF p. 13), which he names "my thesis of human-determinism in warfare — against that of technological determinism" (p. xi / PDF p. 14). The "Guns" essays (VIII–XVII) document, from chronicles and the Burgundian artillery registers, how rapidly gunpowder weapons evolved and how much they changed siege warfare — and then insist that the change was evolutionary, commander-driven and financially conditioned rather than "revolutionary" or "decisive". The methodological essay XVIII states the programme: "All these recent efforts to define the 'effectiveness' of premodern military technology in deterministic terms have done military history and the history of technology a disservice" (XVIII p. 455 / PDF p. 349).

On the question that matters to this project — guns and the state — the volume's thesis is explicitly Hale's, quoted with approval and adopted as the essay's frame: "the complex shifts towards more effectively centralized forms of government began before cannon were effective or readily transportable and can be explained without reference to gunpowder weapons" (XVI p. 129 / PDF p. 314). DeVries sets out "In support of Hale's dissent" (same page) to test Parker's centralisation claim against France, Burgundy and England, and concludes that "France and Burgundy do follow a pattern showing the decline of feudalism and the rise of the central state which conforms to that proposed by Geoffrey Parker and others" while "in the case of England, the pattern was not followed" (XVI p. 145 / PDF p. 330). The book is therefore, in the manuscript's own vocabulary, the field's anti-determinist speaking at length — and speaking, on close reading, rather more for the mechanism than his framing admits.

## 2. What it CONFIRMS

- **The train as institution, not gadget (ch06 §III).** Manuscript: "The protagonist of this chapter is not a gadget. It is a payroll." The existing pin [^6-5] ("intricate organization and superior leadership", XVI p. 132 / PDF p. 317) holds. Better, the 1997 Vienna paper carries the mechanism unhedged: "smaller ducal or comital entities who could not afford to expend large amounts on the acquisition of their own gunpowder weapons began to be forced out of power by the more fiscally strong central states" (XII p. 293 / PDF p. 264). A stronger concession than the 1998 sentence the manuscript uses, and not in corpus.

- **The state seizes local guns and turns them on their owners (ch10 §VI, intro, coda).** Each rising state "seized this technology by whatever means they could and then turned it against these same local entities to force their submission" (XVI p. 129 / PDF p. 314). Pins: John the Fearless ordered all the duchy's guns "not actually in use in his castles" into "a special arsenal in Dijon" (XVI p. 135 / PDF p. 320); Gaier, quoted: the dukes "were able, in time of need, to requisition the machines of war and cannons from the towns" (XVI p. 134 / PDF p. 319); after Liège's rebellion in 1466 "all gunpowder and gunpowder weapons were taken from the town to the more passive Namur" (XI p. 130 / PDF p. 249); Crotoy's 1423 capitulation demanded "all guns and powder; other weapons were not so designated" (same page).

- **Siege tempo collapsed (ch06 §I–II; spine §2).** "By the middle of the fifteenth century every siege was undertaken with numerous guns, and most besieged targets fell rapidly to fortification breach or intimidated surrender" (X p. 347 / PDF p. 224; repeated XI p. 121). Dinant, "which had resisted 17 sieges during the Middle Ages, fell to Charles the Bold… after only a week" (XIII p. 231 / PDF p. 274) — a same-walls comparison of exactly the kind ch06 §IV runs on Rouen and Harfleur.

- **The wall repriced (ch06 §II).** The manuscript's "tall and thin" paragraph rests on a flagged T3 English Heritage line. DeVries supplies the T2 sentence: traditional walls "with their tall, flat surfaces were easy targets for guns… the weight and relative thinness of the base of these walls invariably made it easier to penetrate these fortifications" (XIII p. 233 / PDF p. 276).

- **England as the broken case (ch10 §VI).** The existing pins to XVI pp. 143–5 hold; the Wars of the Roses kings "simply never had strong royal control over these weapons similar to that held by their continental counterparts" (XVI p. 142 / PDF p. 327); the private-war catalogue is at p. 144 / PDF p. 329.

- **Composition without a shot (ch06 §I; spine §8(e)5).** Bourg, June 1451, "captured in just six days after his heavy artillery was brought up to the walls of the castle, although they never fired a shot" (XIII p. 231 / PDF p. 274, from Vale); Harcourt 1449, "a single shot… caused the English to surrender" (same page).

- **The economic bar (ch05, Ottoman comparator).** DeVries endorses Bartusis: "the failure of the Byzantines to make extensive use of firearms can be attributed largely to economic factors" (X p. 351 / PDF p. 228); Urban's defection to Mehmed was over pay (X p. 356 / PDF p. 233, quoting Ducas).

## 3. What it CORRECTS

1. **Chapter 10 §V, note [^10-23]** cites "DeVries 1998: 137" for Commynes's "I have never seen so much shooting in such a few days". The sentence is on **p. 138** (PDF p. 323); p. 137 carries only the ten-salvo and "most fearsome artillery duel" lines.

2. **Chapter 6 §III** says "No baron had this. No city had this. No league of either could plausibly assemble it." As written this is false for guns and true only for the standing train. Ghent in 1382 fielded "two hundred carts full of this type of weaponry" and fired "more than three hundred cannons all at the same time" at Bevershoutsveld (VIII pp. 296, 299 / PDF pp. 195, 198, from Froissart); Nuremberg in 1462 held 2,230 guns for its defence (XVIII p. 469 n. / PDF p. 363); "many other western European magnates, some even below the status of the duke of Burgundy, owned and operated bombards between 1380 and 1420" (XII p. 291 / PDF p. 262). The sentence should be narrowed to the permanently financed, salaried, standardised train — which is what the paragraph actually argues.

3. **Chapter 10 §VII** quotes Porter ("too expensive for the nobility… a monopoly of the Crown") and Rogers's spiral "at DeVries 1998: 128" as if DeVries were their conduit. He quotes them there in order to oppose them: "only one historian has disagreed with this technologically determined rise of the early modern state, Sir J.R. Hale… In support of Hale's dissent, this article will investigate…" (XVI p. 129 / PDF p. 314). The note should say the passages are quoted by DeVries as the target of his critique.

4. **Chapter 6 §VI** has Talbot killed by an axe (Pollard) after Basin's culverin shot. DeVries's surgical essay lists Talbot among those "killed by gunshot" at Castillon (XVII p. 136 / PDF p. 337), without argument — a variance to log in critiques.md, not a correction. (He also misdates Castillon "1452" at XVI p. 133; do not follow.)

5. **Chapter 6 §III** dates corned powder "shortly after 1400"; DeVries gives "c. 1410" (X p. 349 n. / PDF p. 226, citing Hall) and "c. 1407" (XI p. 123 / PDF p. 242). Compatible; carry Hall's date once retrieved.

## 4. What it ADDS

- **ch10 §VIII, the drone objection — the two-layer fifteenth century, pinned (does NOT close the Hussite gap).** Hand-held guns date "from the 1380s" (XVIII p. 469 / PDF p. 363); Richard II "may have outfitted his army with the first handguns" (XVI p. 140 / PDF p. 325); Compiègne's bronze culverins in 1430 fired lead balls that "were even able to penetrate the armour of a man-at-arms" (IX p. 14 / PDF p. 217); Philip the Good's 1456 crusade plan counted "500–600 gunners outfitted with hand-held weapons… (the same incidentally as the number of cavalry)" (XI p. 122 / PDF p. 241); at Murten 1476 "as many as 10,000 culverniers may have participated on both sides" (XII p. 292 / PDF p. 263); the Garnier database of 4,406 Burgundian guns, 1412–74, lists 1,755 couleuvrines against 24 bombards after 1420 (XII appendix p. 297 / PDF p. 268). Best single datum: of 2,230 guns ready for Nuremberg's defence in 1462, "2,034 were hand-held" (XVIII p. 469 n.). Diffusion at the projectile layer, concentration at the train — DeVries's own numbers.

- **ch10 §VIII, export controls, fifteenth-century form.** "Usually, this trade was strictly forbidden, but by 1418–1436 this prohibition had largely been forgotten, at least by Charles VII of France, who frequently purchased gunpowder and saltpetre from Italian and German merchants" (XI p. 130 / PDF p. 249; England's 1414 prohibition, *Foedera*, at n. 60); and "despite numerous attempts to prohibit trade… there was a large cross-Mediterranean trade of these arms" (X p. 353 / PDF p. 230). Supporting colour for the denial paragraph.

- **ch06 §III, powder as the consumable line.** Compiègne 1430 took "17,000 lbs of gunpowder" (IX p. 13 / PDF p. 216); Constantinople "fifty-five thousand pounds weight of powder" on Tedaldi's count (X p. 357 / PDF p. 234); bombards needed "on average a charge of more than 80 lbs" (XI p. 129 / PDF p. 248); sieges failed for want of it — Oudenaarde 1452, Cologne 1475 (XI p. 130). Context for the 1442 programme's 20,000 lb and its annual character.

- **ch06 §II, the tipping-point GAP.** DeVries's periodisation: phases "c. 1326 to 1382… 1382 to 1436… 1436 to 1494" (IX p. 2 / PDF p. 205); technologically, pre-1380 small guns, 1380–1420 bombard dominance, post-1420 smaller guns with "crumbed" powder (XII p. 286 / PDF p. 257). On Rogers directly: "in using this date [1420], Rogers must disregard several similarly successful sieges which took place between 1377 and 1420; gunpowder was also corned more than a decade prior to his date" (IX p. 2 n. 2 / PDF p. 205). This is the Rogers-vs-DeVries text the GAP note wants; it records the dispute at page level without settling it.

- **ch10 §VI, a fourth counter-case.** "England and the Holy Roman Empire do not follow the same pattern as France, Spain and Burgundy/the Low Countries" (XII p. 293 n. 54 / PDF p. 267). The Empire is the comparator the chapter's triad lacks.

- **ch06 §VI / spine §8(e)6, contemporaries and finance.** DeVries's Tournai study finds that in 1340 "The English perceive defeat to have come to their king because of a lack of funds which their Parliament unwisely failed to appropriate for the war on the continent" (IV p. 105 / PDF p. 97). The defeated *could* see the purse in 1340; the ruling's claim that the defeated of 1450 could not see the fiscal apparatus needs to be stated as specific to the reconquest's post-mortems, not as a general property of losers.

- **ch06 §VI, Castillon primary.** The anonymous letter of 19 July 1453 (*BEC* 8): "each ball hit five or six men, killing them all" (X p. 348 / PDF p. 225, DeVries's translation). T1 via T2.

## 5. What it CONTRADICTS

Stated at full strength, in DeVries's words.

**(a) The mechanism's arrow — reaching spine §8(a) and the DEFEND tier.** DeVries's frame for the only essay the manuscript uses is Hale's: "The case for the suggestion that artillery was an instrument centralizing power is… feeble… the complex shifts towards more effectively centralized forms of government began before cannon were effective or readily transportable and can be explained without reference to gunpowder weapons" (XVI p. 129 / PDF p. 314). He reads the whole gunpowder story as "a technological evolution happening slowly and over a long period of time and not a technological revolution" (XVIII p. 469 / PDF p. 363). Against ch06's "decade in which the curve turned", he holds that "from at least 1374 onwards they had been effective in bringing down fortification walls" (XVIII p. 467 / PDF p. 361) and calls Oman's mid-fifteenth-century dating of artillery's triumph an "unfortunate underestimation" (XIII pp. 227–8 / PDF pp. 270–1). And his explanation of who wins is the commander, not the fisc: the Turkish guns "effected nothing without their master" (X p. 346 / PDF p. 223); the Muslim powers lacked "the prophetic leadership of a Henry V, Charles VII, Louis XI, Philip the Bold…" (X p. 353 / PDF p. 230).

*The book's best answer:* the manuscript never claims the gun conjured the fisc; §8(a) concedes the Curry/Hale ordering and answers with the ratchet. DeVries's evidence serves that answer better than his frame: the "forced out of power by the more fiscally strong central states" sentence (XII p. 293) is a fiscal-threshold claim; his England is a monopoly that "did not… use them to control his own subjects" (XVI p. 145) and lost; his Byzantium lost for "economic factors". The answer is good on the mechanism and weaker on tempo: the manuscript should stop implying the technology turned in the 1440s and say plainly that the *institution* did — the technical curve is DeVries's slow one, and ch06 §II's GAP should resolve that way rather than await a monograph.

**(b) England — the Tudor turn without artillery.** English gunpowder weaponry "had almost completely disappeared by the middle of the fifteenth century and would not reappear until the middle of the sixteenth century" — "a virtual 'dark ages' of military technology which would last for more than a century" (XVI pp. 142, 130 / PDF pp. 327, 315). If true, the settlement that names the book was executed by a polity without the ratchet.

*The book's best answer:* spine §7 already casts England as latecomer, consolidating under a price set by France; and DeVries's own pages undercut his "century": Henry Tudor "had also brought French artillery with him" to Bosworth (XVI p. 143 / PDF p. 328), both sides used guns at Stoke (p. 144), cast iron revived with "the Wealden iron process… in the early sixteenth century" (XII p. 293), and his own footnote invokes "Henry VII's castles… Deal" (XIII p. 240 n. 60 / PDF p. 283) — Henry VIII's artillery forts. The honest statement: the English fiscal-administrative moves (ch11) precede the English train, as the French settlement of 1439–46 preceded 1449–53; the English ratchet is Henry VIII's ordnance, not Henry VII's. Consistent with §8(a), but ch11 must not let Henry VII stand as an artillery prince. The answer is adequate; England remains the mechanism's weakest leg and DeVries makes that plain.

**(c) Local guns first.** "The history of fourteenth-century gunpowder weaponry in continental Europe is one of almost complete local control" (XVI p. 130 / PDF p. 315). This inverts the ch06 §III sentence corrected above but supports ch10's seizure move; the reconciliation is the train/gun distinction, which the manuscript has and should apply consistently.

**(d) A terminological hazard.** DeVries closes his methodological essay by adopting Buchanan's "image of the ratchet" for *cumulative evolutionary* change against Rogers's "punctuated equilibrium" (XVIII p. 470 / PDF p. 364). The spine's "RATCHET" means the opposite — the irreversibility step. Specialists will read the coinage against Buchanan's; one sentence at first use should distinguish them.

## 6. Placement

- **ch06 §II–III:** T2, evidence. Essays XII and XIII pin the wall paragraph, the periodisation and the Rogers dispute; engage DeVries **by name** where the chapter concedes the technical curve is slow.
- **ch06 §VI:** T1-via-T2 for the Castillon letter; footnote for the Talbot variance. The Howard retrieval flag is not closed here (DeVries cites Howard only on Constantinople, X p. 345).
- **ch10 §VI–VII:** T2, rival-turned-witness. State that DeVries quotes Porter and Rogers to oppose them; let XII p. 293 carry the concession; add the Empire footnote; engage Hale's sentence **by name** beside Curry's reversal.
- **ch10 §VIII:** T2, evidence for the two-layer paragraph; footnote for the export-control parallel; Hussite GAP stays open with the pointer to Hall.
- **ch05:** footnote, essay X, for the Byzantine economic bar and Urban's pay.
- **Intro and coda "sceptics-concede" passages:** upgrade the DeVries entry from the 1998 "conform" sentence to the 1997 "forced out of power" sentence, and name his frame as Hale's.
- **critiques.md (ch06, ch10):** the anti-determinist objection at full strength from XVI and XVIII; the "ratchet" clash.

## 7. Verdict

The book changes the weight and one point of register, not the direction: it strengthens the mechanism with better-pinned concessions than the manuscript now uses, while forcing ch06 to give up any hint that the *technology* turned in the 1440s and to say that the institution did. The single most important thing the author should know is that this volume contains no Hussite material at all — the ch10 §VIII gap stays open — and that its author's one sustained essay on guns and the state was written to support Hale's claim that centralisation "can be explained without reference to gunpowder weapons", so every DeVries pin in the book should be cited as a witness testifying against his own frame, with that frame named. Secondary: DeVries's "dark ages" of English artillery to the mid-sixteenth century makes the English case the mechanism's weakest leg, and his approving use of "ratchet" for slow cumulative evolution is a terminological collision with the spine's key word that needs one defusing sentence.
