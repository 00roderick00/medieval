# Brands and Gavin (eds), *COVID-19 and World Order: The Future of Conflict, Competition, and Cooperation* (Johns Hopkins UP, 2020) — assessed against *The Artillery State*

*Text: /home/claude/books/Brands.txt (524 PDF pages, ~179k words; open-access edition). No printed page numbers survive the conversion — no running heads or feet — so every pin is a PDF page only. Body text is clean; the sole garble is a Chinese-language citation at p. 279 n. 20. Read in full: the editors' introduction (pp. 16–37); MacMillan; Bobbitt; Fox and Scott; Applebaum; Farrell and Han; Stein; Steinberg; Brands, Feaver and Inboden; Wright; Schake; Hicks; Schmidt; Ferguson; Economy and Allison in their policy and power-shift sections. Skimmed, and reported as skimmed: the public-health chapters (Greene and Vargha, Inglesby, Rutkow, Kahn et al.), the climate and food chapters (Urpelainen, Fanzo), Lipsky on the G20; Steil read for his "Two Worlds" model only. Not on the project's ledger; Roderick added it. What he most plausibly wants — 2020 witnesses to the state's reassertion, baselines earlier than the manuscript's 2024–26 record, and the authors who argue the opposite — is set out in §§4–6.*

## 1. The book in two paragraphs

A conference volume assembled "in a little over four months" (p. 14) at the Kissinger Center in summer 2020, unified by a question rather than a thesis: will the pandemic remake world order? The editors' answer is deflationary and is the volume's centre of gravity: "That COVID will have a similarly transformative effect seems unlikely… COVID will probably not dramatically change the distribution of material power" (pp. 26–27); the likeliest settlement is "a reversion to something like the 'two worlds' approach of the postwar era" — "to lessen dependence on China while deepening cooperation among the democracies" (p. 27). The recurrent observation across contributors of very different temper is Stein's: "COVID-19 is an accelerator of global changes that were already under way, much more than it is a generator of sharp shifts in direction" (p. 304).

What was accelerated is the manuscript's subject: the state taking things back. Stein names it "the rebordering of the world" — "geography and borders, which never really went away, were back with a vengeance even before COVID-19" (p. 308); "In the last decade, after more than twenty-five years of hyper-globalization, control and rebordering have become relatively more important. The pandemic can only accelerate that trend" (p. 311). Steil: "'National security' will become the umbrella logic for all manner of government intervention in this and other areas of commerce" (p. 227). Fox and Scott: "the post-COVID-19 technology order will require the return of a robust role for government—direction, regulation, funding, and linkage to policy goals—that would have been anathema to Silicon Valley as late as a decade ago" (p. 212). Against that consensus stand three dissenters — Bobbitt, Ferguson, Schmidt — given at full strength in §5.

## 2. What it CONFIRMS

**The exogenous pressure was back on the books before the war.** Manuscript ch. 12 §II: "the exogenous consolidating pressure, back on the ledgers." Wright, June 2020, dates the doctrinal turn to the 2017 NSS and the 2018 NDS, which stated "that 'inter-state strategic competition, not terrorism, is now the primary concern in U.S. national security'" (p. 371); Brands, Feaver and Inboden: "the pandemic has made great-power competition more important, not less" (p. 363).

**Globalisation reorganising inside blocs.** Manuscript ch. 11 §VI: "consolidation within blocs, not above them." Brands, Feaver and Inboden: "re-globalization along geopolitical lines… globalization will increasingly occur within rather than across geopolitical lines" (pp. 356–57), issuing in "a two-tiered order" (p. 359). Steil's "Two Worlds" (pp. 236–38) and Schake — "free societies using policies occasioned by the pandemic to bifurcate technology, investment, education, and supply chains to exclude China" (p. 389) — are the same geometry at T3, dated 2020.

**China's absorption mode is fusion** (Appendix C, C4-1). Fox and Scott: "In China there is no expectation of separation among the private, public, and nonprofit sectors—academia, business, and the military" (p. 212). Steil: "China has committed at least $1.4 trillion in public funds over the coming five years to investments in artificial intelligence, data centers, mobile communications, and other technology-related projects—all of which are to be based on domestic firms" (p. 237).

**The American candidacy is "the least self-aware"** (ch. 12 §IV). Fox and Scott: "The US government, as many have observed, is still largely organized around a 1947 model designed during the smokestack era" (p. 213). Schmidt: "The United States is not playing to win" (p. 481); federal R&D "is now at pre-Sputnik levels as a percentage of gross domestic product" — 0.61 per cent in 2017 against 1.86 in 1964 (p. 480 and n. 15).

**The state is reaching for the platforms' switch in 2020** (ch. 11 §VI move two; ch. 12 §IV). Farrell and Han: "both the Trump administration and the Biden campaign want to unravel Section 230 of the Communications Decency Act, the cornerstone of platform self-regulation… the European Union, India, and other powers are similarly moving decisively against self-regulation" (p. 284). Fox and Scott: "No longer 'citizens of the world,' major US technology companies would need to operate more like 'national champions'" (p. 209).

**The state does not wither; the substitutes cannot carry the load** (ch. 1 §§V–VI). Schake names "corrosion of the state system," invokes "the medieval model where states recede in importance and other groupings—businesses, religious organizations, cities—become the unit of action," cites Bobbitt, and rules: "Distributed powers and mobilized publics may not destroy the state but can combine to act in its absence… The American experience of the COVID-19 pandemic suggests they cannot yet substitute for action of the federal state… These are things only the federal government has the breadth to do" (pp. 396–97). Stein, the volume's nearest heir to Slaughter, concedes the capacity test: "centrality in these international networks allows states to weaponize interdependence to their advantage" (p. 318).

**The EU as apparatus without a centre** (ch. 12 §IV). Applebaum: "most of its failures are in fact the result of its members' refusal to pool resources—their refusal, in other words, to share sovereignty" (p. 273). And MacMillan, for ch. 1's "requires a cause": "It is possible that the need to deal with the repeated waves of the plague encouraged the move to stronger centralized states, but war was already doing that" (p. 62).

## 3. What it CORRECTS

The manuscript never mentions the pandemic, so there is no factual claim about 2020 to correct. Two datings are imprecise by this book's account.

Manuscript (Chapter 1 §IV) says: "Since 2022 that species of pressure has visibly begun returning: a land war on the continent, rearmament, alliance consolidation." Wright p. 371 dates the doctrinal return to the 2017 NSS and 2018 NDS; Stein pp. 306–07 and p. 319 n. 5 date the first decisive-layer control — Huawei on the Entity List, the foreign-produced direct product rule of 5 May 2020 — to 2019–20; Fox and Scott p. 210 give the May 2019 order and its May 2020 extension, after which "Huawei could no longer obtain semiconductors from its largest and most important supplier in Taiwan." The war confirmed the pressure; it did not begin it. Read "since 2017–20, and visibly since 2022," or say the doctrinal turn preceded the war by five years.

Manuscript (Appendix C, C11-1 baseline) scores the EU "no fisc." Ferguson p. 498: "The Next Generation EU plan, presented by the European Commission on May 27, proposed 750 billion euros of additional EU spending, to be financed through bonds issued by the EU"; Applebaum p. 273 records its reception as "a 'Hamiltonian' deal." "No fisc" holds for a standing revenue instrument; it is imprecise as a statement that no bloc-level borrowing exists. Note NGEU (2020) as the precedent the 2025–26 own-resource proposals extend.

## 4. What it ADDS

**A dated counter-forecast for the context variable.** Appendix C baselines exogenous pressure at 2025–26. The volume supplies the opposite expectation from two defence professionals: Hicks, "US defense spending is likely to decline in the coming years" (p. 419), "the new fiscal reality has only increased the pressure to constrain defense spending in the next presidential administration, no matter which candidate wins" (p. 416), with China announcing "its smallest planned increase in defense spending in thirty years" (p. 414); Schake, "sustained downward pressure on defense spending" (p. 399). The Hague pledge and ReArm Europe are the record moving against a sourced, dated forecast — exactly what the scoring discipline wants. Appendix C context variable; ch. 12 §II in a sentence.

**Earlier baselines for C10-2 and C10-1.** The May 2020 FDPR (Ferguson p. 494: the rules "would cut Huawei off from using advanced semiconductors produced anywhere in the world using US technology or intellectual property. This includes the chips produced in Taiwan by… TSMC"); US firms at "more than half the global revenue for chip manufacturing equipment compared with Japan's 27% and Europe's 17%" (Fox and Scott p. 210); China at "about 45% of global semiconductor demand" importing "more than 90% of its chip needs," spending "more on silicon than it does on hydrocarbons" (Schmidt p. 477); the $354m Phlow contract of May 2020 as the first pandemic-era public financing of a strategic base (Fox and Scott p. 214). These let C10-1's "subsidy → equity → ownership" arc begin two years before CHIPS.

**The foundry as casus belli, named in 2020.** Ferguson: Chinese nationalist posts proposed invasion "as a solution to the threat that Huawei will be cut off from TSMC… 'Reunification of the two sides, take TSMC!'" (p. 496), and Allison warned that the ambition to "kill Huawei" "could play a role similar to the sanctions imposed on Japan between 1939 and 1941" (p. 496). A T3 witness that the object of a future siege was already the fab: ch. 12 §V footnote; ch. 6's capitulation thread.

**Absorption by equity, 2020.** Farrell and Han p. 285: US efforts to secure a European vaccine "have led to the German government taking a controlling stake in the company in question." Outside the stack, inside the pattern: C4-1.

**Schmidt on the stack, from the platform side.** "When we fragment the technology stack, we split the world" (Stein pp. 305–06, from the conference keynote). Bratton is the platform-side theorist; Schmidt is the practitioner saying in 2020 that the stack is the unit of order. Ch. 12 §IV.

**Legibility witness.** Applebaum: "Taiwan and Estonia, for example… have successfully digitalized their governments and their communications. But most nations have not done so" (p. 274). Ch. 11 §VI move five.

**A hierarchical-plurality exhibit for ch. 8.** Steinberg on FATF: its decisions, "although technically binding only on its own members, became de facto global standards, since smaller states had little choice but to comply or risk financial isolation" (p. 330), because of "its members' ability to impose costs" (p. 331). The club rules because its members are states with the switch.

**Bobbitt's 2020 restatement** of the market-state checklist (p. 85): footnote to the Shield engagement (chs. 1 and 10).

## 5. What it CONTRADICTS

**Ferguson: the winners were city-states; scale was a liability.** "History shows that plagues are generally bad for big empires… city-states are generally better at limiting the spread of pathogens… The key point is that there are diseconomies of scale when a new pathogen is on the loose… The winners in the short run are none of the above empires. The winners are today's equivalents of city-states" (pp. 490–91); and "the centrifugal forces unleashed by the pandemic are a much bigger threat to a monolithic one-party state than to a federal system" (p. 491). This cuts against bloc-scale consolidation and against the hold-on-balance ruling that China is furthest along (spine §6).

*The book's best answer.* The mechanism prices decisive force, not disease control; Venice ran Europe's best quarantine (MacMillan p. 61) and was still absorbed into the world of the artillery states. Ferguson's city-states sit under a patron's constellation and a patron's foundry — TSMC is the reason Taiwan matters (p. 496). The centrifugal point against China is the manuscript's own two-body caveat (ch. 12 §IV), and a wager. Good on the mechanism; honest on the ranking: Ferguson has named the variable that could make the China wager fail.

**Bobbitt: the successful polity is the one furthest from the consolidated state.** "Is it possible that those states who have responded most effectively to the pandemic are those who have moved the furthest towards the new constitutional order of the informational market-state? Singapore comes to mind, as do Germany, New Zealand, and South Korea" (p. 89); "the industrial nation-state is not equipped to handle completely predictable crises… and its poor responses to these crises undermine its capabilities even further" (p. 89); and the "Westphalian Fallacy" — "the assumption that the constitutional order of states has not changed since 1648 and is unlikely to do so now" (p. 88). Turned on the manuscript: a Tudor turn assumes a constitutional order can return to a form. This reaches the DEFEND-tier mechanism (spine §6) at the joint the Shield assessment named — field versus ratchet.

*The book's best answer.* The manuscript predicts a return of function, not form — permanent finance, subordination of intermediaries, licensed canon — in whatever constitutional dress each bloc wears (ch. 12 §III). Bobbitt's own list of market-state stirrings includes sovereign wealth funds and the all-volunteer force: a state that owns capital and buys its soldiers is ch. 4's domestication in modern dress. Good, but it must be made by name; Bobbitt has now stated the terminus reading twice, eighteen years apart.

**Schmidt: the stack must not be split, and the contest is between firms.** "The future will increasingly become a battle over platform technologies dominated by a small number of companies from information-rich countries" (p. 472); "deliberately splitting the 'tech stacks' upon which our modern lives are built will much more likely make the world smaller, not bigger" (p. 478); "Decoupling completely is not a viable option" (p. 481). The units are companies; the state funds and fences. This cuts against ruling §8(b): for the United States, "the state will absorb the stack."

*The book's best answer.* Schmidt's five elements begin with "some purposeful decoupling" and end with "greater federal investment" (p. 470); his conditional — "If market and platform dominance really is synonymous with geopolitical dominance, then we must prepare for a great power relationship with China that could risk outright confrontation" (p. 481) — is the manuscript's premise stated as a fear. Adequate, not decisive: Schmidt describes a state that funds and fences, not one that licenses and switches. Quote him as the platform side's preferred settlement, not as an ally.

**Steinberg: the great-power frame is a distraction; no single model fits.** "The current preoccupation with managing state-to-state relations among politicians and pundits in Washington has distracted attention from the far more pressing set of issues that will dominate the coming decades" (p. 325); "there is reason to doubt whether any single, parsimonious, overarching model is either appropriate or achievable… 'horses for courses'" (p. 327). The Slaughter-optimist read at 2020 strength, aimed at exactly this kind of book.

*The book's best answer.* Ch. 1 §V's capacity test, and Steinberg's own FATF paragraph: the courses are run by horses the states own. Good.

**The deepest contradiction is structural, and no contributor states it because none is arguing against this book: the reassertion of 2020 was compelled by a virus, not by the price of decisive force.** Export bans in "almost ninety countries" (Steil p. 227); the Defense Production Act "to block the export of medical goods" (p. 239); borders "thickened quickly" inside Europe (Stein p. 308); MacMillan's "renewed appreciation of the power of government to take and, where necessary, enforce measures for the good of society" (p. 75). This is the decade's most-cited "return of the state," and Part II's mechanism does not produce it. If the state reasserts for reasons the mechanism does not name, the mechanism is one cause among several, and spine §7's "compelled" loses its exclusivity.

*The book's best answer.* Schake: "many of the constrictions on globalization are temporary… as the pandemic recedes, those reasons will reverse direction" (p. 387); "Restrictions creeping into the globalized order and likely to be of long duration are not the result of the pandemic" (p. 388). And the record the volume dates: the reassertions attached to the decisive layer — Entity List, FDPR, 5G exclusions — preceded the pandemic and outlasted it, while mask and PPE onshoring did not. The mechanism does not claim every reassertion; it predicts which ones stick. Good, and scoreable: medical onshoring that lapsed against semiconductor controls that hardened is a natural experiment ch. 12 §II should name.

## 6. Placement

T3 throughout — serious scholars writing think-tank synthesis in four months, self-described as first thoughts (Farrell and Han p. 282). Never load-bearing; valuable as dated commentary.

- **Ch. 1 §IV**: the dating correction (§3), Wright p. 371 and Stein pp. 306–07 in the note; Stein's "rebordering" named in one sentence as the 2020 vocabulary for the pressure's return.
- **Ch. 1 §V**: Steinberg (pp. 325–27) and Stein (pp. 314–18) named as Slaughter's 2020 successors, answered by the capacity test and Stein's own concession. Rivals, by name.
- **Ch. 11 §VI**: Farrell and Han p. 284 as the 2020 baseline for move two; Fox and Scott p. 209 and Applebaum p. 274 as footnotes to moves two and five; CureVac under move three.
- **Ch. 12 §II**: Hicks and Schake's 2020 defence forecasts as the counter-baseline; Schake's "temporary" distinction and the medical-versus-semiconductor experiment as the answer to the structural contradiction.
- **Ch. 12 §IV**: Ferguson pp. 490–91 by name (city-states; the China ranking); Schmidt pp. 472–81 by name beside Bratton; Bobbitt's 2020 chapter in the Shield note; Fox and Scott p. 213 and Schmidt p. 480 n. 15 as footnotes to "least self-aware."
- **Ch. 12 §V**: Ferguson p. 496 (TSMC as casus belli) in the notes.
- **Appendix C**: context variable — Hicks p. 419, Schake p. 399; C10-2 — FDPR (Stein p. 319 n. 5), Schmidt p. 477; C10-1 — Phlow, the R&D series; C11-1 — NGEU (Ferguson p. 498); C4-1 — CureVac. No Appendix A entry.
- **Coda**: one sentence saying the silence on the pandemic is deliberate — the mechanism prices force, not disease — with this volume as reference.

## 7. Verdict

The book changes neither direction nor register, only weight — and one dating: the return of exogenous pressure and the state's first reach for the decisive layer are on the record from 2017–20, not 2022, which strengthens the tense argument by lengthening the run. The single most important thing the author should know is that the manuscript never mentions the pandemic, and a reader who lived through 2020 will notice: the decade's most-cited "return of the state" was compelled by a virus, not by the price of decisive force, and the book must say, in one paragraph in ch. 12 §II, that its mechanism predicts which reassertions stick — the medical onshoring that lapsed against the semiconductor controls that hardened. Ferguson's city-states, Bobbitt's market-state and Schmidt's unsplit stack are the three 2020 voices arguing the opposite, and each should be engaged by name rather than absorbed.
