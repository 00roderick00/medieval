# Assessment — Juliet Barker, *Conquest: The English Kingdom of France 1417–1450* (Little, Brown 2009; Harvard 2012 ed. scanned)

Tier: T3, but her notes cite the administrative record — Parliament Rolls (PR), Stevenson, the Mont-Saint-Michel chronicle (CMSM), Beaurepaire, Curry, Harriss, Jones — so figures can be re-pinned to T1/T2 through her. Read in full: preface; chapters 15–25 (PDF 240–413); epilogue (PDF 414–418); their endnotes; the 1418–19 Rouen siege (PDF 40–45). Skimmed only: chapters 1–14 (1417–35). Text clean; no garbling. Printed page = PDF − 21 (pp. 19–24), − 17 (ch. 15–16), − 15 (ch. 17–20), − 13 (ch. 21–epilogue).

## 1. The book in two paragraphs

A chronological narrative of the Lancastrian "English kingdom of France" from 1417 to Cherbourg, written against neglect: "its last thirty years have not attracted a dedicated narrative history" (p. xii / PDF 13). Her explanatory line is political-fiscal. The kingdom rested on Burgundy and Bedford; after 1435 it was defended by Talbot and drained by "an unsustainable financial burden on England and Normandy" (p. xi / PDF 12); the truce of Tours then killed it: "while the English took advantage of the truce to demobilise and cut taxes, Charles VII used it to rearm and reorganise his armies so that, when he found the excuse he needed to declare that the English had broken its terms, he was ready and able to invade with such overwhelming force that he swept all before him" (p. xi / PDF 12).

Her verdict on 1449–50 is multi-causal. "The reconquest was achieved in exactly the same way as the original conquest. Charles VII had belatedly adopted his greatest adversary's methods: his troops were trained and disciplined, he had invested heavily in the latest artillery, he had a deep war chest to finance his campaigns and he had taken to the field in person" (p. 402 / PDF 415). The other half is English disintegration: "the Norman towns and garrisons in the last days of the English kingdom of France knew that no one would come to their rescue. They therefore chose to submit voluntarily rather than be forced to do so" (p. 402 / PDF 415); and of the surrenders generally, "The prosaic truth was that it owed more to self-interest than patriotism" (p. 386 / PDF 399). Artillery is one of four adopted methods in her account; finance, leadership and the relief-calculus carry the weight.

## 2. What it CONFIRMS

- Ch. 6 §I/§V ("the din, not the breach"): Mantes, 26 August 1449 — the inhabitants resolved "that a way should be found, without having to suffer the destruction of the town by cannon or otherwise, to obtain a good composition" (p. 385 / PDF 398); "Most walled towns, when forced to choose between capitulation on generous terms which maintained the status quo, and being assaulted and probably losing everything, sensibly chose the former" (p. 386 / PDF 399). The priced-surrender reading from a witness who does not share the thesis.
- §IV, Rouen "some three weeks": "The siege had lasted less than three weeks from start to finish and although the castle had been invested no bombardment of it had begun" (p. 391 / PDF 404); the 16 October scaling attempt repulsed by Talbot in person, "some fifty or sixty Frenchmen... killed or captured" (p. 390 / PDF 403) — confirms the draft's reattribution of that scene to Rouen. Charles "surrounded the castle with his men and a huge array of guns, as if in preparation for a siege" (p. 391): the display coerced; the guns never fired.
- §IV, Harfleur seventeen days: "On 8 December the Bastard of Orléans took six thousand soldiers, four thousand francs-archers and sixteen great cannon to lay siege to Harfleur... hold out until Christmas Day, but then was forced to agree to submit on 1 January 1450" (p. 394 / PDF 407). England had sent in "barley, wheat and malt" — provisioned, so not a starvation fall.
- §IV, Honfleur: "withstood a combination of heavy bombardment and mining for four weeks before it too agreed to yield if no relief was brought before 18 February" (p. 394 / PDF 407). Bayeux: Bureau's guns "in the space of sixteen days, reduced the town walls to rubble" (p. 396 / PDF 409). Caen: 24 June, mines "brought down a tower and stretch of wall" (p. 397 / PDF 410). Falaise deferred "until 21 July in case a relieving army came" (p. 398 / PDF 411); Bellême surrendered "after no relief force came" (p. 394).
- §V, the Somerset dispatch (spine §8(e)3): Barker gives the T1 pin — presented to parliament on 12 February 1449 by Abbot Boulers on Beaufort's behalf, PR xii, 54–5: "there is no place in the king's obedience there which is provided for either in terms of repairs, equipment, or any kind of artillery... would incur inestimable expense" (p. 366 / PDF 379); the estates "declared the impossibility of levying future grants because of the general poverty of the duchy" (p. 367 / PDF 380).
- §V, the defender bankrupt: "Only 2100 men were now stationed in garrisons, down from around 3500 before the truce began" (p. 360 / PDF 373; Curry 1994, Harriss); the king "£372,000 (£195.3m) in debt", the July 1449 subsidy with "none of the money... earmarked for the defence of Normandy" (p. 377 / PDF 390); crown jewels pawned, December 1449 (p. 392 / PDF 405).
- §VI, Formigny: the English "archers even sallied out to seize two of their small field guns"; "Three thousand seven hundred and seventy-four Englishmen were buried on the field in fourteen grave pits" (pp. 395–6 / PDF 408–9).
- Ch. 7 §II: Moleyns "denounced as 'the traitor who sold Normandy' and murdered by Cuthbert Colville" at Portsmouth, 9 January 1450, while paying Kyriell's rioting troops (p. 393 / PDF 406).

## 3. What it CORRECTS

- Ch. 6 §IV says the Rouen ransom was "50,000 écus"; Barker p. 391 / PDF 404 says "a ransom of 50,000 saluts (£4.01m) within twelve months and... eight hostages" (Stevenson ii, 609–17). Salut and écu differ (her note, p. xvi). Verify against the treaty text.
- §IV says Talbot was forfeited because "Curson had earlier refused to deliver Honfleur under the Rouen capitulation's terms". Barker's Rouen places are "Caudebec, Tancarville, Lillebonne, Harfleur, Montivilliers and Arques" (p. 391) — no Honfleur — and Talbot "had been kept a prisoner... because the terms of the capitulation had been breached by Harfleur's refusal to submit" (p. 398 / PDF 411); her note warns that "Honfleur is sometimes mistaken for Harfleur in some accounts of the terms of surrender" (p. 437 / PDF 450). Check the draft's Blondel reading against Stevenson ii, 609–17 before print.
- §IV: Fauconberg at Pont-de-l'Arche was not "the captain... taken at breakfast"; he "had unluckily chosen to spend the night there" and was wounded refusing to surrender to an archer (p. 375 / PDF 388).
- §IV: Harcourt "tint huit jours" (Somerset list); Barker, from Chartier, "The siege lasted fifteen days" (p. 388 / PDF 401). Avranches: draft "fifteen days"; Barker "three weeks" (p. 396 / PDF 409). Record both.
- §IV: Fougères was not seized by "an English freebooter". "The capture of Fougères had been carefully planned in London at least fifteen months earlier" by Suffolk; Surienne was a Garter knight; Beaufort sent "bows, arrows, gunpowder and culverins" afterwards (pp. 371–4 / PDF 384–7), and Surienne was paid "10,000 écus (£729,167) to evacuate Fougères" (p. 392 / PDF 405).
- §VI: Kyriell did not land "some four thousand men". He "had with him 2500 men and a great artillery train" (p. 394 / PDF 407); 1,800 under Vere, Gough and Norbury joined at Valognes (p. 395). Her contract figures (425 men-at-arms, 2,080 archers, p. 392) do not support §V's "sailed short of its establishment".
- §IV [GAP on Rouen 1418–19]: Barker gives "almost six months" to 19 January 1419 (p. 23 / PDF 44) and the expelled "poor, the old, women, children and the sick" dying in the ditch, but NO figure of twelve thousand (p. 22 / PDF 43). The number is not carried by *Conquest*; source it elsewhere or cut. Her 1419 terms — "eighty hostages as surety for the payment of a fine of 300,000 écus" (p. 23) — mirror Caen 1450's 300,000 écus (p. 397).
- §V: "the Lancastrian establishment... cut to some 2,500 men" (Curry 2010). Barker's Curry-1994 figure is 3,500 → 2,100 (p. 360). Reconcile.
- §I/§V "the two documented artillery breaches": Barker records Argentan, where "a cannon blew a hole large enough to admit a cart through the walls" of the castle (p. 389 / PDF 402), and Caen's mined tower. Say "the two the list records".

## 4. What it ADDS

- **The English trains that failed** (§III "the protagonist is a payroll"; §VII): Talbot's Dieppe bastille, 1442–43 — Fforsted, master of the king's ordnance, "poured two hundred cannon, bombards, catapults and other artillery, great and small, into the bastille" (p. 302 / PDF 317); ten months later the dauphin stormed it and "all the artillery found there was carried into Dieppe" (p. 314 / PDF 329). Somerset's 1443 expedition sailed with "a vast artillery train, including twenty cartloads of ribauldequins" (p. 309 / PDF 324); it "was later discovered where Somerset had left it, with his lieutenant at Avranches" (p. 312 / PDF 327). Guns without the institution: the English control case the chapter lacks.
- **The relief clause as an employment term** (§IV's délai de secours): Bourgchier, taking Le Crotoy in 1441, "obtained Henry's promise that he would not be held responsible for surrendering if a relief army was not sent to his aid within a month of being besieged" (p. 290 / PDF 305). Contractual before it was capitulatory — and English. Cf. Pont-de-l'Arche 1418 (p. 20 / PDF 41).
- **1439–41, the curve turning** (part-closes the §II tipping-point GAP): Meaux 1439 — Chamberlain "could not have withstood Richemont's artillery and, without relief, his position had been hopeless" (p. 275 / PDF 290); Avranches 1439 — "three or four weeks of bombardment... had left it unscathed" (p. 276 / PDF 291); Creil, May 1441 — Bureau's train made "a substantial breach... in just over a fortnight" (p. 287 / PDF 302); Pontoise 1441 — bombardment, then storm, four to five hundred English dead (pp. 288–92 / PDF 303–7).
- **The Castillon park's genealogy** (§VI): at Pontoise 1441 the French, "Having learned from the English at Harfleur the previous year... had dug themselves in securely, surrounding their encampments not only with ditches but also with wooden palisades, stakes and carts, and installing cannon and artillery" (p. 291 / PDF 306), and refused battle.
- **Attacking the guns, 1450** (§VI, spine §8(e)4): Beaufort at Caen paid Robin Campbell of the Scots guard to kidnap the Bastard and lead 1,500 men out; "the rest would destroy the French artillery by torching the powder kegs and spiking the guns. The plot, if it was genuine, obviously failed" (p. 397 / PDF 410; Vale 138). The one documented English plan of 1450 targets the train — contemporary warrant for the Talbot conjecture, hedged as Barker hedges it.
- **Talbot and Falaise**: Falaise "yielded to Xaintrailles and Bureau in return for the unconditional liberation of its captain, John Talbot" (p. 398 / PDF 411) — Talbot was Falaise's captain, which explains the term. No jubilee pilgrimage in Barker; that GAP stays open.
- **Finance ratios** (ch. 6 §V, ch. 10): Norman estates, December 1437, "300,000l.t. (£17.5m)... for five quarters" (p. 263 / PDF 278); spring 1447, "a grant of just 30,000l.t." (p. 360 / PDF 373). Barker states the 1439 French levy as "a fixed levy of 1,200,000l.t. (£70m)... annually" (p. 278 / PDF 293) — the textbook sentence the spine's 2 September ruling holds as shorthand; T3, not to override Contamine.
- **Basin bought** (§VI, spine §8(e)6): "Twelve days after surrendering Lisieux he did homage to Charles VII at Verneuil and was appointed a member of the great council with an annual pension of 1000l.t." (p. 383 / PDF 396). The chronicler who gives Talbot "temerity and providence" was the reconquest's pensioner.
- Ch. 5/9: Henry's cession of Maine, "a private letter conceived, written and sent in secret" (p. 337 / PDF 350); parliament's 1446 disclaimer, "none of the lords or your other subjects... have in any way stirred or moved you" (p. 339 / PDF 352; PR xi, 471–2).

## 5. What it CONTRADICTS

**(i) Cherbourg was sold, not priced.** Ch. 6 §IV makes Cherbourg's "roughly five and a half weeks — the longest resistance of the entire campaign" the proof that "the *maximum* duration any fortress achieved against the train... was shorter than the *typical* duration of a serious pre-artillery siege." Barker: "underpinning the usual public treaty of capitulation was a private deal which reveals that Gower had, in fact, been bribed to surrender... So the last bastion of the English kingdom of France did not fall after a heroic but futile defence: it was simply sold to the French" (p. 399 / PDF 412; CMSM ii, 237–9). And the epilogue's opening: "there was nothing inevitable about Cherbourg's fall. It had held out before, and it could have done so again. After all, Calais would spend another century in English hands" (p. 401 / PDF 414). *The book's best answer*: the bribe is the composition market in cash — the same instrument as Bordeaux 1451's pensions — and the train on the sands set the price at which selling beat holding; her Calais counterfactual concedes the point in the book's own terms, since Calais survived on "the financial support and political muscle of the powerful mercantile lobby". Fair, but the superlative must go: the census's longest resistance ended in a purchase and cannot carry "maximum duration" as ballistic evidence.

**(ii) The Grey Tower starved.** §V's central exhibit — Verneuil's tower "impregnable so long as there was food within", which "surrendered anyway", so that "the entire argument of this chapter sits inside it" — is on Barker's reading of Chartier, Blondel and Berry a starvation surrender: the tower "lacked a suitable stock of provisions"; "when they finally capitulated, through lack of food, only thirty men were found inside" (pp. 381, 383 / PDF 394, 396). Talbot's relief failed because he was "Hopelessly outnumbered and outgunned" at Harcourt (p. 381). *The book's best answer*: Verneuil is a relief-clock case, not a repricing case — an unprovisioned truce-era garrison (Somerset's dispatch made flesh) that surrendered when relief was shown impossible. That still serves the chapter, but the Berry Herald sentence must be re-read as the chronicler's explanation of why it fell, not as a paradox. The answer is good only after the rewrite.

**(iii) Compressions read as provisioning failure.** "Even the supposedly impregnable Château Gaillard, which had previously required many months to starve into submission, capitulated on 23 November after a siege of just five weeks, proof, surely, of the lack of provisions and munitions in the Norman garrisons about which Beaufort had complained" (p. 394 / PDF 407). Her same-wall comparison yields finance, not guns. *Best answer*: this is §V's third counter-argument, already conceded and claimed; but the same-wall proofs must be restricted to walls where guns are documented (Harfleur, Bayeux, Caen), not extended to every compression.

**(iv) "A list with one entry."** §VII's census of who fielded a siege train has one entry. Barker has English trains at Tancarville 1437, Dieppe 1442–43, Somerset 1443, Kyriell 1450. *Best answer*: §III's hardware-versus-institution distinction absorbs this, and Barker supplies the proof (the train abandoned at Avranches). But the sentence is false at the level of guns and must say "establishment".

**(v) Barker's causal ranking.** Her epilogue puts demobilisation and leadership first: "both England and Normandy had been lulled into a false sense of security by the truce. When war came they were unprepared, disorganised and did not have either the will or the means to resist" (p. 402 / PDF 415). Artillery is one of four adopted methods. No spine ruling is reached — §8(a)'s co-evolution admits a multi-causal collapse — but a reviewer holding Barker will say the chapter over-weights the guns against the truce. *Best answer*: Curry's sentence the chapter already quotes; and Beaufort's speech, which names the enemy's "great and well equipped army" and "sixty thousand francs-archers" (p. 366) — the English saw the machine's edge and could not fund the reply. That cuts against §V's "the same English record never names the machine": narrow it to "never names the coupling".

## 7. Verdict

Barker changes the chapter's weight and its discipline, not the book's direction: she supplies the Lancastrian administrative record the census needs and, in the same pages, the deflationary reading — provisioning, leadership, purchase — that a specialist reviewer will bring, so the chapter must now distinguish the same-wall proofs where guns are documented from the compressions where hunger and bribery did the work. The single most important thing the author should know is that two of chapter 6's set-piece exhibits, Cherbourg's "longest resistance" and Verneuil's "impregnable" tower, are on her account a sale and a starvation, and both passages need rewriting before the mechanism can rest on them. Her compensating gift is the English side of the ledger — two hundred guns at Dieppe and a train abandoned at Avranches — which proves the chapter's real claim, that the decisive object was an institution and not a park of cannon, better than anything the draft currently cites.
