# Assessment — Halil İnalcık, *The Ottoman Empire: The Classical Age 1300–1600* (Weidenfeld & Nicolson, 1973; Phoenix pbk 2000, tr. Itzkowitz and Imber)

*Read in full (nineteen chapters, chronology, notes, bibliography; index skimmed). OCR: running heads interleaved into paragraphs, diacritics mangled ("Gandarli"), sultans' numerals broken, a few dates corrupted ("1595-1603" for Mehmed III, p. 60); Tables 1 (p. 82) and 6 (p. 170) unreadable; maps lost. Nothing pinned rests on a garbled passage. Pins give printed page; PDF page = printed + 12 for pp. 3–132 and + 40 for pp. 133–257 (plates intervene); page breaks are double form-feeds, so the brief's count rule must be halved.*

## 1. The book in two paragraphs

İnalcık's book is the standard anatomy of the classical Ottoman state, framed as a near-eastern-state thesis: the frontier principality "became, by the end of the sixteenth century, an empire in the traditions of the ancient near-eastern states, such as the Sassanid and, more especially, the Abbasid Empire" (p. 3 / PDF p. 15). Its load-bearing sentence: "The two fundamental institutions of the classical Ottoman Empire were the slave and timar systems. They defined the state's military and political order, taxation system and forms of land tenure, determining its whole social and political structure" (p. 47 / PDF p. 59). The kul system "was the foundation stone of the Ottoman state" (p. 77 / PDF p. 89); beneath both lay the principle that "'the reaya and the land belong to the sultan'... It was in fact the cornerstone of the autocratic and centralizing Ottoman regime" (p. 73 / PDF p. 85).

It is anatomy, not causal argument, but its narrative carries a periodisation: Part I ch. IV is "The Definitive Establishment of the Ottoman Empire, 1453–1526", and "Mehmed the Conqueror was the true founder of the Ottoman Empire" (p. 29 / PDF p. 41); its close is decline, from the 1580s "The institutions of the classical near-eastern state disintegrated" (p. 52 / PDF p. 64). Published 1973, before Kafadar and the anti-"decline" revisionists; İnalcık's 1994 *Economic and Social History* supersedes the fiscal figures, which are Barkan's.

## 2. What it CONFIRMS

- **Ch. 5 §IV — the [GAP] formulation, now first-hand.** "The most potent factor was the Ottoman kul – slave – system. In particular the Janissary corps, whose numbers had risen to six or seven thousand, gave the Ottoman sultan an undisputed superiority over his rivals" (p. 18 / PDF p. 30; context: the recovery of 1402–53). The gap closes.
- **Ch. 5 §IV — 1446.** "Finally, Çandarlı Halil engineered a Janissary revolt, removed Mehmed II and his advisers from power, and in May 1446 once again brought Murad II to the throne" (p. 21 / PDF p. 33); Murad "returned to the throne only when convinced that this was the wish of the Janissaries" (p. 63 / PDF p. 75). Buçuktepe is not named.
- **Ch. 5 §IV — the vacancy.** "The twelve-year-old sultan, Mehmed II, was not in control of events. A power struggle broke out between the grand vizier Çandarlı Halil and the sultan's tutors, Zaganos and the beylerbeyi of Rumelia, Şihabeddin" (p. 20 / PDF p. 32); "The Byzantines and the pope, not wishing to waste this opportunity, encouraged the Hungarian war party" (p. 20).
- **Ch. 5 §IV / Ch. 9 — the over-mighty servant; personalised sovereignty.** "grand viziers such as Çandarlı Halil, Gedik Ahmed or Yemişçi Hasan, who had the support of the Janissaries, could exercise unchallenged authority" (p. 63 / PDF p. 75). "When a sultan died all appointments and legal regulations were considered null and void until the new sultan confirmed them" (p. 61 / PDF p. 73).
- **Ch. 11 §IV — Çandarlı's fall and the kul vizierate.** "Immediately after the conquest, Çandarlı was arrested, charged with treason and executed" (p. 26 / PDF p. 38); "Zaganos had his rival executed and replaced him as the grand vizier" (p. 97 / PDF p. 109); the classical slave grand vizier begins with Mahmud Pasha, 1455–68 (p. 95).
- **Ch. 11 §IV — Janissary strength.** Six or seven thousand (p. 18); c. 1475 "the Janissaries six thousand" against 39,000 sipahis (p. 108 / PDF p. 120); Table 2: 10,000 in 1480, 12,789 in 1568, 37,627 in 1609 (p. 83 / PDF p. 95). "Five to ten thousand" is exactly İnalcık's spread.
- **Ch. 11 §IV — fratricide.** "In his kânûnnâme, therefore, Mehmed the Conqueror codified a practice which in fact had been general since the early years of the empire: 'For the welfare of the state, the one of my sons to whom God grants the sultanate may lawfully put his brothers to death'" (p. 59 / PDF p. 71). Authenticity assumed, as in Kafadar; the second kanunname is "about 1476" (p. 72).
- **Ch. 5 frame / Ch. 6 / Ch. 10 — 1453 as an artillery event; the prior standing army.** "The Conqueror battered the city with cannons, larger than any that had yet been seen. The storming of the city walls, the strongest fortifications of the Middle Ages, was a victory for these modern weapons. Ottoman and western sources agree that the Turks entered the city in a general attack through a breach in the walls opened by cannon" (p. 23 / PDF p. 35). "the Janissary corps, the first standing army in Europe. The sultan had formed the corps from prisoners of war, after the capture of Adrianople" (p. 11 / PDF p. 23).

## 3. What it CORRECTS

1. **Manuscript (Ch. 5 §IV) says Murad II, "weary of the throne, abdicated in 1444... and retired"; İnalcık p. 20 (PDF p. 32) says:** the Byzantines "were sheltering Orhan, a grandson of Bayezid, presenting a threat of civil war which caused grave concern to Murad. In 1444, while still in good health, he abdicated in favour of his son Mehmed, hoping thus to establish him securely on the throne." On Kafadar's correction 3: İnalcık agrees "weary" is unsupported but gives a dynastic, not a factional, motive — while supplying Kafadar's vocabulary: "the real power had passed to the war party" (p. 22 / PDF p. 34). Write both: a father's insurance against a pretender, captured by the war party.
2. **Manuscript (Ch. 11 §IV) "the last of the old aristocratic vizieral dynasty"; İnalcık p. 21: "Çandarlı Halil, who had risen from the ranks of the ulema"; p. 95: "a bureaucrat from the ulema"; p. 65: "The first Ottoman viziers were from the ulema."** Agrees with Kafadar's correction 1 outright.
3. **Manuscript (Ch. 11 §IV) "canonically one per forty households"; İnalcık p. 78 (PDF p. 90) carries no ratio.** Boys "between the ages of eight and twenty", "only the children of Christian villagers engaged in agriculture, excluding urban children and any only child"; "levies every three to seven years, according to need. One source estimates the number of boys taken annually in the devşirme in the sixteenth century as a thousand; another source sets it at three thousand annually." Source the ratio elsewhere or cut it; the per-levy [GAP] closes here.
4. **Manuscript (Ch. 11 §IV) "Mehmed II manufactured his service class from raw material"; İnalcık dates the material earlier and the rule later.** The devşirme "was in operation already at the end of the fourteenth century" (p. 78); Bayezid I appointed "slaves brought up in his own Palace" to annexed principalities (p. 15 / PDF p. 27); and the slave monopoly of the vizierate was imposed *on* the sultan in 1481: the Janissaries "forced the new sultan to accept the condition that henceforth he would elevate only men of slave status to the grand vizierate" (pp. 77–78 / PDF pp. 89–90). Agrees with Kafadar's correction 2 and his 1481–82 sealing.
5. **Manuscript (Ch. 11 §I, §V) "1470–1530"; İnalcık's "definitive establishment" is 1453–1526 (p. 23), with the instruments a century older:** "The fundamental principles of the kânûn-i osmânî had been formulated by the end of the fourteenth century, even before their codification in the kânûnnâme of Mehmed the Conqueror" (p. 73). On Kafadar's correction 4 İnalcık splits the difference: the window overlaps the manuscript's; the instruments do not.
6. **The confiscation figure — the two T2 authorities differ.** Kafadar "more than a thousand villages" (dervish-held); İnalcık "some twenty thousand villages and farms, previously held as vakıf or emlak" (p. 30 / PDF p. 42), c. 1470, "in order to increase the number of timar-holding cavalrymen" (p. 109 / PDF p. 121); Bayezid II reversed most of it (p. 109). Carry İnalcık's as the total, Kafadar's as the dervish subset, Özel against both.
7. Not carried: the 1 June date; "first grand vizier... to be executed" (İnalcık names only later executions "for coveting the sultanate", 1536 and 1614, p. 95). Ch. 10's "two generations" understates 1361 by one.

## 4. What it ADDS

- **The Ottoman guns before 1453 (Ch. 6; Ch. 5 §IV).** "the Ottomans were quick to adopt from their enemies the superior weapons of the west – cannon and muskets... The Ottomans used large cannons in the siege of Constantinople in 1422, and muskets at Varna in 1444" (p. 21 / PDF p. 33): a Balkan-war acquisition synchronous with the Bureaus.
- **The peace party staked on the walls (Ch. 6 §VI; spine §8(a)).** "the dispute continued between Çandarlı, who maintained that the siege would not be successful and should be raised immediately, and the sultan and the military group whose future depended upon its success" (p. 26 / PDF p. 38). The breach refuted the accommodation party; the arrest followed "immediately" — the ratchet's object, Ottoman-side. Mehmed's aim: Constantinople "would assure him of the prestige and authority necessary to create an empire" (p. 22).
- **The firearms line to Çaldıran and Cairo (Ch. 6, beside Ibn Iyas).** "it was largely the use of firearms which enabled Selim to achieve his decisive victories against Ismail in Iran and against the Mamluks of Egypt" (p. 33 / PDF p. 45); Uzun Hasan "lacked" them and Ismail asked Venice for artillery (pp. 28, 32).
- **State arms industry (Ch. 10; Ch. 12 mirror table).** "To meet its own need for firearms and powder the state created, in Istanbul, factories employing hundreds of labourers, financed by state capital and directed by government-appointed commissioners. During the Cyprus campaign of 1571 the Kağıthane gunpowder factory in Istanbul produced seventeen tons of gunpowder a month. Private individuals, however, did not imitate this model" (p. 160 / PDF p. 200). Gunners 100 (1480), 1,204 (1568) (Table 2, p. 83); English tin and steel "vitally important for the Ottoman arms industry" (p. 138 / PDF p. 178).
- **State finance — check against Pamuk before hardening (Ch. 11 §IV; Appendix B).** 1432: revenue "2,500,000 ducats" (p. 21); 1528: "9,650,000 gold ducats, of which only 37 per cent was distributed as timars. About 50 per cent belonged directly to the sultan... The sultan distributed the greater part of the central Treasury's revenues to the Janissaries and kapıkulu cavalry as salaries paid in cash" (p. 116 / PDF p. 128); 1534 central Treasury "five million gold ducats" (p. 49). Mehmed II's fiscal move: "he had repeatedly debased the silver coinage and tightened financial controls" (p. 30 / PDF p. 42).
- **The timar as the pre-artillery fiscal workaround — the disanalogy re-grounded (Ch. 11 §IV).** "The need to support a great imperial army on the foundations of a mediaeval economy gave rise to this system... Shortage of coin was a fundamental problem of near-eastern empires... the state had difficulty in financing its great undertakings and, especially, in maintaining a large standing army" (p. 107 / PDF p. 119); "no hierarchy or bond of vassalage" (p. 114), "no question of blood nobility" (p. 115); "The need for land to distribute as timars constantly forced the state to undertake new conquests" (p. 116).
- **The fiscal-military circle stated in 1069 (Ch. 11 §I or Coda).** Kutadgu Bilig: "'To control the state requires a large army. To support the troops requires great wealth. To obtain this wealth the people must be prosperous. For the people to be prosperous the laws must be just'" (p. 66 / PDF p. 78); Yazıcıoğlu, 1430s: "a sultan could secure his position only by possessing a Treasury and slaves" (p. 77); Kafadar's losers' chronicle is here aimed at Bayezid I (p. 65).
- **The fourth move, which ch. 11 §IV lacks.** Zâviyes abolished because "so long as they remained as vakıfs the state could not use their lands for financial and military purposes" (p. 150 / PDF p. 190); "the government's control of vakıfs enabled it to force the Mevlevis into obedience" (p. 201 / PDF p. 241); 1597, "a mosque built in each village" and doubters executed (p. 182 / PDF p. 222).
- **The fifth move — the register, not the press (Ch. 11 §V; Ch. 12).** "as early as 1555 Busbecq reported that the Turks esteemed it a sin to print religious books" (p. 174 / PDF p. 214); against that, at each accession "a general cadastral survey was ordered, showing the sources of taxation throughout the empire, the legal status of all subjects, and their tax-exemptions" (p. 62 / PDF p. 74), and "literally millions of documents... are proof that the Ottoman Empire was a bureaucratic state" (p. 102 / PDF p. 114). The Ottoman fifth cell reads "cadastre; print refused" — a disanalogy ch. 11 §V must state.
- **Smaller items.** Pension and exile beside Bergavenny (p. 27 / PDF p. 39); for ch. 9, the sultans "were little more than cogs in a machine" (p. 85 / PDF p. 97); for ch. 4/10, İnalcık's own parallel: "Anatolia was in the same situation as France after the hundred years war, with companies of discharged soldiers pillaging the land" (p. 50 / PDF p. 62).
- **Appendix B row.** İnalcık fills every cell: intermediaries (frontier beys, vakıf/emlak families, tribes, ahis, Çandarlı, dervish lodges); instruments (corps 1361, palace slaves under Bayezid I, timar from pronoia, kanun by 1400, kanunnames 1453 and c. 1476, land review c. 1470, pension/exile, garrisons, vakıf control); army and revenue figures above; fifth move = cadastre; sealing 1453 and 1481.

## 5. What it CONTRADICTS

**At full strength.** İnalcık's Ottoman state is consolidated before the gun and dissolved by it. Every instrument in his anatomy is fourteenth-century (pp. 11, 65, 73, 78); cannon arrive as borrowed kit in the 1420s (p. 21). Worse for the mechanism than absence is reversal. When the firearms-driven fiscal transition does run, in his decline chapter, it destroys the settlement: the timar cavalry "formed a mediaeval army whose day passed when it met the German infantry equipped with firearms" (p. 48 / PDF p. 60); "In order to pay the Janissaries their monthly salary and the sekban troops their daily wage, the state had to amass an ever-increasing amount of coin in the central Treasury. To meet these requirements, some of the lands assigned as timars were brought under direct Treasury control" (p. 49 / PDF p. 61); "Money taxes became a principal source of state revenue, a development which revolutionized the tax system" (p. 50 / PDF p. 62). The product: provincial governors raising local taxes for sekbans, which "encouraged minor military commanders... to operate independently and plunder the people on their own account" (p. 50); the celali wars; provincial Janissaries who "came to represent a new upper class... and to form the basis of the class which was to dominate the provinces in the eighteenth century" (p. 51 / PDF p. 63). "The empire had, in fact, exceeded its material capabilities" (p. 44 / PDF p. 56). The chain weapon → cash → central treasury → suppression of revenue-holding intermediaries ran, and its output was a new stratum of intermediaries. That reaches the DEFEND-tier mechanism (spine §6) wherever the Ottoman case is carried as its limit case, and refutes ch. 11 §V's "none was executable without it": all five moves were executed on an in-kind base that existed because cash finance was impossible (p. 107).

Lesser: the near-eastern-state frame makes the Ottoman "new monarchy" a re-instantiation of a millennium-old template; and the fifth move is the cadastre with print refused (p. 174), against ch. 11 §V's "the technology arrived on schedule".

**The book's best answer.** The Ottoman state demonstrates the *requirement* twice over. First, a polity that had built household, register and cash-paid core by pre-artillery means could cast and feed the guns and use them to lock its settlement — Çandarlı staked his position on the walls holding and lost it "immediately" (p. 26); Selim bought Çaldıran and Cairo with firearms (p. 33). Second, the base beneath the guns was revenue assignment, adopted because "Shortage of coin was a fundamental problem" (p. 107); when firearm infantry had to be paid in coin at scale the settlement converted late, in crisis, and re-dispersed power. That is the failure mode appearing exactly where the condition — a cash settlement of the 1439–46 kind — fails: the Russian demonstration case a century and a half early. Is the answer good? Adequate, and stronger than the Kafadar answer alone, on three conditions: ch. 11 §IV stops calling the Ottoman case "the most complete answer to the over-mighty subject the fifteenth century produced" without adding that the answer was pre-artillery, in-kind, and undone by the gun's fiscal demand; §V's closing sentence exempts the Ottomans; and Appendix B's Ottoman row carries a "conversion failed, 1580s–1600s" cell pinned to pp. 48–51. Kept as drafted, the Ottoman variant is refuted by its own standard authority.

Ducat figures are Barkan's via İnalcık — cross-check with Pamuk.

## 7. Verdict

İnalcık changes the Ottoman comparator's weight and register, not the book's direction: he closes the ch. 5 and ch. 11 gaps at first hand and confirms nearly every Kafadar correction, but his decline chapter carries the mechanism running in the Ottoman Empire with the sign reversed — firearm infantry demanding coin an in-kind fiscal base could not supply, re-dispersing power to a new provincial stratum. The Ottoman case must therefore be written as the requirement demonstrated twice: a pre-artillery consolidation that could build and lock with the guns, and a fiscal settlement that could not convert when the guns' bill came due. The single thing the author must know: the standard authority describes the classical Ottoman state as made without artillery and unmade by its fiscal demand, so ch. 11 §IV's "limit case" and §V's "none was executable without it" cannot stand for the Ottomans as drafted.
