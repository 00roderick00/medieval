# Assessment — Joseph R. Strayer, *On the Medieval Origins of the Modern State* (Princeton, 1970)

*Read whole (c. 33k words). Printed page P = PDF page P+7 throughout the body; pins give "p. N / PDF N+7". OCR is clean except that the three chapter headings and their opening words were dropped (pp. 3, 57, 89 begin mid-sentence) and "Cuttino" is rendered "dittino" (pp. 81, 85). Nothing load-bearing is garbled.*

## 1. The book in two paragraphs

Strayer's thesis is that the modern state is a European invention of the period 1100–1600, and that its decisive work was done early, by lawyers and accountants, not late, by soldiers. "The modern state, wherever we find it today, is based on the pattern which emerged in Europe in the period 1100 to 1600" (p. 12 / PDF 19). He looks for four "signs": persistence in space and time; "permanent, impersonal institutions"; "agreement on the need for an authority which can give final judgments"; and "acceptance of the idea that this authority should receive the basic loyalty of its subjects" (p. 10 / PDF 17). All four, he argues, were present in England and France by 1300: "Sovereignty existed in fact long before it could be described in theory (1300 AD as opposed to 1550)" (p. 9 / PDF 16), and "The two pillars of the medieval state were the Treasury and the High Court; by the end of the thirteenth century both institutions were manned by experienced, professionally minded officials" (p. 33 / PDF 40). The sequence is internal first, external last: "High courts of justice and Treasury Departments existed long before Foreign Offices and Departments of Defense" (p. 26 / PDF 33).

The second and third chapters treat 1300–1450 as a crisis *inside* already-existing states — depression, plague, an exhausting war, an estate-manager bureaucracy unable to assess taxes, magnates feeding on the state — and the "New Monarchies" as a recovery made by a change of attitude among the possessing classes rather than by new instruments. The sentence the book must reckon with: "Neither administrative gimmicks nor military power explain the success of the sixteenth-century state. Intelligent use of existing resources and increased cooperation between rulers and subjects were the essential ingredients of the 'New Monarchies'" (p. 92 / PDF 99). Artillery, Charles VII, the *compagnies d'ordonnance* and the *taille* do not appear in the book at all.

## 2. What it CONFIRMS

**Coda §I — "polities with latent fiscal-administrative capacity to mobilise"; Chapter 6 — "fiscal and administrative capacities that had existed before the guns and had not, before the guns, been decisive."** Strayer is the canonical T2 description of that latent capacity: the Exchequer "early in the twelfth century" (p. 37 / PDF 44); writ, jury and circuit judges; the professional Parlement. The manuscript's concession now has its authority.

**Chapter 3 §II and Chapter 5 §V — the affinity feeds on the state; "the law ran in the king's name to the end."** Strayer on bastard feudalism: "the purpose of the political game was not to create a new government, but rather to get control of some part of the existing government… The basic structure of government had to be preserved in order to generate the revenues sought by the upper classes" (pp. 62–63 / PDF 69–70).

**Chapter 5 — Henry VI and Charles VI paired as one syndrome.** "This kind of irresponsibility on the part of the policymakers reached its height when the king was weak or incapable, for example, in the reigns of Charles VI of France or Henry VI of England" (p. 75 / PDF 82) — a T2 pairing of the chapter's two cases, made in 1970 without the book's frame.

**Chapter 8 §VII and Chapter 12 — "hierarchical plurality": the sixteenth century "did not close the church courts or the manorial courts; it ranked them."** Strayer describes the original ranking, two centuries earlier: "Theoretically the lower (baronial) court still had jurisdiction; actually the order of the superior court usually settled the case… A lord whose decisions could be overruled was a lord who had lost much of his authority" (p. 30 / PDF 37). On the conciliar courts, England saw the process "most completely institutionalized in the creation of Conciliar courts such as the Court of Star Chamber or the Court of Requests" (p. 98 / PDF 105).

**Chapter 11 §II — the fisc "personalised… precisely in order to be institutionalised."** "The kings of the late fifteenth century quite sensibly decided that it was easier to reform the policymakers than the bureaucrats" (p. 92 / PDF 99); "a new bureaucracy began to crystallize around the professional members of the Council… more amenable to the wishes of the ruler and more flexible in its procedures than the old, corporate bureaucracy inherited from the Middle Ages" (p. 94 / PDF 101). The Act in Restraint of Appeals is read as the judicial idea of sovereignty giving way to "possession of executive power" (p. 102 / PDF 109).

**Chapter 10 §VIII / spine §7 — "buy, license, or align."** "As soon as the modern world touches an area, the inhabitants must either form a state or take refuge in the shadow of an already existing one" (p. 4 / PDF 11).

## 3. What it CORRECTS

1. **Chapter 10 §III says "The constitutional theory of French taxation before 1439 was consent: the Estates granted, for a purpose and a term, and the grant lapsed."** Strayer p. 54 / PDF 61: by 1300 "the right of the king to impose taxes, especially taxes for the defense of the kingdom, was generally recognized… it was almost impossible to deny completely a royal demand for money"; n. 48 cites Pierre Jame of Montpellier admitting "the king could levy taxes without consent for the defense of the realm," echoing Boniface VIII's *Etsi de statu* (1297). The pre-1439 theory was not simple consent but consent-with-a-defence-of-the-realm override — which is exactly the doctrine the 1446 "jusqu'à la conclusion de la paix" formula rode. The correction *helps* ruling §8(a): permanence was made inside an existing doctrine, not against a consent constitution.

2. **Introduction §I: the mechanism "compelled the permanent taxation, the standing institutions and the administrative reach that we retrospectively call the state"; Chapter 6 §VII: the payers were "the ones we would shortly begin calling states."** Strayer: standing institutions and general taxation existed by 1200 in England ("the imposition of direct taxes on the entire realm," p. 42–43 / PDF 49–50); "by 1300 the king of England… had, and knew that he had, sovereign power" (p. 44 / PDF 51); the late thirteenth century is "the period in which the concept (if not the word) of sovereignty appeared" (p. 35 / PDF 42). The phrasing must be narrowed to what 1440–1500 actually added (see §5).

3. **Chapter 1 §II: c. 1200 "no internal tendency was visibly carrying [the plural order] toward the exclusive territorial state."** Strayer's whole first chapter is the visible tendency: "in each country the theoretical supremacy of the king became a little more real every decade" (p. 31 / PDF 38); Philip Augustus "the real founder of the French state" (pp. 50–51 / PDF 57–58). The chapter already concedes Capetian France and Henry II's England in a parenthesis; the parenthesis must govern the sentence.

4. **Chapter 3 §II carries McFarlane's view that the disorder "traced not to the institution but to inadequate kingship."** Strayer offers a rival T2 cause: "the stagnant or declining economy placed the possessing classes under heavy financial pressure" (p. 62 / PDF 69). Record the disagreement per CLAUDE.md §5.

5. **Minor.** Chapter 10 §V: "Louis XI's France is the first polity in this book's story in which the crown is systematically stronger than any of its subjects" — Strayer has the French king "strong enough to attack and defeat the strongest provincial ruler" by 1200 (p. 50 / PDF 57). Say "first in this book's period."

## 4. What it ADDS

- **Chapter 10 §III [GAP: Wolfe; Henneman] — why the fiscal settlement needed new machinery.** Strayer's estate-manager thesis is the best short statement of the legibility problem: "when rulers began to depend on taxation… what was needed was a new estimate (of hearths, land, income, and so forth) every time there was a new tax… The old bureaucracy could not adjust" (pp. 69–70 / PDF 76–77); by mid-century "the number of hearths listed in France or the amount of personal property assessed for taxation in England bore no relationship to the real facts" (p. 71 / PDF 78); England's 1334 surrender to a conventional £34,000 (p. 68 / PDF 75); the Chambre des Aides split from the Chambre des Comptes (p. 72 / PDF 79); tax-farmers and the gabelle "oppressed the people without increasing the king's revenues" (p. 73 / PDF 80). This is the pre-1439 baseline the *élus/généralités* passage lacks, and the medieval original of Appendix C's legibility indicators.

- **Chapter 8 §II — the Pastons.** "barons, prelates, and the self-governing towns soon found that if they agreed to play the game according to the new rules, they could often thwart the government more effectively by legal obstructionism than by armed resistance" (p. 63 / PDF 70), with the Gévaudan suit of 1269–1341 (n. 57) as the case. The same sentence frames Chapter 11 move two.

- **Chapter 12 / ruling §8(b) — the per-bloc hybrid.** Strayer's contrast of the English and French models is a ready-made T2 typology for how consolidators hold heterogeneous provinces: Philip Augustus "allowed each province to keep its own customs and institutions but sent out men from Paris to fill all important provincial offices" (p. 51 / PDF 58); "France was a mosaic state, made up of many pieces, and the bureaucracy was the cement" (p. 53 / PDF 60). And the caution: "Well into the seventeenth century the best-organized states were in a sense only federations of counties or provinces" (p. 100 / PDF 107). The EU is a mosaic-state candidate on Strayer's terms.

- **Chapter 11 §V–VI — legibility before force as the Tudor instrument.** "Internal security was preserved not by the use of force, but by the acquisition of timely knowledge and the establishment of a network of personal relationships between the Secretaries… and influential local men" (pp. 95–96 / PDF 102–103). Also the collegiality device — several Secretaries so that "there could be no Foreign Secretary and no Foreign Office to interfere with the monarch's direct control" (p. 103 / PDF 110).

- **Chapter 1 §II / Part III — a state-scoring instrument.** Strayer's four signs (p. 10 / PDF 17) and "the turning point was the recognition of the need for a final authority, not the possession of a 'monopoly of power'" (p. 9 / PDF 16) give the blocs a non-Weberian test.

- **Against Tilly (ruling §8(a) differentiation).** "War sometimes stimulates the growth of administrative institutions, but this particular war was so exhausting for both sides that it discouraged the normal development of the apparatus of the state" (p. 60 / PDF 67). A T2 witness that war-in-general is not the motor — the book's two-stroke differentiation.

## 5. What it CONTRADICTS

At full strength, three contradictions, the first two reaching spine §6 DEFEND tier and ruling §8(a).

**(i) The state was made 1100–1300, before gunpowder, by law and accounting.** "Thus in the centuries between 1000 and 1300 some of the essential elements of the modern state began to appear. Political entities… gained legitimacy by enduring through generations. Permanent institutions for financial and judicial business were established. Groups of professional administrators were developed" (p. 34 / PDF 41). "Instead of scattered islands of political power, each almost isolated from the other, there was a solid block of territory in which one ruler had final authority" (p. 31 / PDF 38). By 1300 "loyalty to the state was stronger than any other loyalty" (p. 57 / PDF 64). The Introduction's claim that the plural order "was ended by an identifiable mechanism, at an identifiable price, in an identifiable half-century" — 1440–1500 — is, on Strayer's account, two centuries late and wrong about the agent.

**(ii) Neither standing armies nor military power made the New Monarchies.** "It is doubtful if any medieval state could have supported even a small permanent army" (p. 87 / PDF 94). "The creation of standing armies, emphasized by some historians, was more important for external than internal affairs. England had no standing army; the French standing army was small and stationed mainly on the frontiers… Neither administrative gimmicks nor military power explain the success of the sixteenth-century state" (pp. 91–92 / PDF 98–99). "There was no need to create new institutions if the old institutions could be made to work better. There was no need to use force when most of the subjects were ready to obey of their own free will" (p. 91 / PDF 98). And on England specifically: after 1453 "England avoided any serious war for over a century" (p. 90 / PDF 97). This cuts Chapter 11 §V's closing line ("each move spends the fiscal-military surplus the artillery state generated, and none was executable without it") and spine §7's "the mechanism left them no alternative if they wanted to be a power" — the Tudors, on Strayer's reading, were not trying to be a continental power and did not build the instrument.

**(iii) The fifteenth-century disorder was not a return of plurality.** The baronial resurgence "did not create a really feudal situation" (p. 62 / PDF 69); English rebels of 1215, 1258 and 1297 "had no thought of destroying the unity of England" (p. 46 / PDF 53). If the fifteenth century was a crisis *within* states that already held their subjects' loyalty, the 1450 hollow crown is a weak state, not a non-state.

**The book's best answer.** On (i) and (iii), the answer is already in ruling §8(a) of 2 September: the ratchet's object is "everyone else's ability to reverse it." Strayer's state of 1300 was real and reversible, and he says so: "while the sovereign state of 1300 was stronger than any competing political form, it was still not very strong" (p. 57 / PDF 64); "No state had the military power, the bureaucratic personnel, or even the information needed to impose unpopular measures on opposition groups that had any political and social standing" (p. 61 / PDF 68). What 1440–1500 supplied was the military power that made the thirteenth-century settlement irreversible — and Strayer's own explanation of the turn is a confessed blank: "It is difficult to decide what factors changed the behavior of the possessing classes… Some of them may have been impressed by the failure of most late fifteenth-century rebellions" (p. 91 / PDF 98). The book's mechanism is the content of that "may have been impressed": rebellions failed because magnates could no longer hold walls or match the train. That answer is good. But it requires the Introduction and Chapter 1 §II to stop saying the plural order was *ended* in 1440–1500 and say it was *locked* — the trellis (p. 8 / PDF 15: institutions "may form a trellis on which the idea of the state can grow") was Strayer's; the ratchet is the book's.

On (ii), the answer is only partly good. For France the book has Contamine, published two years after Strayer, which rewrote precisely the period Strayer skips; Strayer's "small" French standing army and his silence on artillery are superseded evidence, and the manuscript should say so rather than argue. For England the book cannot answer Strayer with Contamine. Its honest position is spine §7's: England is the latecomer whose consolidation ran by law, bond and Chamber first, the arsenal following under Henry VIII — and "England avoided any serious war for over a century" is a fact Chapter 11 must carry, not smooth. The "Tudor turn" survives as the name of the settlement's *form*; as the name of a mechanism it survives only with France as the motor and England as the copy. The spine already says this; the draft chapters do not always.

## 6. Placement

- **Chapter 1 §II and §VI — T2, rival, engaged by name.** The strongest scholarly statement that the "substrate" was already a state; the baseline paragraph must meet him in the text. His 1300-versus-1550 dating belongs beside Osiander/Teschke in §VI.
- **Chapter 10 §III — T2, ally and corrector; named where the pre-1439 tax doctrine is restated.** The estate-manager and defence-of-the-realm passages (pp. 47, 54, 69–71) go into the §8(a) fiscal-settlement research job.
- **Chapter 11 §I–II and §V — T2, rival, engaged by name.** The "so little that is new" and "neither administrative gimmicks nor military power" sentences quoted and answered in the text; the Council/Secretaries material as ally evidence for §II.
- **Chapter 5 §III, Chapter 8 §II and §VII — T2, evidence, footnotes.**
- **Chapter 12 / ruling §8(b) — T2, evidence, named once** for the mosaic-state typology.
- **Appendix A §VII — T2, beside Green as the historiographical anchor,** with a one-line note that his fifteenth century is superseded by Contamine (1972) and Henneman, and that he never mentions artillery — which is the point of citing him.

## 7. Verdict

Strayer does not change the book's direction but forces a change in its register: the Introduction and Chapter 1 must stop claiming that 1440–1500 *ended* the plural order and claim instead, as ruling §8(a) already does, that artillery made a thirteenth-century settlement irreversible — the trellis was Strayer's, the ratchet is the book's. The single most important thing the author should know is that Strayer's own account of the late-fifteenth-century turn is a confessed blank ("It is difficult to decide what factors changed the behavior of the possessing classes," p. 91), so the book's mechanism fills the hole the canonical medievalist left rather than contradicting a settled account. The one thing it cannot answer with Contamine is England: "England had no standing army… England avoided any serious war for over a century" must be carried into Chapter 11 as a fact, and the Tudor turn defended as the settlement's form copied by a latecomer, not as the motor.
