# Assessment — Colin Morris, *The Papal Monarchy: The Western Church from 1050 to 1250* (Clarendon Press, 1989)

*Text: books2/Morris.txt, 693 PDF pages; printed page = PDF page − 19 throughout. OCR clean. Read in full: Introduction; ch. 1 §iii; ch. 4 §§iv–v; ch. 5; ch. 7 §§ii–iv; Part II introduction; ch. 9; ch. 16 §§iv–v; ch. 17 §§i–iii; ch. 22; Conclusions; bibliography notes on Ullmann and Berman; the military-orders pages (280, 482–3). Skimmed, not assessed: chs. 2–3, 6, 8, 10–15, 18–21 (monastic, crusade, pastoral, intellectual and diocesan chapters); nothing there bears on the manuscript.*

## 1. The book in two paragraphs

Morris's title is a provocation against his subject. "The title of this book expresses a paradox, not a fact. A papal monarchy was in principle and in practice inconceivable in medieval Europe" (p. 1 / PDF 20). What existed was a two-power theory under strain: "The ineffectiveness of state power at the beginning of our period meant that the clergy supervised activities which in the ancient or the modern world alike have been the business of the state," so that "Christendom was ruled by kings and princes under the supervision of the clergy, and especially of the Roman Church, which alone possessed the fullness of power" (pp. 1–2 / PDF 20–21). The spine is institutional: the Roman Church's growth "from the local church of Rome to a curia which recruited personnel from many parts of the west to run an international organization" (p. 581 / PDF 600), by a demand-led mechanism — appeals, judges delegate, decretals — Morris calls "rescript government."

The conclusion deflates the monarchy and inflates the institution. "We can well understand how such developments gave rise to the rhetoric of papal lordship and even of papal monarchy. We have also seen reasons why such rhetoric should not be taken too seriously… Absolutist claims which ignored these limitations were rare, unrealistic, and usually the polemical product of a critical situation" (pp. 581–2 / PDF 600–601). The curia's growth "can be ascribed mainly to two things. One was the demand throughout Europe for an organization which would offer protection, exemption, or relief to the great numbers of petitioners who addressed it… The other was the long tradition of alliance between Rome and creative movements" (p. 582 / PDF 601). Morris writes against Ullmann's hierocratic reading, recorded as "widely challenged" (p. 585 / PDF 604).

## 2. What it CONFIRMS

**Chapter 1 §II — "two genuine universalisms, each claiming the orbis."** P. 550 / PDF 569: "national sovereignty was inhibited by the claims to universal jurisdiction made by both empire and papacy. Frederick II asserted that God 'has set us above kings and kingdoms'." Liemar of Bremen on Gregory VII: "a dangerous man, who ordered bishops about as if they were his bailiffs" (p. 114 / PDF 133).

**Chapter 1 §II — the order "lacked… hierarchy among laws… a politics of boundary dispute, privilege, exemption and appeal."** P. 555 / PDF 574: "Two coherent jurisdictions, temporal and spiritual, now confronted one another." P. 233 / PDF 252: "Royal lawyers devised methods for defending their jurisdiction, such as writs of prohibition, and a great deal depended on the preference of litigants, who often could choose between the two tribunals."

**Chapter 1 §II — plurality durable, "no internal tendency… toward the exclusive territorial state."** P. 550 / PDF 569: "The process of their formation was still in its early stages in 1250 and was in progress only in parts of the west. In Germany, Italy, and Poland the opposite was happening: power was being devolved from the centre to princes, communes, and duchies." T2 authority on the other side of the correction assess/Strayer.md §3.3 entered against this sentence; record the disagreement per CLAUDE.md §5.

**Chapter 2 §II — Dictatus, Canossa, Worms.** P. 131 / PDF 150: "The Dictatus Pape asserted that the pope can depose emperors and absolve subjects from their allegiance, and these powers were actually exercised against Henry IV." Canossa: Henry, "after being obliged to wait for three days in the garb of a penitent… was absolved on 28 January 1077" (p. 116 / PDF 135). Worms, 23 September 1122: "The emperor surrendered 'all investiture by ring and staff'… the elect would receive the regalia from him by a sceptre" (p. 164 / PDF 183); as truce, "no more than half a victory" (p. 173 / PDF 192). The [GAP: Ullmann] can be closed from Morris, with §3's corrections.

**Chapter 2 §§I, III — the universalism as "rule-making, adjudicating, sanctioning"; deplatforming as excommunication.** P. 575 / PDF 594: "the concern of the western church to Christianize society… took the form not of issuing statutes but of extending the accessibility of its courts." The interdict on England (1208) "was widely observed… But strikes are an uncertain way of influencing public opinion, which was probably inclined to blame the church rather than the king" (p. 429 / PDF 448).

**Chapter 2 §III — the "Gregorian moment": officers hold office *of* the centre.** Bernold of Constance, p. 130 / PDF 149: the pope "has in no way deprived himself of his universal and principal power; just as the king has not diminished his royal power although he has divided his kingdom among different dukes, counts and judges."

## 3. What it CORRECTS

1. **Chapter 2 §II: "Gregory VII's *Dictatus Papae* (1075)."** Morris p. 112 / PDF 131 n. 147: "the text is undated… Perhaps spring 1076 is the more likely date. We do not know that Gregory composed the sentences; the existence of very similar texts in other manuscripts suggest that he did not." Write "1075 or 1076"; drop sole authorship.

2. **Chapter 2 §II: the Dictatus as "the monarchic answer"; the [GAP] asks Ullmann for "the Dictatus as monarchic sovereignty."** Morris p. 112: "its main purpose was not to provide a blueprint for papal absolutism, but to define the emergency powers inherent in the Roman see"; p. 129 / PDF 148: "The Dictatus Pape, which has sometimes (wrongly) been regarded as a definition of Gregory's reforming programme, was primarily a list of the special prerogatives of the Roman Church… the claiming of power to overrule them was a declaration of a state of emergency." Restate as emergency powers later read as monarchic, and cite Morris, not Ullmann.

3. **Chapter 2 §II: Worms "partitioning the symbols" — emperor "retained investiture with the sceptre."** True in Germany only: "Elsewhere in the empire far more of the imperial rights were signed away, and the only concession to the emperor was that a new bishop should do homage within six months of consecration" (p. 164 / PDF 183); in Italy the concessions "gravely eroded the prospects of imperial control there" (p. 173). The asymmetry is the better fact: Worms was already jurisdiction-by-jurisdiction, the shape §V claims for Epic–Apple.

4. **Chapter 2 §II: investiture as "the question it litigated."** P. 119 / PDF 138: "the traditional name for the whole dispute, 'the investiture contest', is wrong in implying that investitures were the primary issue… it was only after 1100 that the royal right of investiture became the central, and intractable, issue." The template survives for 1100–1122; do not backdate it.

5. **Chapter 2 §II: Canossa "measured the claim's reach."** Morris reads it as Henry's "coup": "it was not unprecedented for kings to do public penance… he lost nothing further by his reconciliation with Gregory" (pp. 116–17 / PDF 135–136); deposition proper came on 7 March 1080 (p. 118). Say "limits," or cut.

6. **Chapter 1 §II: Innocent III "claimed ultimate arbitration… at the apex's apex, in temporal ones."** P. 433 / PDF 452, on *Per venerabilem*: "while he did not normally exercise authority in secular affairs, he could intervene on occasions, *casualiter*… This important element in his thought forbids us to define Innocent's doctrine as a crudely hierocratic or monarchical one." *Novit* distinguishes "the king's judgment of feudal tenure (*iudicare de feudo*) and the church's decrees about sin (*decernere de peccato*)" (p. 433). And the apex is the century, not the man: "Innocent IV took the struggle against the emperor even further" (p. 568 / PDF 587). Write "occasional intervention in temporal affairs by reason of sin; apex in the thirteenth century, doctrine fixed by Innocent III."

Nothing else in the manuscript's papal material is wrong by Morris's account.

## 4. What it ADDS

**Closes [GAP: Morris], Chapter 1 §II.** Innocent, Sermo 2: the pope is "lower than God but higher than man… who judges all, and is judged by none" (p. 431 / PDF 450); Sermo 3: "the fulness of spiritual and breadth of temporal powers"; Reg. ii.209: Christ "left to Peter the government not only of the church but of the whole world" (p. 431). Morris: "even the few passages quoted here suggest strongly that his claims sometimes included the secular sphere. Few statesmen have ever limited their aspirations to a rigorously consistent scheme" (p. 432 / PDF 451).

**Partly closes [GAP: Nicholson/Forey], Chapter 1 §II.** P. 280 / PDF 299: *Pie postulatio voluntatis* (1113) "effectively made [the Hospital] the first self-governing international order"; "the Templars were the first uniformed force in the new Europe." P. 483 / PDF 502: "The Golden Bull of Rimini in 1226 granted their master, Hermann of Salza, the status of prince of the empire for his prospective conquests, and in 1234 the territory of the Knights was recognized as a papal fief." T2 general; Forey stays the specialist anchor.

**The papal fisc — the most important addition, for Chapter 2 §III ("a church with no divisions") and Part III's question whether canonical authority without material capability survives.** Hadrian IV: "no one can make war without pay" (p. 214 / PDF 233). The *Liber Censuum* (1192) implies "1,214 gold ounces. By international standards this was very small, amounting to less than 5 per cent of Richard I's annual income from England"; Peter's Pence yielded "just under £200" (p. 215 / PDF 234). "Papal taxation did not have much of a prehistory" (p. 558 / PDF 577); Honorius III's 1225 scheme to fund the curia by a prebend in every cathedral was rejected at Bourges and London. On the Papal State: "Innocent was the creator of the Papal State… It was more of a tribute to temporary imperial weakness than to the strength of the apostolic see. The Papal State was created on the battlefields of Sicily and Germany" (pp. 421–2 / PDF 440–441). This is the disanalogy Chapter 2 owes: the medieval universalism could not pay for force, and its territorial existence was a function of the territorial powers' balance.

**Rescript government — the T2 account of how a universalism acquires jurisdiction, for the Oversight Board passage (Chapter 2 §I).** P. 212 / PDF 231: rescripts "were issued in response to petitions from outside the curia, and… often simply echoed the wording of the request… At the heart of what may seem a very active administration the pope or king was passive." P. 217: "The idea of the papacy as an absolute monarchy able to decide at will the affairs of the provincial churches is an illusion." P. 574 / PDF 593: the pope as "'universal ordinary', accessible to everyone with a grievance and money to pursue it"; p. 575: "the litigants and the curia between them had conspired to create a system of appeals of enormous range."

**The ratchet, in the ecclesiastical register — Chapters 2 and 12.** P. 113 / PDF 132: "it was easier for the papacy to assume authority than to restore it once regional powers had lost the directing initiative."

**The seigneurial dispersal as the medieval original of the neomedieval one — Chapter 3.** P. 27 / PDF 46: "In the church as in the kingdoms the idea of corporate institutions with public functions had collapsed into bundles of rights owned by individual proprietors… the seigneurial revolution proceeded in parallel in the secular and spiritual spheres" — *Eigenkirchentum* rendered as "the privatization of churches." (Dated 950–1050, not 1200.)

**The territorial power's switches over the universalism — Chapter 2 §V, Chapter 12.** Pp. 232–3 / PDF 251–252: "The kings of England, Hungary, and Sicily claimed the right to exclude papal legates from their territories"; kings "occasionally suspended [appeals] altogether as a means of coercing the papacy, as Barbarossa did after the quarrel at Besançon." Louis IX would enforce excommunications only "provided that he were given full knowledge of the sentence in each case, so that he might judge whether it was just or not" (p. 557 / PDF 576). The gate ran the other way too: Fourth Lateran c. 46 required that before a clerical subsidy to the lay power "they shall first consult the Roman pontiff" (p. 551 n. 701) — the veto the Act of Supremacy removed.

**The annexation argued before it happened — Chapter 2 §VI.** The Berkshire rectors (1240): "the churches belong to the care of the pope, not to his dominion and ownership"; the protest for Louis IX (1247): "the endowments of the churches were the gift of kings and princes, at whose disposal they are in case of need" (p. 559 / PDF 578). Hubert Walter as "an early foretaste of Cardinal Wolsey" (p. 231 / PDF 250).

**The universal authority certifying the territorial sovereign — Chapters 1 and 9.** P. 550 / PDF 569: *Per venerabilem*'s teaching "that the king of France 'acknowledges no superior in temporal affairs' was incorporated into canon law."

**Appendix B row 2.** Appendix B is empty in the assembled manuscript. Morris fills the Church row: intermediaries (pp. 27, 224, 280); instruments (pp. 111, 213–14, 403, 422); fisc (pp. 215, 429, 558); limit — Papal State 1198–1209, *Rex pacificus* 1234, Lyon 1245, "the victim of its own success" after 1250 (pp. 421, 576, 582).

## 5. What it CONTRADICTS

**(i) The papal monarchy was not a sovereign, and Chapter 2's medieval template is stated in a register Morris rejects.** "A papal monarchy was in principle and in practice inconceivable" (p. 1); "The papacy was not surprisingly a monarchy of its period, far removed from the absolutism which became possible in later centuries" (p. 213 / PDF 232); Gregory "had no machinery for centralizing their powers in his own hands… The threat to the traditional position of the bishops did not arise from a conscious project for centralization" (p. 112). Ullmann, named in Chapter 2 §II as "the chapter's scholarly spine," is the reading set aside: "a controversial book, treating the canonists as if they had essentially one opinion, and that wholly in favour of papal absolutism. Its thesis has been widely criticized" (p. 653 / PDF 672). *The book's best answer.* The argument needs jurisdiction over subjects also claimed by territorial powers, not sovereignty — and Morris confirms that at full strength ("two coherent jurisdictions"). Innocent's jurisdiction over sin, exercised *casualiter*, fits the platforms better than hierocracy would: they claim conduct, not territory. Good answer, on condition that Chapter 2 drops Ullmann as spine and rewrites per §3.2 and §3.6. Diagnostic claim 1 survives; only its medieval warrant is restated.

**(ii) The universalism's power was pulled by demand, not pushed by design.** "The apparatus of government in both church and state essentially functioned by response to appeals or complaints from subjects, and was not adapted to the formulation of high policy" (p. 180 / PDF 199). Chapter 2 §I's "a universal authority — self-constituted… claiming rightful power" reads the platforms as pushing. *The book's best answer.* Demand-driven jurisdiction is how a universalism grows; the Oversight Board is a rescript tribunal. This strengthens the structural claim and predicts the failure mode: an institution that cannot form policy cannot resist a state that can. Good.

**(iii) The Church could not pay for force; its state was a by-product of imperial weakness (p. 422).** Not a contradiction of the mechanism, but of any reading in which the hyperscaler church is the medieval Church's heir *in capability*. The medieval heir is the European church; the American one holds components of the decisive stack, which the twelfth century never had. State the disanalogy in Chapter 2 §III per CLAUDE.md §6.

**(iv) On the Strayer/Berman "first modern state" thesis.** Morris never uses the phrase. On Strayer's "the Gregorian concept of the church almost demanded the invention of the concept of the state": "This is at best a half-truth. The development of the state was primarily a response to changing social, economic, and educational conditions" (p. 553 / PDF 572). On Berman: "combines old-fashioned history with interesting ideas" (p. 600 / PDF 619). His direction of copying is the reverse of Berman's: the papal household was "reorganized into a pattern much closer to that of the courts of northern Europe" (p. 168 / PDF 187), and rescript government "can be traced in the rise of common law in England" (p. 212). *The book's best answer.* None needed: the manuscript does not argue that the Church made the state, and Morris removes a rival mechanism. File in ch02/critiques.md as contested interpretation.

No contradiction reaches a spine ruling (§8(a)–(f)) or the DEFEND tier (§6).

## 7. Verdict

Morris changes Chapter 2's register, not the book's direction: the medieval universalism it uses as template was a jurisdiction, not a sovereignty, and the Ullmann reading the chapter names as its spine is the one the field has set aside, so the Dictatus, Canossa and Innocent sentences must be restated in Morris's terms — emergency powers, *casualiter*, jurisdiction over sin — which fit the platforms better than hierocracy did. Its weight is large in one place: the papal fisc (1,214 gold ounces against Richard I's England; a Papal State "created on the battlefields of Sicily and Germany") is the T2 statement of the disanalogy Chapter 2 owes — a universalism that could not pay for force — and it sorts the three modern churches, placing the European one where the medieval Church stood and the American one somewhere the twelfth century never was. The single thing the author should know: Morris's "it was easier for the papacy to assume authority than to restore it once regional powers had lost the directing initiative" (p. 113) is the ratchet stated for the other universalism, and it cuts both ways — the platforms' jurisdiction, once assumed by the state, will not be restored to them either.
