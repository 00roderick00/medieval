# Assessment — J. R. Lander, *Crown and Nobility 1450–1509* (Edward Arnold / McGill–Queen's, 1976)

Read in full (pp. 1–305: introduction, eleven essays, Appendices A–C); Appendices D–E (councillor lists, 1475 combatant tables) skimmed for figures only; index not read. Pins give printed page first; PDF page = printed + 11 throughout. OCR: prose is clean but numerals are unreliable ("145o", "g" for "9", "1498" for 1466, "1458" where the chapter-5 heading reads 1453); the Appendix C tables are garbled and must be checked against the page image before use.

## 1. The book in two paragraphs

A reprint of essays of 1956–71 with a 56-page introduction revising them. The thread is McFarlane's: the English fifteenth century was not an age of over-mighty subjects destroying under-mighty kings, but of a crown that could not govern without its nobility and never tried to. "Edward IV and Henry VII would have regarded as little more than simple-minded the interpretation of history which saw them putting down the nobility and basing their rule on the middle classes" (p. 67 / PDF 78). The Wars of the Roses saw campaigning of "little more than 12 or 13 weeks in 32 years" (p. 62 / PDF 73); the peerage was "remarkably loyal" to Henry VI and afterwards showed "a caution so striking as to amount almost to indifference" (pp. 27, 55 / PDF 38, 66). And the "New Monarchy" was not a strong state: "To call such a monarchy strong is to mistake shadow for substance. The English monarchy emerged from the Wars of the Roses under firm, strong guidance, but inherently it was a ramshackle structure … By continental standards it was one of the shallow little backwaters of monarchy" (pp. 72–3 / PDF 83–4).

The two essays the manuscript cites are ch. 5 (attainder, 1961) and ch. 11 (bonds, 1971). Their joint finding: attainder "came to work at least in part as a kind of probation system … a method of political control, the extreme form of the current system of recognizances for good behaviour … and of the heavy suspended fines imposed on prominent men and others by Henry VII" (p. 156 / PDF 167), which under Henry VII grew into "an immensely tangled, complicated series of relationships in which a majority of the peerage were legally and financially in the king's power and at his mercy" (p. 293 / PDF 304). Lander's verdict is not the manuscript's: "This psychological brutality hardly succeeded. Most probably it transformed an earlier indifference into a dangerous backlash of resentment" (p. 26 / PDF 37).

## 2. What it CONFIRMS

**The tally (ch11 §II).** Manuscript: "of sixty-two peerage families, thirty-six gave bonds or recognizances … some three-quarters lay under one coercive instrument or another." Lander p. 292 / PDF 303: "out of 62 peerage families in existence between 1485 and 1509, a total of 46 or 47 were for some part of Henry's reign at the king's mercy. Seven were under attainder, 36 gave bonds and recognizances, of whom 5 were also heavily fined, another was probably also fined, and 3 more were at some time under subpoenas which carried financial penalties. Only 16 (possibly 15) remained free." "At least three quarters" recurs at pp. 34 and 297. Caution for the pin: p. 281 / PDF 292 says "During the Yorkist period about two thirds of the titled families … Between 1485 and 1509 the proportion rose to slightly more than four fifths" — the "two-thirds" the note corrects against is Lander's *Yorkist* figure, and "four fifths" is his own unreconciled variant. Cite p. 292.

**Bergavenny (ch11 §I).** p. 289 / PDF 300: "In the Michaelmas term 1507 the court of king's bench fined Burgavenny the grand total of £70,650 for unlawfully retaining 471 men below the rank of knight or squire. This was a fine which no one at the time could possibly have paid, so now bargaining began." Recognizance of 5,000 marks (24 December) "not to enter the shires of Kent, Surrey, Sussex, and Hampshire at any time during his life without the king's licence"; indenture accepting "£5,000 payable in instalments over ten years at Candlemas"; all "cancelled during the first year of Henry VIII's reign." "Never meant to be collected" is Lander's reading of the Dorset indenture — "a disposition in terrorem, probably only partially carried through" (p. 287 / PDF 298) — and his gloss on King John's "enormous fines imposed (but generally uncollected)" (p. 156 n. 106).

**The protectorate formula (ch09 §II).** Rot. Parl. v 242 is printed in full at p. 76 / PDF 87; the 1455–56 terms ("dismissed only by the king in parliament … Once again he did not gain the powers of a regent", p. 84 / PDF 95) match ch09. [^9-4] can read "Lander 1976: 76".

**Chamber inherited, not invented (ch11 §II).** p. 41 / PDF 52: Henry VII "for the first two years of his reign let the system lapse. Then, under the influence of ex-Yorkist administrators, he gradually revived it … until by the middle of the 1490s Yorkist methods were more or less in full operation once more"; land revenue fell "from £22–25,000 p.a. under Richard III to about £11,700 in Henry's first year" (n. 222). Half the [GAP: Chrimes] closes here.

**England as monopoly without fisc (ch10 §VI).** "English kings of the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries had no standing army for they simply could not afford one. Neither could they afford a police force, nor anything but the most exiguous bureaucracy" (p. 13 / PDF 24); "Apart from the garrison of Calais … the only standing force available was the king's bodyguard of two hundred archers formed in 1467" (p. 240 / PDF 251); fortification "almost unknown in England until Henry VIII, between 1538 and 1540, built a chain of coastal artillery forts in a revolutionary foreign style" (p. 63 / PDF 74).

**The 1504 licence (ch03 §II).** The act "allowed the king to issue licences or placards under his sign manual, signet or privy seal, for the keeping of retainers" (p. 34 / PDF 45).

## 3. What it CORRECTS

1. Manuscript (ch11 §I): Bergavenny "paid two instalments." Lander p. 289: "So Burgavenny probably paid no more than £1,000"; only the Candlemas 1508 payment is traced (n. 123).
2. Manuscript (ch11 §I): the fine was "comparable to a year of the crown's whole ordinary revenue." Lander p. 42 / PDF 53: Henry's revenue "between £104,000 and £113,000 towards the end of his reign." The fine was about two-thirds of a year; the figure that approaches a year is the indenture's admitted debt "in £100,000 'or thereabouts' for unlawful retainers" (p. 289).
3. Manuscript (ch11 §I): retaining "contrary to the statute of 1504." Lander reads the offence against the rank rule of 1390: the 471 "were described as gentlemen, clerks, yeomen and a cobbler and a tinker. As they were not knights and esquires Burgavenny had thus violated the act of 1390 which was still on the statute book" (pp. 33–4 / PDF 44–5). Lander dates the case 1506 at p. 33 and Michaelmas 1507 at p. 289; the latter is the King's Bench record.
4. Manuscript (ch03 §II): "livery restricted to household men in 1390." Lander p. 33: "only peers might give liveries, except to household servants, and even then only to knights and esquires who had to be retained by indenture for life."
5. Manuscript (ch11 §II): Henry VIII retained "to the letter, his father's machine." Lander pp. 298–9 / PDF 309–10: "Henry VIII cancelled at least 45 recognizances during the first year of his reign and 130 more over the next five years. In 51 cases the recognizances were stated to have been unjustly extorted … Henry reduced the use of the system in terrorem over peers to minute proportions"; by mid-1510 "possibly nine peers at the most remained bound." The fisc relaxed too: "By 1515, his landed income had fallen from £40,000 to £25,000 a year" (p. 48 / PDF 59).
6. Manuscript (ch05 §VI): "private armies." Lander p. 31 / PDF 42: "The term 'private army' has been very loosely handled … completely misleading … Magnates could of course raise considerable numbers of men in an emergency, but they could never keep them in arms for long." Buckingham's retinue, the largest, "cost him just over £900"; Hastings's retainers served in "mainly civilian" capacities (pp. 31–3).

Ch05 §I's dates (March 1454; recovery by end of December; Somerset released 7 February 1455) match pp. 75–6.

## 4. What it ADDS

**1475 as the anti-France (ch10 §VI).** Chapter 9 documents the absent settlement. The 1472 grants were made "with the grudging stipulation that the money was to be returned to the taxpayers if the army were not mustered … They refused to trust the king with the money. It was not to be paid into the royal exchequer" but lodged with St Paul's and in "provincial repositories, local castles, towns, houses of religion" (p. 231 / PDF 242); "Twenty-six months after the first war grant had been made, the king still had no money in his hands except the meagre proceeds of the lords' tenth" (p. 233 / PDF 244); archers to men-at-arms ran "over seven to one", showing "a drying up of the supply of men-at-arms" (pp. 239, 241). Same decade, same enemy, opposite fisc — the paired case ch10 lacks.

**Calais as contractor state (ch03/ch04).** The act of retainer (1466, refounded 1473): "the Staple was to take over financial responsibility for Calais" against a debt of £32,861 (p. 166 / PDF 177; pp. 193–7); the 1464 commission urged "an increase in their artillery and war stores" (p. 195). A fiscal-military function delegated to a private corporation — "operative but never invested" in period form.

**Harrington's binary (ch11 §VII / ch12).** "monarchy could be of two kinds, 'the one by arms, the other by a nobility', and he added, 'a monarchy, divested of its nobility, has no refuge under heaven but an army.' Henry had no army. As his taxation policy … was principally aimed at avoiding discontent … he could not afford to maintain one" (p. 296 / PDF 307). The mechanism's other branch, at source.

**Fortescue pins (ch05 §I).** "there was no subject 'equipollent' to the king himself, to use Fortescue's own misleading phrase" (p. 16 / PDF 27; Plummer ed. 130); "the might of the land after the might of the great lords thereof standeth most in the king's officers" (p. 269 n. 10; Plummer 150–1). The McFarlane [GAP] stays open — Lander paraphrases (pp. 21, 273).

**The licence both ways (ch03 §II).** "In 1512, in preparation for the king's invasion of France, Henry VIII issued Burgavenny a licence 'to retain as many men as he can get in Kent, Sussex and Surrey and elsewhere'" (p. 290 / PDF 301). Fined for 471 in 1507, licensed without limit in 1512: private force as a switchable state resource.

**Attainder figures (ch11 §II).** 397 attainted 1453–1504, "about 64 per cent ultimately had their attainders reversed"; among the peerage "just over 84 per cent" (pp. 129–30 / PDF 140–1); 34 peers attainted, 9 executed (p. 273 n. 32); Zouche and Surrey as the worked cases of restoration "by stages" (pp. 144–7).

**Devon 1455 and the second protectorate (ch08 §IV; ch05/ch09).** Powderham reconstructed from KB 9/16: the assault "from eight in the morning until four in the afternoon", and "In the end they received a royal pardon for every offence … even for the murder of Nicholas Radford" (pp. 86–9 / PDF 97–100). And Lander's case that Henry VI suffered "no second breakdown" in 1455 (p. 90 / PDF 101): the second protectorate was "an attempted palace revolution which failed miserably for lack of support" (p. 99 / PDF 110), driven through by York's feoffee Burley. Ch05/ch09 treat both protectorates as responses to incapacity; the second was a magnate working the device.

## 5. What it CONTRADICTS

**(a) There was no English fisc; the New Monarchy was consolidated by peace, not by paying for force.** "Both Edward IV and Henry VII … were exceedingly circumspect in their demand for direct taxation and raised in fact very little from it, Edward IV no more than an average of £10,700 a year, Henry VII doing rather better with £12–13,000 … their experiments turned out to be humiliatingly unsuccessful. Political exigencies therefore forced both kings back on … the customs system, the royal estates and fiscal feudalism" (pp. 39–40 / PDF 50–1). "The so-called 'New Monarchy' coincided with one of the longest periods in English history free of prolonged and expensive foreign warfare. Between 1453 and 1544 campaigns abroad were short and took place at long intervals. The end of the Hundred Years' War of itself was enough to end the principal drain on the royal coffers" (p. 71 / PDF 82). "The tax-paying classes had tied the king's hands behind his back" (p. 54). "Such restriction left the English monarchy in a financial straitjacket for almost two centuries" (p. 55 / PDF 66). On the spine's end-point: "A generation later even the reforming schemes of Henry VIII's minister, Thomas Cromwell, failed to obliterate its inherited weaknesses" (pp. 72–3); Henry VIII "could defy a distant spiritual power, but he could not exact his admitted traditional feudal rights from his own subjects" (p. 47 / PDF 58).

This reaches spine §3 ("standing taxation established") and ch11's restated schema ("fiscal reform — permanent central taxation" executed by England 1470–1530). On Lander's evidence the endowed monarchy was a device for *not* taxing, adopted because the commons had forced "live of his own" on Henry VI and the Yorkists "adopted as their official policy the clamours of the disgruntled house of commons" (p. 40). Henry VII consolidated without artillery, army or permanent taxation — without the mechanism — and the result produced backlash and was unwound in 1509.

*The book's best answer.* Ch10 already casts England as monopoly-without-fisc and spine §7 already makes England the latecomer. Lander sharpens that: Henry VII's recognizance state is what consolidation looks like *before* the fisc — legal terror substituting for capability, hence "chronic watchfulness, suspicion, and fear" (p. 293) and non-survival of its author. The English settlement proper becomes Henry VIII's: the dissolution as endowment-in-lieu-of-taxation, the 1538–40 forts as the guns, the 1529–47 creations (p. 273 n. 32) as the nobility of service. Ch11 §II must be rewritten on those lines and "fiscal reform" qualified for England. The answer is good on sequence and fits the ratchet-and-lag logic, but Lander's "two centuries" and his Cromwell sentence mean the book cannot claim England *established* standing taxation inside its window, and must say so: England is the mechanism's slowest and least complete case, funded by a confiscated endowment rather than an Estates' grant.

**(b) The over-mighty subject is a myth; the crisis was one of arbitration, and the cure was a strong king.** "The popularity of Sir John Fortescue's works has created something of a myth of the overmighty subject … not even the greatest subject possessed an income much more than one twelfth of that of the king … the English nobility possessed no fortresses and only very limited judicial powers" (p. 16). "It was certainly no warlike, factious spirit in a turbulent nobility which produced the Wars of the Roses" (p. 55); Henry VI "had failed to arbitrate reasonably, fairly, impartially in the disputes of the nobility" (p. 20 / PDF 31); the system "demanded a king of strong character for its successful control" (p. 33), which Edward IV supplied "using the lines of command of bastard feudalism" (p. 38 / PDF 49). This cuts against ch05 §VI ("Over-mighty subjects, encountering an undermighty king, produce the crisis … the structural problem outlasted him, because it was structural").

*The book's best answer.* The claim is relational — capability concentrated relative to the crown's executive capacity — and Lander concedes the relation: royal courts "had far too little coercive power at their command. The authority necessary to ensure social peace lay largely in private hands" (p. 30 / PDF 41); "The margin between order and disorder was still small … and it depended on the action of a strong king" (p. 170 / PDF 181). The answer holds if ch05 drops "private armies", states Lander's disanalogy (scattered estates, no fortresses, "merely rich gentlemen", p. 68), and recasts "structural" as the crown's deficit rather than the magnates' surplus. It does not hold for "outlasted him": Lander's Edward IV managed the problem, and the docility of 1485–1540 is attributed to noble caution and to the Tudors giving "no great cause for political and social offence" (p. 297), not to instruments. Retreat to: Edward's management was personal and non-transferable — Lander's own formulation.

**(c) Discipline touched conduct, not power.** Henry VII's measures "affected the nobles' conduct rather than their powers" (p. 297); he "made no frontal attack upon their local powers except in two or three cases" (p. 34). This is close to the book's own leashed-not-destroyed thesis and can be quoted for it — provided ch11 stops calling the bonds a machine the successor kept.

No DEFEND-tier claim is touched: Lander has nothing on artillery or France. The damage is to the English limb of the predictive analogy and to spine §3's wording.

## 7. Verdict

Lander changes the register of the English limb, not the book's direction: it supplies the tallies ch11 was waiting for, but it also says, in the standard authority's words, that England 1461–1509 consolidated by avoiding war and taxation, kept no army because it could not pay for one, and disciplined its nobles by a legal terror its author's son dismantled within a year. The one thing the author must take from it is that Henry VII cannot be the book's English consolidator — he is the pre-settlement case, monopoly-without-fisc governing by recognizance — so the English Tudor turn must be dated to the 1530s with the dissolution named as its fisc, or spine §3's "standing taxation established" rewritten for England. Done that way, Lander becomes the book's best-documented picture of a polity in which the mechanism has not yet run.
