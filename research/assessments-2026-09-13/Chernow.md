# Assessment — Ron Chernow, *Titan: The Life of John D. Rockefeller, Sr.* (1998; Vintage 2nd edn 2004)

Text: /home/claude/books/Titan.txt (~335k words, 920 PDF pages, ebook conversion). Pins are PDF page indexes (1 + form-feeds); the conversion carries **no printed page numbers, no index, and the endnotes are unnumbered** (note markers survive only sporadically in the text; I matched notes by counting from each chapter's last note, and say so where it matters). Text itself is clean.

Read: Foreword and epigraphs (pp. 7, 14–17); ch. 12 (pp. 238–51, pipelines, Tidewater, the 1879 Hepburn Committee); ch. 14 (pp. 260–64, the 1879 trustee device and 1882 trust); ch. 18 (pp. 376–78, Ohio 1892); ch. 21 (pp. 484–86, the 1899 holding company); ch. 26 (pp. 581–83, Bureau of Corporations, Hepburn Act); ch. 27 "Judgment Day" in full (pp. 600–24: the 1906–11 prosecutions, decree, successor companies, fortune); ch. 28 (pp. 629–30, the Foundation charter); the later-fortune passages (pp. 690–93, 737–39). ## 1. The book in two paragraphs

Chernow's book is a full-scale life, not a business history, and its thesis is about the man's coherence: "the pious, Bible-thumping Rockefeller wasn't simply a cunning façade for the corporate pirate. The religious and acquisitive sides of his nature were intimately related" (p. 17). It rests on the Rockefeller Archive Center papers, above all the 1,700-page Inglis interview transcript of 1917–20 and "the twenty thousand pages of letters that Rockefeller received from his more outspoken business associates" (p. 16) — which is why it can document what Nevins could not: pipeline coercion, legislative bribery, the trust's internal mechanics. Its epigraph is Russell's: "Two men have been supreme in creating the modern world: Rockefeller and Bismarck" (p. 7).

On what concerns this project, Chernow's judgement is flat and in his own voice: the 1911 dissolution "proved to be the luckiest stroke of his career. Precisely because he lost the antitrust suit, Rockefeller was converted from a mere millionaire… into something just short of history's first billionaire" (p. 621); and "It was certainly not their intention, but the trustbusters helped to preserve Rockefeller's legacy for posterity and unquestionably made him the world's richest man" (p. 624). He is a T3 carrier of the highest grade — archival, with some 1,900 notes — but, as §3 shows, the two figures this project most needs are among the sentences he does not footnote.

## 2. What it CONFIRMS

**"Rockefeller kept every share and grew richer" (Chapter 12, l. 6629).** Chernow p. 621: "In December 1911, he was finally able to jettison the presidency of Standard Oil, but he continued to hold on to his immense shareholdings. As the owner of about one quarter of the shares of the old trust, Rockefeller now got a one-quarter share of the new Standard Oil of New Jersey, plus one quarter of the thirty-three independent subsidiary companies created by the decision." And p. 622: "Thanks to this staggering appreciation, Rockefeller's net worth reached a lifetime peak of $900 million in 1913—more than $13 billion in 1996 dollars… making more money in retirement than on the job." The pro-rata mechanism the probe could only infer from 221 U.S. 10 is stated as fact here and independently by Yergin (*The Prize*, l. 5454–55).

**The decree's two operative terms (Chapter 12, ll. 6629–32; probe §1).** p. 619: White "upheld the decision to dismantle Standard Oil, which was given six months to spin off its subsidiaries, with its officers forbidden from reestablishing the monopoly." The six months and the no-recombination bar match §§ 5–6 as the probe reads them.

**"pipelines made common carriers from 1906, five years before the breakup" (Chapter 12, ll. 6635–36).** p. 582: Roosevelt "supported the Hepburn bill, which granted broader power to the Interstate Commerce Commission to set railroad rates and placed interstate pipelines under its domain. By bringing Standard Oil to heel, Roosevelt hoped to check two abuses at once: railroad collusion and industrial monopoly." Chernow adds that the Bureau of Corporations' 500-page report was made public on 2 May 1906 precisely "as a potent tool to push through the Hepburn bill" (p. 583) — the carriage instrument was aimed at this trust, not at railroads in general.

**The pipeline as the private off-switch (spine §8(c): "the vertical integration that made oil sovereign").** p. 238, quoting Tarbell: "Practically not a barrel of oil could get to a railroad without [Rockefeller's] consent." p. 240: "By 1879, the combine controlled almost the entire pipeline system"; p. 251, after Tidewater's absorption (88.5 : 11.5 division of the Pennsylvania pipeline trade), producers "could no longer ship oil from Pennsylvania without paying tribute." The switch was literal: O'Day "might hint that the producer's backcountry well was too inaccessible for Standard to run a line" (p. 240); Rockefeller to O'Day: "Don't let them get a pipe to Buffalo" (p. 241).

**The republic "confronted an over-mighty citizen… and in no verified case did it confiscate" (Chapter 12, ll. 6616–20).** p. 620: "The antitrust suit against Standard tested whether the American legal system could cope with the new agglomerations of wealth and curb their excesses."

**The scale of the fortune against the fisc (Chapter 5's undermighty king, applied).** p. 622: "the total accumulated national debt of the United States stood at $1.2 billion that year [1913], equivalent to 3 percent of the gross national product; federal spending was a mere $715 million." A private fortune exceeding the federal budget is the American 1450 in one sentence.

## 3. What it CORRECTS

1. **The fortune figures are not footnoted in Chernow either.** The sentences carrying "$300 million in 1911" (p. 621) and "$900 million in 1913" (p. 622) sit between n. 74 (*Bradstreet's*, 2 Sept 1911) and n. 75 (Kolko, *Triumph of Conservatism*, p. 194); neither figure has a note. Roosevelt's "give us another dissolution" is n. 76 = Yergin, *The Prize*, p. 113 — and Yergin (l. 5461–62) gives "$900 million" with no base figure and no source. The review pack's hope (l. 115) that Chernow or Yergin would supply "a T2 carrier with apparatus" is not met: the figures move from an unfootnoted article to an unfootnoted, archivally serious biography. Carry them at T3 as "Chernow's estimate," not as verified.

2. **Chernow is internally inconsistent on the base figure.** p. 390: "When he stepped down from Standard Oil, he was probably worth about $200 million… his fortune soared to $1 billion by 1913." pp. 621–22: $300M in 1911, "a lifetime peak of $900 million in 1913," and "his father never had a billion dollars at any one moment." Cite pp. 621–22, not p. 390. Rockefeller's own 1908 denial to Hoster — not a billion, "not by one-third of that amount" (p. 591), a self-estimate near $650M — is left unreconciled with the $300M of 1911. The 1907 shareholding was "27.4 percent" (p. 601), not a round quarter.

3. **34 v. 37 — resolved.** The circuit court in November 1909 "ruled unanimously that Standard Oil of New Jersey and thirty-seven affiliates had violated the Sherman Antitrust Act" (p. 618); that is the decree's § 4 schedule, which the probe correctly counts at ~37. But the dissolution produced "the thirty-three independent subsidiary companies created by the decision" plus the shorn parent (p. 621) — "the thirty-four new companies, with their seventy thousand employees" (p. 623; again "one of the thirty-four companies," p. 623). The review pack's sentence "every share of the thirty-seven successor companies" (l. 115) conflates the named affiliates with the successors; it should read "thirty-four successor companies (thirty-three spun off, plus the parent)" or "the thirty-seven affiliates named in the decree." (Do not confuse either number with "the thirty-seven investors of Standard of Ohio" under the 1879 trustee device, p. 260 — a coincidence.)

4. **Sequence.** Suit filed 18 November 1906 against "Standard Oil of New Jersey, sixty-five companies under its control" and seven chieftains (p. 600); circuit court November 1909, "thirty days to divest" (p. 618); Supreme Court 15 May 1911 (p. 619); successor shares traded from 1 December 1911 (p. 622). The review pack's "when the Supreme Court broke it up in 1911" (l. 113) should be "affirmed the 1909 breakup."

5. **Hepburn 1906 did not bite at once.** After Elkins 1903 and Hepburn 1906 "the public naively assumed [rebates] had ended. Then the Interstate Commerce Commission reported in January 1907 that Standard Oil was still secretly accepting rebates" (p. 601). The manuscript's "from 1906" should not imply enforcement from 1906 — which strengthens the lag reading in §8(c)(i).

## 4. What it ADDS

**(a) The answer to the spine's question — who controlled flow after 1911 — is not "no one" but "the same men, informally, for a decade." (Chapter 12 §IV; probe §3.)** p. 623: Rockefeller "quietly worked to undermine the dissolution, suggesting that officials of the Standard Oil companies meet at 26 Broadway at ten-thirty each morning to maintain amicable relations and swap information. (For legal reasons, everyone was cautioned not to exchange thoughts on paper.)" "For the next decade, the divestiture often seemed a sham. The Standard companies continued to divide the country into eleven marketing territories, selling the same brand names and not competing on prices." The technical lever: Standard of Indiana, holding the 1913 Burton cracking patent, "required its cousin companies to restrict sales of 'cracked' gasoline to their pre-1911 marketing territories, helping to extend the trust structure for another decade" (p. 624). Morgan's "How the hell is any court going to compel a man to compete with himself?" (p. 623, n. 78 = Winkler p. 157) and Roosevelt's "All the companies are still under the same control" (p. 623, n. 79 = Nevins II p. 383) are the colour. This closes the [RESEARCH] flag in spine §8(c) at the level the probe could not reach: private command was severed *de jure* in 1911 and dissolved *de facto* over 1911–21, before the public switch (proration, 1930) existed.

**(b) The contemporaries' three alternatives (probe §5 mirror table).** pp. 623–24: critics thought the government "should have done one of three things: keep the trust intact and regulate it; force shareholders to take stock in only one of the thirty-four companies; or create fully integrated companies." The first is the road the republic later walked; the second is the wealth-severing remedy it refused — "title left, discretion severed," stated by the losing side in 1911.

**(c) The syndicate offering to take a licence, and the Crown refusing (spine §8(c), the H200 paragraph).** September 1907: Standard "would open its books and abide by any recommendations to guarantee compliance with the antitrust laws if the government withdrew its suit"; Roosevelt: "If we have a criminal case against these men… I should be very reluctant to surrender it" (p. 608). The contrast: the Morgan interests were treated more leniently "because they had submitted to guidance from the Bureau of Corporations and worked out informal arrangements to correct violations" (pp. 600–01).

**(d) Rockefeller asked for the licence himself.** Before the Industrial Commission in 1899 he "endorsed a federal incorporation law, even if a measure of government regulation came with it" (p. 485). The over-mighty citizen petitioning for a national charter; the state declined it, and in 1910–13 declined even a charter for his foundation.

**(e) The Foundation charter fight (ch. 28).** Wickersham to Taft, February 1911, asked whether "at the moment when the United States through its courts is seeking in a measure to destroy the great combination of wealth… the Congress of the United States should assist in the enactment of a law to create and perpetuate in his name an institution to hold and administer a large portion of this vast wealth" (p. 630). The state severed command of the oil and refused to lend its name to the fortune's institutional form — the two halves of the decoupling in one attorney general.

**(f) The state's non-antitrust levers in the same years (probe §3 table).** 1909: "Congress largely repealed the duty that had protected the trust from foreign competition; the secretary of war halted purchase of petroleum products from it; and the president set aside petroleum-rich territory for conservation purposes" (p. 619). Tariff, procurement, public land — the licence-and-block repertoire before 1911, from primaries the probe did not list.

**(g) The 1907 Panic (ch. 5; the Money Trust case).** Of Rockefeller's AP statement: "A single citizen had promised to bail out Wall Street" (p. 607) — the crown borrowing the magnate's treasury four years before the decree.

**(h) The protean-form genealogy, for the tempo problem (Chapter 12, ll. 6650–58).** 1879 trustees (p. 260) → 1882 trust ("the 1882 trust agreement executed by Standard Oil led straight to the Sherman Antitrust Act eight years later," p. 263) → 1892 Ohio dissolution as "mostly shadow play, a charade to appease the courts" (p. 378) → 1899 New Jersey holding company (p. 485): "the combine had reconstituted itself many times, like some mythical, protean creature" (p. 377). The Microsoft court's "six years" warning has a thirty-year prehistory.

**(i) The earlier Hepburn.** The 1879 New York Assembly committee under A. B. Hepburn found railroad bias to Standard "the most shameless perversion of the duties of a common carrier to private ends" (p. 250) — by which time, Chernow notes, the trust had moved to pipelines and "the railroads no longer mattered" (p. 250). Two Hepburns, twenty-seven years apart, each regulating the carrier the trust had just outgrown; do not conflate them.

## 5. What it CONTRADICTS

**First, at full strength: on Chernow's account Rockefeller had no power to lose in 1911, so "richer and less powerful" is not what happened to him.** From 1899 the presidency was "entirely nominal… Rockefeller never attended a meeting or drew a salary, and Archbold, the new vice president, ran the organization" (p. 485). In 1907 "As nominal president of Standard Oil, he was in a bind, responsible for actions he had not approved" (p. 601). Chernow's Rockefeller decoupled wealth from power himself, by retiring in 1897–99; what the decree severed was Archbold's command — and even that, per §4(a), survived informally to 1921. Spine §8(c)'s sentence — "Rockefeller got RICHER when Standard Oil was broken up, and less POWERFUL — wealth and power decoupled by legal instrument" — is, as a statement about the man, not Chernow's account.

**Second: the dissolution rode a market that was already dissolving the monopoly.** "By the time of the Supreme Court's 1911 decision, evolutionary changes in the marketplace had already eroded the trust's dominance" (p. 620): crude share 32% (1899) → 14% (1911); refining 86% → 70% in the five years before the breakup (p. 621); Royal Dutch Shell 1907, Texas after the 1900 expulsion and Spindletop (pp. 486, 621), gasoline overtaking kerosene in 1910. Chernow attributes the fortune's tripling to the litigation discount lifting, "hidden assets" from conservative capitalisation, and "the explosion of the automobile industry" (pp. 621–22) — none of them state action. The consolidator pushed on an opening door.

**Third: the instrument was read at the time as capture, not consolidation.** La Follette: "I fear that the court has done what the trusts wanted it to do" (p. 620); the rule of reason "opened a loophole large enough to tolerate many trusts" (p. 620); Wall Street was "laughing in its sleeve" (p. 622). The manuscript's "the republic decouples in one act" is Chernow's "at best, a partial victory" (p. 620).

**The book's best answer.** On the first point: the spine's claim concerns the *office*, not the man — the holding company's unified discretion (§ 5) and the right to rebuild it (§ 6) — and Chernow's own evidence that successor coordination had to be oral, unenforceable and patent-dependent (pp. 623–24) is proof the instrument bit: a command that must meet at 10.30 and may not write anything down is not a command. Rewrite the personal sentence into the structural one already at ll. 6629–32 and drop "less powerful" as a claim about the retired man. That answer is good. On the second: the ratchet reading (§8(a)) survives — market made the settlement irreversible, law made it lawful — but the chapter must concede that in the oil case the market did the dissolving and the state supplied the form; the disanalogy with a stack that is concentrating, not dispersing, is already in the probe's mirror table and should enter the text. That answer is honest but reduces "proof case" to "worked example," where §8(c)'s ruling of 2 September already put it. On the third: concede it; the reformers' complaint is evidence that the republic's method leaves title intact. None of this reaches §8(a), (b) or the DEFEND-tier mechanism; it reaches the wording of §8(c) and the sentence at l. 6629.

## 7. Verdict

Chernow changes neither the book's direction nor its register; he changes the weight and the wording of one proof case, and he supplies the answer to the spine's open question — after 1911 the switch stayed, informally and eroding, with the same men until about 1921, before the state's own switch existed. The fortune figures ($300M → $900M) are now carried by a serious archival biography but remain unfootnoted in it and in Yergin, so they can be stated as Chernow's estimate at T3 and not as fact, and the count is thirty-four successor companies against thirty-seven named affiliates. The single most important thing: on Chernow's evidence Rockefeller had already retired from power in 1899 and the decree severed Archbold's command, not his — so the sentence "Rockefeller got richer and less powerful" must become a sentence about the office, which the manuscript at l. 6629 already half-writes and should finish.
