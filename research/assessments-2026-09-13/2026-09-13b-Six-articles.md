# Assessment — six short works (Ágoston 2014; İnalcık 1960; Payling 1998; Grummitt 1999; Elliott 1992; Baker 1979)

*All six read in full. Pins give printed page / PDF page. Ágoston: printed = PDF + 83; İnalcık: + 406; Payling: printed = PDF; Grummitt: + 228; Elliott: + 46; Baker: + 292. OCR: Payling's footnotes are badly garbled (body text clean); İnalcık and Elliott have JSTOR run-together words and stray digits ("1192" for 119); Baker's footnote numerals are displaced but the body is legible. Nothing load-bearing is lost.*

## 1. Gábor Ágoston, "Firearms and Military Adaptation: The Ottomans and the European Military Revolution, 1450–1800", *Journal of World History* 25:1 (2014), 85–124 — T2

**The article in a paragraph.** A demolition of the "Ottoman lag" and a redating of Ottoman army growth. The Ottomans adopted guns in the 1380s, paid gunners by timar from the 1390s and by salary a generation later, and "From the 1390s on, preceding their rivals by centuries, the Ottomans established a corps of permanent salaried troops who specialized in the manufacturing and handling of firearms" (p. 93 / PDF 10). Mehmed's Tophane was "one of the first arsenals in late medieval Europe to be built, operated, and financed by a central government, in a time when European monarchs obtained their cannons from artisanal workshops" (p. 101 / PDF 18). Firearms were "but one element" at Constantinople (p. 109 / PDF 26); "Firepower and military superiority were achieved through the sheer numbers of weapons and troops, and not so much by their quality" (p. 108 / PDF 25), and that quantity "reflected the strength of the Ottoman administrative-fiscal and logistical systems" (p. 111 / PDF 28). The thesis sentence for this book: "whereas in Western Europe the Military Revolution supposedly led to enhanced state centralization and resulted in the rise of military-fiscal states, the Ottoman experience was military devolution" (p. 123 / PDF 40).

**Confirms.** Ch. 10's caveat that "the Ottoman janissaries predate the ordonnance companies by two generations": p. 93 dates the salaried firearms corps to the 1390s and the janissaries to Orhan or Murad I. Ch. 11 §IV's "the register-based counts at the lower end": Table 7 gives 7,841 janissaries on the 1484 pay sheet (p. 113 / PDF 30). Ch. 6 §VI's point that the defeated misread the fiscal apparatus: Ágoston's Mamluks "deployed dozens of field guns and had trained arquebusiers at Marj Dabiq, but could not match Ottoman firepower and numbers of troops" (p. 110 / PDF 27) — the copy-the-gun-and-lose case the chapter already carries from Ibn Iyas. Spine §8(e)2, the *délai de secours*: "Firepower, even in combination with numerical and logistical superiority, was still insufficient in the mid fifteenth century if a relatively strong relief army with superior leadership arrived in time, as Mehmed II's failed siege of Belgrade in 1456 demonstrated" (p. 109 / PDF 26) — the relief clock stated from the Ottoman side. The Sharman §5(v) finding ("technology was not the problem"): Ágoston's whole second section shows self-sufficiency in cannon and powder "well into the eighteenth century" (p. 100 / PDF 17).

**Corrects.** Manuscript (Ch. 11 §IV) says the janissary strength "under Mehmed II [was] roughly five to ten thousand"; İnalcık (below, p. 426) says "increased from four or five thousand to ten or twelve thousand by Mehmed II", and Ágoston's first register is 7,841 in 1484. Write the growth, not the band. Manuscript (Ch. 11 §IV) implies the Ottoman fisc was a cash household; Ágoston p. 88 / PDF 5: even gunners were "remunerated with timars from the 1390s", salaried cannoneers only "a generation later" (p. 93) — the artillery itself began on the in-kind base and was converted to cash in the 1420s. Nothing else in the chapter is wrong by Ágoston's account.

**Adds.** (i) *Ch. 11 §IV and Coda — the fifth failure mode in the authority's own words.* Sharman's "ratchet slipped" (assess/Sharman.md §5(v)) is now dated and mechanised: "civilianization" of the corps began under Süleyman (p. 115 / PDF 32); prebends "were turned into royal estates in order to pay the expanding standing army", the treasury "faced recurring deficits from the early 1590s onward", and governors' private armies rose from 33 to 58 per cent of the timariot muster (p. 121 / PDF 38); "the janissaries themselves seized the moment and used it to enlist their sons and relatives into the corps. Selling janissary certificates … became a lucrative business" (p. 123 / PDF 40). That is absorption undone by the absorbed capability becoming an estate, pinned. (ii) *Ch. 10 §II, the payroll.* The Tophane sentence (p. 101) is the cleanest T2 statement that a central government owned a foundry before any Latin monarchy did; and n. 13 (p. 89 / PDF 6): "the Ottomans registered eight cannons ten days after its conquest" — the gun entered the register before the province did. (iii) *Ch. 5 §IV.* The 1444 crossing: Halil Pasha at the Bosporus with "four hundred arquebusiers, and two hundred artillery gunners", the guns "delivered to him by the Genoese of Pera" (p. 90 / PDF 7 and n. 14) — the peace-party vizier operating the guns in the crisis year. (iv) *Ch. 11 §V.* Ágoston's rebuke to the Long-War thesis — "a reductionist reasoning bordering on technological determinism" (p. 112 / PDF 29) — is a sentence this book should quote against itself to show the discipline it keeps.

**Contradicts, at full strength.** Ágoston denies the gun any special agency in Ottoman success: "were these Ottoman victories due largely to firearms? The reexamination … suggests a more complex picture" (p. 109); at Mohács "the Ottoman cannons played a minor role" (p. 111 / PDF 28); the janissary core was "less than 15 percent of the mobilized Ottoman army" (p. 111). And the polity that had the foundry, the payroll and the register in 1453 did not consolidate on that base; it devolved, with the gun in hand. Combined with Kafadar and İnalcık (assess/Kafadar.md §5; Inalcik.md §5) this reaches the DEFEND-tier mechanism wherever the Ottomans are carried as its limit case. *The book's best answer:* the answer already given for Kafadar and İnalcık, now with Ágoston as its evidence — the Ottoman state is the *requirement* demonstrated, not the causation: a pre-artillery household that could therefore build and lock; and the devolution is what happens when the cash settlement is never made (p. 121's conversion of prebends is the settlement attempted late, in deficit). It is adequate on the condition already stated: ch. 11 §V's "none was executable without it" exempts the Ottomans, and §IV names devolution as the case's end.

**Placement.** Ch. 11 §IV, by name, T2 ally on capacity and rival on causation; Coda §I and Appendix B's Ottoman row (the "conversion failed" cell now pinned to pp. 121–123); Ch. 10 §II footnote for the Tophane and the 1390s corps; Ch. 6 §VI footnote for Belgrade 1456.

## 2. Halil İnalcık, "Mehmed the Conqueror (1432–1481) and His Time", *Speculum* 35 (1960), 408–427 — T2 (a review-article of Babinger, built on Tursun Beg, Kemal Pasha-zade and the registers)

**In a paragraph.** "A determining factor in all the major political developments of Ottoman history between 1444 and 1453 was the struggle for supreme power between Chandarli Khalil Pasha, the all powerful grand vizier since 1436 or 1437, and a group of ambitious military leaders including Shahabeddin Shahin, Zaganos, and Turakhan, who were seeking to seize the control of the government by claiming to be protectors of the young Sultan's rights (he was only twelve in 1444). By repudiating Chandarli's peace policy they became responsible for Mehmed's aggressive expansionist policy … Having failed in 1446, when Chandarli managed to bring back Murad II to the throne, they finally gained the upper hand after Mehmed's restoration in 1451, and caused Chandarli's dismissal and execution immediately after the conquest of Constantinople in 1453" (p. 410 / PDF 4). The rest is chronology corrected from the Ottoman sources, and a closing page on Mehmed's "unusually radical financial measures" (p. 426 / PDF 20).

**Confirms.** Ch. 5 §IV: the 1446 restoration engineered by Çandarlı (p. 410); the twelve-year-old sultan; the war-party reading already adopted from Kafadar. Ch. 11 §IV: Zaganos as Çandarlı's successor — against Babinger's vacancy and Ishak: "before Mahmud's appointment, Zaganos Pasha was grand vizier" (p. 413 / PDF 7), the list at p. 415 / PDF 9. Ch. 11 §IV's confiscation caveat: "over twenty thousand villages or estates came under direct state control, which meant a new heavy taxation" (p. 426) — the revenue reading, from Tursun, "a high official in the finance department".

**Corrects.** Manuscript (Ch. 11 §IV) says "on 1 June 1453, three days after Constantinople fell, Mehmed executed his grand vizier Çandarlı Halil Pasha"; İnalcık p. 412 / PDF 6 says the arrest was on the third day (Babinger), that Babinger's own editions disagree on the execution (10 July German; 10 June French), and that on the Ottoman sources — Uruj's "forty days after the conquest of Enos", the submission of Enos "in the midsummer of 1453" — "the execution of Chandarli Khalil in August or even later in September". His vizier list closes Çandarlı's term at "30 May 1453" (p. 415). The 1 June date is the *amān-nāme* to the Genoese of Pera bearing Zaganos's signature (p. 414 / PDF 8), not the execution. Write: dismissed and arrested within three days; executed at Adrianople in late summer. Manuscript (Ch. 5 §IV) "weary of the throne": unsupported here too; İnalcık's frame is factional.

**Adds.** *Ch. 11 §IV's fiscal move, at last from a T2 authority:* three measures after 1471 — recoinage "forcing people to exchange the old coin at its metal value with the new one at its face value. The difference at the rate of one sixth meant a heavy tax on the possessors of cash in silver. He used this hated device three times after 1471"; the land review; the extension of monopolistic tax-farming (p. 426). "Toward 1481 the state treasury had in its chests about three and a half million ducats worth of ready money" (p. 426, from Topkapı E. 9713). *Ch. 5 §IV / Ch. 9:* "the Janissaries (Yeni-cheri) always disliked Mehmed II … On his death they burst into a fearful revolt and were instrumental in bringing to power a reactionary administration" (p. 426) — and the histories of Bayezid's reign were commissioned as that reaction: "the reaction to Mehmed's policies can be seen in many details, especially in financial matters and in the rehabilitation of the Chandarli family" (p. 410) — the losers writing the record, for ch. 6 §VI's theme. *Ch. 6 §VI:* the two collisions between Çandarlı and the war party during the siege, after the naval failure of 20 April and on 26 May (p. 411 / PDF 5), with Ak-Shemseddin's letter blaming "your misjudgment and lack of authority". *Ch. 11 §III–IV, absorption by annexation:* Kemal Pasha-zade on the *tekvours* — "The goal was to take away from these people all sovereignty" (p. 422 / PDF 16); and Serbia after 1459 "maintained its own legal and financial system to a considerable degree" (p. 421 / PDF 15) — the composite texture inside the limit case.

**Contradicts.** Nothing structural beyond what the 1973 book already carries. Note only that the article is a 1960 review; its dates are authoritative, its framing pre-Kafadar.

**Placement.** Ch. 5 §IV footnote (1444–46); Ch. 11 §IV by name for the execution chronology, the Zaganos succession, and the three fiscal measures; Ch. 6 §VI footnote.

## 3. S. J. Payling, "Murder, Motive and Punishment in Fifteenth-Century England: Two Gentry Case-Studies", *EHR* 113 (1998), 1–17 — T2

**In a paragraph.** Two gentry murders (Basynges, 1446; Chaworth, 1464) against the McFarlane–Carpenter–Powell consensus that gentry violence was "more apparent than real" and punishment inexpedient. Payling: "Inherent in the new interpretation is a confusion between the problem of disorder and the related but distinct problem of crime" (p. 2). The commissions of the peace worked; "the principal problem lay in the ease with which the indictments were subverted by appeal to the King's mercy … anyone of landed rank could rely on a pardon if called to answer for a crime short of high treason" (pp. 15–16). "Thus the chief deterrent to murder among the landed class lay not in fear of the courts but in the threat of private vengeance" (p. 16). "The Crown seems to have viewed the judicial execution of a member of landed society for felony as an unjustifiable intrusion into private matters" (p. 17).

**Confirms.** Ch. 8 §II, "royal justice was not absent from this world — it was *enlisted*": Payling's Alice Makworth got her pardon through Lord Say and Queen Margaret — "Say used his influence with Queen Margaret to obtain a pardon for Alice; in return he demanded or was offered the Basynges lands in Kent" (p. 8), the Queen's letter of 26 March 1448 quoted. Ch. 8 §VII's sequence, the Tudor assertion arriving in the two generations after: "The execution of Charles, Lord Stourton, in 1557 for the murder of his father's steward suggests a far lower tolerance of aristocratic crime by the mid-sixteenth century … the landed felon was never again protected from the punishments of the common law to the same degree as he had been in the later middle ages" (p. 17). Ch. 7: "Such corruption offers one explanation for the dramatic fall of Say and his master, the Duke of Suffolk, in 1450" (p. 9) — the Treasurer beheaded on London Bridge was, two years earlier, trading pardons for Kentish manors.

**Corrects.** Manuscript (Ch. 8 §I) reads Paston's "not abille to sue the commone lawe … for the gret myght and alyaunce" as the courts failing against might; Payling relocates the leak: "these limitations did not primarily lie in the Crown's reliance for indictments on partial or negligent local agencies" (p. 15) but in the prerogative of pardon — "royal justice was evenhanded in its negligence" (p. 10). The private-justice chapter's failure point is the crown's own mercy, not the sheriff's fear. Manuscript (Ch. 8 §II), "the plurality was the practice", leans on the self-regulating-community thesis Payling attacks by name (Maddern, Powell, Carpenter, p. 1 n. 1); the draft should say which side it takes.

**Adds.** The Chaworth petition of 1465 — Parliament asked to vest the appeal in the victim's brother, with summary process and a £500 penalty on a negligent sheriff (p. 13) — is the community asking the state for the deterrent the state would not supply, and the sheriff was pardoned his fines anyway (p. 14). Payling's own open question is the chapter's: "when and why the Crown abandoned its indulgent attitude to aristocratic crime. Did it do so in reaction to changing social attitudes or as an assertion of its growing power? Such complex questions are well beyond the scope of this essay" (p. 17) — the book supplies the answer he declines to give, and should say so.

**Contradicts.** Not the mechanism; but Payling's Crown is not weak — it is *unwilling*, "a tolerance of violence on the part of a martial aristocracy" (p. 16). If the plural order was tolerated rather than unaffordable, "outspent" (ch. 8 §VII) is the wrong verb for England. *Best answer:* toleration was the price of a crown that could not pay for the alternative; when it could, it stopped tolerating (1557). Plausible, unproven here.

**Placement.** Ch. 8 §II by name, as T2 evidence and as the corrective to the Paston reading; Ch. 7 footnote on Say; Ch. 8 §VII for the 1557 threshold.

## 4. David Grummitt, "Henry VII, Chamber Finance and the 'New Monarchy': some new Evidence", *Historical Research* 72 (1999), 229–243 — T2, from three rediscovered clerks' books (PRO E 101/694/1, E 36/123, E 101/415/16)

**In a paragraph.** Heron's chamber books "are no more a straightforward ledger of receipts and payments than the medieval issue and receipt rolls" (p. 232 / PDF 4); beneath them ran Fowler's and Daunce's accounts, Tower deposits, the Calais pension and Dudley's cash, none entered in the king's book. The treasure was real; and "the system of revenue management employed by Henry VII was not simply a continuation of the policies of the Yorkist kings. It attempted rather to make all sources of revenue readily available for the execution of royal policy" (p. 243 / PDF 15).

**Confirms.** Ch. 11 §II's revenue figure: "Between 1502 and 1505 the average annual total of receipts recorded by Heron was £105,163" (p. 233 / PDF 5). Ch. 11 §II's paradox — "personalised … precisely in order to be institutionalised" — is Grummitt's conclusion: "the 'public' and 'private' facets of national finance were merged to reflect a fundamental reorganization of the way in which royal finance was perceived and managed and that this was driven above all by the personal energy of the king" (p. 241 / PDF 13); "a consolidated royal revenue" (p. 242 / PDF 14). Henry's involvement: "the pages written in his own hand recording the movement of cash are evidence of a real interest and involvement in administration" (p. 241).

**Corrects.** Manuscript (Ch. 11 §II) says "the machine was inherited and enlarged, not invented", carrying Wolffe; Grummitt p. 242 / PDF 14 says the Yorkist chamber "did not represent a financial refoundation of royal government", and p. 243 says Henry's system "was not simply a continuation". The Chrimes [GAP] closes here, on the revisionist side; Ross (1974) is the earlier critique (n. 1). Manuscript says "the sovereign as his own comptroller … signing off payments weekly"; Grummitt p. 234 / PDF 6: "The king's regular signing of the accounts presented by Heron does not represent Henry's daily supervision of some kind of royal 'current' account"; and p. 241: "the old picture of Henry's personal involvement in everyday administration may need revision: the king's money was not kept physically in the king's chamber but in a linked system of deposit treasuries". Manuscript's Wolffe table is a floor, not the fisc: £36,807 went to the Tower coffers 1502–05 and "were not entered in the king's book of receipts" (p. 235 / PDF 7); Wolffe's "all the receipts were spent" (p. 234) is wrong.

**Adds.** The fiscal-military use of the reserve: in 1497 the Cornish rebels' suppression "were, in the first instance, met by the treasurer of the chamber from the reserves stored in the king's coffers. Only afterwards were the coffers replenished by the tellers of the receipt from the proceeds of the grants of taxation" (p. 240 / PDF 12) — a standing cash reserve spent on coercion before the assembly's grant arrived. The scale: £108,000 lent to the Archduke on 25 April 1505 "was almost one year's entire nominal income" (p. 237 / PDF 9); Hoyle's inference from the 1512–17 gap — "very large sums had indeed been accumulated by Henry VII and frittered away on his son's first war" (p. 239 / PDF 11). The Burgundian joint (ch. 10 §VIII / Depreter): the change "may have been linked to his exposure to continental forms of royal authority" (p. 242, n. 60 to Gunn on Charles the Bold). The 1504 subsidy paid to the tellers, one of whom was Daunce: "the formal records mask an informal reality" (p. 234) — the assembly's grant entering the chamber through the exchequer's own door.

**Contradicts.** Nothing in the argument; the register changes. Grummitt's "new monarchy" is Pugh's — it "began and ended with Henry VII" (p. 243) — and the machine was informal, decentralised, hidden; the chapter's clean "national treasury" line should be dirtied.

**Placement.** Ch. 11 §II by name, T2, replacing the Wolffe sentence and the comptroller image; the Tudor Chamber Books note keeps its pin.

## 5. J. H. Elliott, "A Europe of Composite Monarchies", *Past & Present* 137 (1992), 48–71 — T2, the frame's source text

**In a paragraph.** Against Strayer's sovereign-state teleology, Koenigsberger's "most states in the early modern period were composite states" (p. 50 / PDF 4); "it should not automatically be taken for granted that at the turn of the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries this was already the destined end of the road" (p. 51 / PDF 5). Two legal forms: "accessory" union (the Indies to Castile; Wales 1536/1543) and union *aeque principaliter* — Solórzano's "These kingdoms must be ruled and governed as if the king who holds them all together were king only of each one of them" (p. 53 / PDF 7). Then the crux: "war and economic depression appeared to strengthen the case for the concentration of power. Resources had to be mobilized, economic activity directed, and crown revenues increased to meet the costs of defence. All this made a higher degree of union the order of the day" (p. 63 / PDF 17) — and the attempts (Olivares, Charles I) "unleashed counter-revolutionary movements" (p. 63); the options were "retreat, or a conquering, integrative, union in the style of Bohemia" (p. 64 / PDF 18). "The very looseness of the association was in a sense its greatest strength" (p. 69 / PDF 23). Written, he says, from "the vantage-point of 1992", when "multi-national political and economic organizations" and revived regionalisms "have simultaneously placed pressure on the nation state from above and beneath" (p. 49 / PDF 3).

**Confirms.** Ch. 12 §IV's per-bloc hybrid and spine §8(b): Elliott supplies its legal grammar — accessory versus *aeque principaliter* — and the reason the loose form endures: local elites' "measure of self-government which left them without any urgent need to challenge the status quo" (p. 57 / PDF 11). Ch. 12's EU as apparatus-without-centre: "The forces of one kingdom could be used to put down trouble in another … Multiple monarchies presented multiple opportunities as well as multiple constraints" (p. 69). Ch. 1's neomedieval diagnosis, in a Regius professor's voice (p. 49).

**Corrects.** Manuscript (Ch. 11 §V) says "the Habsburg composite followed its own path a generation later"; Elliott p. 53 places Aragon, Valencia, Catalonia, Sicily and Naples under *aeque principaliter* from the union of the crowns, and (p. 51) France itself among composites ("the mosaic of *pays d'élections* and *pays d'états*"), and England–Ireland and England–Wales (pp. 51–52). All four of ch. 11's consolidators were composite polities; Wales is an accessory union executed in the very decade of ch. 11 §V's print statutes. The already-filed correction (assess/Elliott.md §3) is confirmed at the coinage's source.

**Adds.** Ch. 11 §VII / Ch. 12 §IV: the Sforza thread ("operative but never invested") has an exact early-modern legal name — *aeque principaliter* — and the American reading (platforms keeping their own law under one crown) versus the Chinese (accessory incorporation) can be stated in Solórzano's categories. The mechanism's own timetable: "Seventeenth-century rulers … were everywhere talking the language of union" (p. 62 / PDF 16), and the reorderings of 1707–16 "bound their component parts closer to each other" (p. 67 / PDF 21), the Nueva Planta "the most unitary" — union by conquest of the periphery. And Elliott's closing sentence for the coda: "union *aeque principaliter* again becomes the order of the day" (pp. 70–71).

**Contradicts, at full strength.** Elliott runs the book's mechanism — war → mobilisation → union — and reports its failure: the unions attempted under fiscal pressure in the 1620s produced 1640, not 1485; between 1523 and 1707 "there were only three successful secessions from a composite monarchy" (p. 68 / PDF 22), so the loose form was not transitional but durable; "The eighteenth-century fiscal-military state, with more power at its disposal than its seventeenth-century predecessor … Yet the 'enlightened' monarchies of the eighteenth century remained essentially composite" (p. 70 / PDF 24). This reaches spine §7 ("the consolidation is compelled") and the DEFEND-tier chain's last link, suppression of intermediate authorities. Elliott also notes, against the decisive-force premise, that the one state that unified was "the most powerful state, France" (p. 65 / PDF 19). *Best answer:* the mechanism operates on the core (Castile, England, the Île-de-France) and the composite is what the core rules through — hybrid per bloc, exactly §8(b); Elliott's own sentence that France, the unified state, was also the most powerful is the mechanism's positive case, and Bohemia 1620 / Nueva Planta 1707–16 are late arrival "inside the pattern" (spine §7), consolidation completed by force when the fisc allowed it. Good answer, but it narrows the claim: the settlement suppresses the intermediate authorities *of the core* and contracts with those of the periphery — which is what ch. 12 should say about the United States' platforms and the EU's member states alike.

**Placement.** Ch. 11 §V and §VII by name as the rival frame (T2); Ch. 12 §IV by name for the two union types; Coda §V (what the book does not claim); Ch. 1 footnote for p. 49.

## 6. J. H. Baker, "The Law Merchant and the Common Law before 1700", *Cambridge Law Journal* 38 (1979), 295–322 — T2 (the founding English demolition of the *lex mercatoria* myth, twenty-five years before Kadens)

**In a paragraph.** "The medieval law merchant was not so much a corpus of mercantile practice or commercial law as an expeditious procedure especially adapted for the needs of men who could not tarry for the common law" (p. 301 / PDF 9); the one medieval treatise lists only three differences from the common law — speed, pledges, no wager of law — and "there is no suggestion that the law merchant had its own substantive law of contract" (p. 300 / PDF 8); "far from there having been a universal law throughout the world, the local variations seem as numerous as the coincidences" (p. 300). Merchants used the king's courts: the Common Pleas rolls "are full of commercial cases" (p. 302 / PDF 10). The mercantile courts declined as recorders "substituted some of the formality of the common law" (p. 306 / PDF 14), their records "removed into the King's Bench by writ of error" (p. 306), the statute of 1477 confessing that "feigned actions and embracery of jurors were rife" (p. 307 / PDF 15). Conclusion: "Mercantile customs were either local facts or they were the common law of England" (p. 321 / PDF 29); the common-law courts "did not operate those procedures by 'incorporating' a law merchant … They used them to create it" (p. 322 / PDF 30).

**Confirms.** Ch. 8 §V's side in the dispute, and at greater depth than Kadens: Baker names Berman and Kaufman (1978) as having "revived" the seventeenth-century natural-law doctrine (p. 296 n. 5 / PDF 4) — the manuscript's "romanticised medieval original" pinned to the reviver. Ch. 8 §VI's disanalogy, "fora parasitic on state law": stronger than parasitic — the state's courts *made* the substantive law. Ch. 8 §VII's Star Chamber: in the Carrier's Case (1473) "The Chancery and Star Chamber … were indifferent to all laws with respect to procedure, and could therefore bypass due process" (p. 302 / PDF 10) — the prerogative court as procedure, not new law.

**Corrects.** Manuscript (Ch. 8 §V) says the traditional account confined the law merchant "in their own piepowder and fair courts" and the medievalists "have dismantled it" citing Kadens (2012) and Sachs; the dismantling is Baker's, 1979, from the plea rolls, and the chapter should date it so. Manuscript (Ch. 8 §V) treats Admiralty as "the crown's own courts [drawing] knives on each other"; Baker's admiralty is one of the "special courts" of the orthodox story, its business captured by a King's Bench "not averse to capturing some of the business of the admiralty and borough courts" (p. 309 / PDF 17).

**Adds.** *Ch. 8 §VI, code-is-law:* Holt C.J. in *Clarke v. Martin* (1702) on promissory notes — "a new sort of specialty, unknown to the common law, and invented in Lombard-street, which attempted in these matters of bills of exchange to give laws to Westminster Hall" (p. 299 n. 18 / PDF 7) — and Baker's gloss: "it is not competent to private subjects thus to dictate changes in law to the courts" (p. 299). Lombard Street giving laws to Westminster Hall is the platform terms-of-service objection stated in 1702. *Ch. 8 §VII, the dated instruments:* 1466 (error lies from a steward's court), 1477 (17 Edw. IV c. 2), c. 1490 (Dudley's reading: a fair "shall be forfeited" if its piepowder court is abused, p. 307), 1520s (assumpsit "following exactly the formulae of Westminster Hall … even in courts of piepowder", p. 306) — the absorption by procedure, inside the chapter's 1470s–1540s window.

**Contradicts.** Baker's absorption is endogenous to the legal profession — inns-of-court recorders, the action of assumpsit "extended between 1450 and 1550" (p. 308 / PDF 16), writ of error — and it is unfinished in 1700: "no 'incorporation of the law merchant' into the common law before the eighteenth century" (p. 320 / PDF 28). Nothing fiscal or military appears. Ch. 8 §VII's "The plural legal environment was not argued out of existence; it was outspent" gets no support from the English law merchant. *Best answer:* the chapter claims sequence, not payment — the courts' reach followed the fisc's — and Baker's dates fall inside the window; but the honest statement is that for the merchant courts the driver Baker finds is professional and procedural, and the book should say so rather than let "outspent" cover it.

**Placement.** Ch. 8 §V by name, T2, ahead of Kadens; Ch. 8 §VI for Holt; Ch. 8 §VII footnote for the dated instruments, with the concession.

## 7. Joint verdict

None of the six changes the book's direction; three change its register and three add weight. Ágoston, with İnalcık, fixes the Ottoman case as the requirement demonstrated and then undone — foundry, payroll and register before any Latin monarchy, and "military devolution" (Ágoston p. 123) with the guns still in hand — so ch. 11 §IV must be rewritten to end in devolution and §V's "none was executable without it" must exempt the Ottomans; Elliott's 1992 essay is the rival frame stated at its source, and it narrows the DEFEND-tier claim to the core polity ruling a composite by contract, which is what §8(b)'s hybrid already says and ch. 12 should now say in Solórzano's terms; Grummitt corrects the Henry VII passage on both sides (not Yorkist continuation; not a personal ledger) and hands the chapter a cash reserve spent on rebels before the tax arrived. Payling and Baker add evidence and one warning each: the failure point of fifteenth-century justice was the king's pardon, not the sheriff, and the merchant courts were absorbed by procedure, not by money — so "outspent" is the wrong verb for England's law and the chapter must say sequence, not cause. The single most important thing the author should know: the manuscript's Çandarlı execution date (1 June 1453) is the date of the Pera *amān-nāme* that Zaganos signed, not the execution, which İnalcık places in August or September (p. 412) — and the same authority who fixes that date is the one whose Ottoman state, now triangulated by Ágoston, consolidated before the gun and devolved with it.
