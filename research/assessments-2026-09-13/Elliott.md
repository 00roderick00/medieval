# Assessment — J. H. Elliott, *Imperial Spain 1469–1716* (Edward Arnold 1963; Penguin reprint with new foreword dated 23 May 2001)

*Text: /home/claude/books/Elliott.txt, a Penguin e-book conversion with no printed page numbers, so pins are PDF page only (1 + form-feeds). Tables are dropped (the Orders' commanderies, PDF pp. 94–95; noble rent-rolls, PDF pp. 116–117); minor OCR faults ("LIQUIDATION OP", PDF p. 212; "silverver", PDF p. 333). Read in full: Foreword, Prologue, chs. 1–4 (PDF pp. 23–168), ch. 5 §§1–2 and 4–5 (PDF pp. 169–186, 203–215), ch. 9 §1 and ch. 10 (PDF pp. 321–333, 362–386), and the Philip II finance pages (PDF pp. 265–271, 286–288). Skimmed only: ch. 5 §3, ch. 6, ch. 7, the rest of chs. 8–9 (the 1590s, Olivares's wars, 1640). The phrase "composite monarchy" does not occur in this book — it is Elliott's 1992 *Past & Present* coinage; here the idea is "a plural, not a unitary, state" (PDF p. 90), and the manuscript should not cite this book for the phrase.*

## 1. The book in two paragraphs

Elliott's theme, stated in the 2001 foreword, is "the rise and decline of Spanish power" read through "the interaction over two and a half centuries of centre and periphery" (PDF p. 12), against the Francoist textbook "insistence on Spanish unity and the triumph of the centralizing state" (PDF p. 13). Spain, on his account, was not made unitary by Ferdinand and Isabella: "The new Spain was therefore a plural, not a unitary, state, and consisted of a series of separate patrimonies governed in accordance with their own distinctive laws" (PDF p. 90). Castile was consolidated; the Crown of Aragon was deliberately restored to its contractual constitution ("Where the foundations were being laid in Castile for absolute royal power, in Catalonia the old medieval contractual State was scrupulously restored", PDF p. 88); and the viceroy-plus-council solution "played a large part in determining the future structure of an expanding Spanish Monarchy" (PDF p. 89). Under Charles V this hardened into "a mere aggregation of territories almost fortuitously linked by a common sovereign", with the "'freezing' of the various constitutional systems" (PDF p. 172).

The book's judgement on the "new monarchy" is the passage that matters to the project. Elliott opens ch. 3 on the textbook category — Henry VII, Louis XI, the Catholic Kings — and says of Spain that it "diverged in so many respects from the theoretical model of the 'new monarchy', as to make it appear either that it must be entirely excluded from the European model, or alternatively that the model itself is at fault" (PDF p. 83). His resolution: "Like their contemporary, Henry VII of England, they laid the foundations of a new state, not by introducing new institutions but by revivifying old ones … The 'new monarchy' in Spain, as elsewhere in Europe, was in the first instance the old monarchy restored – but restored with a sense of royal authority and national purpose capable of launching it on a radically different course" (PDF p. 91). The centralised state arrives only with the Bourbon *Nueva Planta* of 16 January 1716, which "in effect marks the transformation of Spain from a collection of semi-autonomous provinces into a centralized State" — "too late, and … in the wrong way" (PDF pp. 377–378). Superseded, by Elliott's own admission (PDF pp. 14–15), on the Comuneros (Maravall), the Castilian Cortes and the Inquisition; still the canonical T2 narrative in English for the monarchy's institutional shape.

## 2. What it CONFIRMS

- Ch. 11 §III, the Hermandad: "reorganised the old municipal brotherhoods into the Santa Hermandad … the central council was dissolved (1498)." Elliott: "a perfect example of a medieval institution revived to meet new needs … placed under a unified central control in the form of a council or Junta of the Hermandad" (PDF p. 92); "It was only in 1498 that they finally agreed to suppress the Council of the Hermandad" (PDF p. 93); "essentially a temporary expedient devised to deal with an acute national emergency" (PDF p. 93) — the manuscript's "stood up, used, and wound down".
- Ch. 11 §III, the Orders as "over-mighty corporate subjects": "As long as their wealth and resources remained the preserve of a handful of magnates, they inevitably constituted a State within the State" (PDF p. 94); "a papal bull of 1523 definitively incorporated all three Orders into the Crown" (PDF p. 94), the revenues "permanently vested in it by Charles V's former tutor, Pope Adrian VI, in 1523" (PDF p. 205).
- Ch. 11 §III, the Inquisition as the only institution spanning the kingdoms: "the Inquisition was the one institution, apart from the monarchy itself, common to the possessions of Spain" (PDF p. 112); the Suprema "from 1483" (PDF p. 112); the expulsion edict signed "at Granada on 30 March 1492" (PDF p. 113); the "pragmatic ordering the expulsion of all unconverted adult Moors" published February 1502 (PDF p. 60).
- Ch. 11 §III, "absorption performed with the Church's own silverware": Elliott's own formulation is stronger — "the kings of Spain were to show that despoiling the Church was equally possible without going to the lengths of rupture with the Papacy, and that the long-term advantages of this method were at least as great, and probably greater" (PDF p. 205); the Catholic Kings obtained "a sufficient degree of control over the wealth of the Church to deprive their successors of any financial inducement to follow the example of a Gustavus Vasa or a Henry VIII" (PDF p. 107).
- Ch. 11 §II, the Wolffe corrective on Henry VII ("inherited and enlarged, not invented"): Elliott says the same of both monarchies — "not by introducing new institutions but by revivifying old ones" (PDF p. 91).
- Ch. 11 §VII / spine §8(b), "operative but never invested": "encomiendas never formally became hereditary", and "A great hereditary feudal aristocracy did not develop in the New World" (PDF p. 82).

## 3. What it CORRECTS

- Manuscript (Chapter 11 §III) says Alcántara's administratorship came to Ferdinand "under a bull of 1492"; Elliott PDF p. 94 says "When the Grand Masterships of Calatrava and Alcántara fell vacant in 1487 and 1494 respectively, they duly went to Ferdinand." The two may be reconciled (a bull authorising the succession before the vacancy) but the manuscript should not carry 1492 as the date of acquisition without a source that says so.
- Manuscript (Chapter 11 §III) says Santiago's administratorship came to Ferdinand in 1499, "the date this book carries against the older 1476 tradition." Elliott (PDF p. 94) explains what 1476 was: Isabella rode to Uclés "to insist that the proceedings should be suspended and the office be conferred upon her husband. In the event, Ferdinand graciously waived his claim for the time being, but the correct precedent had been satisfactorily established." The manuscript should state 1476 as the precedent and 1499 as the tenure, not oppose them.
- Manuscript (Chapter 11 §III) says the Hermandad "financed the Granada war." Elliott does not carry this; his account is that its cost "was very heavy, and it was met by fines and by a system of taxation", and that the Crown kept it because it "could provide them with companies of archers to fight against the Moors" (PDF p. 93). Keep the financing claim only if it rests on a source that makes it (Ladero Quesada), not on Elliott.
- Manuscript (Chapter 8) says "the audiencia of Valladolid reorganised by the Catholic Monarchs in 1485, its southern twin settled at Granada in 1505." Elliott gives no 1485, calls the court the *chancillería*, and supplies the missing step: "in 1494 a second chancillería was set up at Ciudad Real. This chancillería was transferred to Granada in 1505" (PDF p. 102).
- Manuscript (Chapter 5 §VII) says the decades that broke England and France "contain Portugal stable, Aragon expanding". Elliott contradicts the second: the Crown of Aragon in 1450–79 is "a society in retreat" (PDF p. 51) — Barcelona's trade "began to founder in the years round 1450" (PDF p. 48), the Generalitat "renounced its allegiance to the King and prepared for war" in the civil war of 1462–72, Louis XI annexed Roussillon and Cerdagne in 1463 (PDF p. 49). Castile is the same: "The Castilian kings, their title dubious, had become pawns in the hands of the magnates; the Cortes were disunited and ineffectual; government had broken down, public order had collapsed" (PDF p. 44), then civil war 1475–79. Iberia belongs inside ch. 5's modal condition; Portugal alone stands (PDF p. 51).
- Manuscript (Chapter 11 §V) says "the Habsburg composite followed its own path a generation later." On Elliott's account the Spanish monarchy was plural from 1479: the Council of Aragon (1494) and permanent viceroyalties were Ferdinand's, "a solution specifically designed to preserve intact the political and administrative identity of the Crown of Aragon" (PDF p. 89). The Habsburgs inherited the composite; they did not build it.

## 4. What it ADDS

*Ch. 11 §III, the Spanish fiscal move (the section currently has no fiscal limb).* Elliott supplies it: "the total revenue from taxation, which is said to have stood at under 900,000 reales in 1474, had risen to 26,000,000 reales by 1504. The increase was achieved not by the imposition of new taxes, but by the more efficient handling of old ones" (PDF p. 97) — alcabala and tercias "entirely independent of Cortes control" (PDF p. 98), "some 80 per cent to 90 per cent of the Crown's entire income" c. 1500 (PDF p. 206); tercias perpetual by bull of 1494, the cruzada perpetuated after the Reconquista (PDF pp. 107–108). And the mechanism's Spanish joint: the Granada war "was essentially a war of sieges, in which the role of artillery and infantry was pre-eminent" (PDF p. 54), and "the war of Granada forced the Catholic Kings to raise loans and to sell juros or annuities, and financial necessity compelled them to resort to the Cortes in 1501 for a servicio" (PDF p. 98). Siege war → debt → church revenue → the absorbed Orders "shortly afterwards assigned to the bankers as security for their loans" (PDF p. 205): the rival annexed, then pledged.

*Ch. 11 §III, subordination of great subjects.* The Act of Resumption of 1480, "by which the nobles were deprived of half the revenues they had alienated or usurped since 1464" (PDF p. 95) — the English resumptions' analogue, with Elliott's limit: "They had destroyed the political power of the great nobility, but left its economic and social influence untouched" (PDF p. 130). The Consejo Real of 1480, magnates "allowed no vote" (PDF p. 95); the Orders yielding "nearly 1,500 dignities … at the disposal of the Crown" (PDF p. 95), with Marineo Sículo's rentals — Santiago 60,000 ducats, Alcántara 45,000, Calatrava 40,000 (PDF p. 94).

*Ch. 11 §III and ch. 8, legibility.* "The generalization of the office of corregidor was incomparably the most effective measure taken by Ferdinand and Isabella to extend the Crown's power over the Castilian municipalities" (PDF p. 100); and the Comunero Junta's demand of 20 October 1520 "that no corregidor should in future be appointed, except at the specific request of the town concerned" (PDF p. 158). Move five has two Spanish lines: the Bishop of Ávila's reply to Isabella on Nebrija's 1492 grammar, "language is the perfect instrument of empire" (PDF p. 132; printing reached Spain in 1473); and "government by paper" (PDF p. 175) — Simancas ordered 1543–45, "a central archive worthy of the new bureaucratic State" (PDF p. 176).

*Ch. 11 §III, domestication of force.* The Spanish standing force was made abroad: Gonzalo de Córdoba's Italian campaigns "saw the creation of a professional army" (PDF p. 137), coronelías reorganised as tercios in 1534 (PDF p. 138). At home: Cisneros's *gente de la ordenanza* of 1516, "some 30,000 well-equipped men … a kind of standing army" (PDF p. 146); and Charles's return in 1522 "as absolute master to a cowed and subdued Castile. But this time he had taken the precaution of coming with 4,000 German soldiers at his side" (PDF p. 164). Elliott's verdict on Villalar: "Castilian liberty, crushed and defenceless in face of the restored royal power" (PDF p. 168). One artillery detail: the regency's force, failing at Segovia, "moved to the great arsenal town of Medina del Campo in search of siege artillery, only to be met with fierce resistance by the townspeople" — and burnt the city on 21 August 1520 (PDF p. 160).

*Ch. 4 and ch. 11 §VII, the Sforza thread.* The *capitulación*: "the Crown was clearly bargaining away many of its rights, but generally it had no alternative" (PDF p. 67), yet it refused Columbus a hereditary viceroyalty because that "would have turned Spain's overseas territories into the feudal domain of a Genoese explorer" (PDF p. 68); "The conquerors of the New World themselves fell victims to the bureaucrats of the Old" (PDF p. 74). The paymaster-state pattern (chs. 3–4), worked in the sixteenth century.

*Ch. 10 / ch. 12, the credit state.* Charles V, "whose normal annual revenue as King of Spain was about 1,000,000 ducats a year, rising to 1,500,000 after 1542, was able to borrow 39,000,000 ducats on the strength of the credit of Castile" (PDF p. 210); "by 1543 65 per cent of these ordinary revenues were devoted to the payment of annuities" (PDF p. 211); bankruptcies 1557, 1575, 1596 (PDF pp. 214, 265, 288).

*Appendix B (empty).* The Spain row can be filled from ch. 3 alone: fiscal (PDF pp. 97–98, 107–108); subordination (PDF pp. 94–95, 116–118); force (PDF pp. 92–93, 137–138, 146, 164); canon — the Patronato of Granada, 13 December 1486, to the universal Patronato of 28 July 1508 and presentation to all Spanish bishoprics in 1523 (PDF pp. 106–107); legibility (PDF pp. 100, 132, 175–176).

## 5. What it CONTRADICTS

At full strength, in Elliott's words. First, the category the chapter runs: "If, then, the introduction of administrative uniformity and the centralizing of power in the monarch's hands were essential features of the Renaissance State, the Spain of Ferdinand and Isabella would scarcely seem to qualify" (PDF p. 88; and PDF p. 83, quoted in §1). Second, the polity the mechanism predicts. The greatest military power of the sixteenth century was not a consolidated fiscal-administrative state but a plurality: "the kingdoms must be ruled and governed as if the king who holds them all together were king only of each one of them" (PDF p. 172); the Crown of Aragon paid "a total of only some 500,000 ducats for each five-year period" and "was paying at the end of the reign no more than it was paying in the early years, in spite of the fact that prices had more than doubled" (PDF p. 206); and the decisive force this composite fielded for a century and a half ran on Castile, credit and silver, not on a bloc-wide fisc — "the dominance of foreign bankers over the country's sources of wealth" (PDF p. 211). Third, the consolidation attempt failed. Olivares's Union of Arms of 1626, the one deliberate attempt at "*un rey, una ley, y una moneda*" (PDF p. 332), was refused by the Cortes of Aragon, Valencia and Catalonia; the seventeenth century became "not federalism by conviction but federalism by default" (PDF p. 364); and the unitary state arrived in 1707–16 by conquest of the periphery, "arbitrarily imposed on the wealthier peripheral regions, to be held there by force – the force of an economically retarded Castile" (PDF p. 378). Fourth, against the manuscript's reading of the Inquisition as "canonical assertion, executed as annexation": "it has yet to be proved that Ferdinand saw in it a weapon for destroying local autonomy and furthering the process of centralization" (PDF p. 112). Fifth, in passing: "A few small cannon and thirteen muskets can hardly have been the decisive factor in overthrowing an empire more than ten million strong" (PDF p. 73).

This reaches spine §6 (DEFEND: "cannon → fiscal-administrative discipline → consolidation") and §8(b) (hybrid settlement per bloc), and the Chapter 11 claim that four polities "executed documentable variants of the same five moves".

**The book's best answer.** (i) The mechanism operates on the fiscal core, and Elliott's Castile executes it: revenue up nearly thirtyfold in thirty years outside assembly control, the magnates' political power broken, the Church's revenue annexed without schism, the corregidor in every town. Elliott's own sentence — "in Castile at least, they left behind them a state far more subordinate at every level to royal authority than the one they had found" (PDF p. 91) — is the manuscript's claim with a boundary drawn round it. (ii) The composite is the hybrid settlement §8(b) predicts per bloc; the American case (a Castile with an unconsolidated Aragon of platforms) is its nearest modern shape, and "operative but never invested" is how the Crown held its contractors. (iii) Elliott makes the mechanism's negative case himself: the periphery's fiscal immunity is why Spain lost after 1650 to states "building up their power by a more rational exploitation of their economic possibilities and their military and financial resources" (PDF p. 379); the 1716 *Nueva Planta* is late arrival "inside the pattern" (spine §7).

Is the answer good? Partly. (i) is sound. (ii) must be argued, not asserted. (iii) is the weak point: a mechanism that treats a 250-year deferral as "inside the pattern" risks unfalsifiability, and Elliott's Spain was the decisive power of its age *without* completing the settlement — so the settlement is not a precondition of decisive force but of *sustaining* it. That is narrower than Chapter 11 currently claims, and the chapter should say so.

## 6. Placement

- **Ch. 11 §III** — T2, engaged **by name in the text**, ally and rival: the fiscal limb from PDF pp. 97–98, 205–211; the "new monarchy" passage (PDF pp. 83, 91) where the chapter declares its position on the category; the Castile/Aragon boundary (PDF p. 88).
- **Ch. 11 §V and §VII** — by name, rival frame: the plural monarchy (PDF pp. 90, 172) against the Tudor turn; the capitulación material (PDF pp. 67–68, 74, 82) for the Sforza thread.
- **Ch. 12, the EU passage** — by name: "An empire was wanted to meet the attack of an empire" (PDF p. 173) is the composite's defensive logic; "federalism by default" (PDF p. 364) the failure mode the Depreter test scores.
- **Ch. 5 §VII** (PDF pp. 44–51), **ch. 8** (PDF pp. 100–103), **ch. 6/10** (PDF pp. 54, 98) — footnotes.
- **Appendix B** — source row for Spain, the 2001 foreword's superseded points (Comuneros, Cortes, Inquisition) to be checked against Edwards 2000 and Lynch 1991.

## 7. Verdict

The book changes Chapter 11's register, not the project's direction: it supplies the Spanish fiscal limb the chapter lacks and, in the same pages, the strongest T2 statement that the "new monarchy" category does not fit Spain and that the monarchy stayed plural for two centuries. The weight it adds is real — Castile 1474–1504 is a clean execution of the mechanism — but the comparator must be relabelled as the consolidated core of a composite, with the composite named as the rival frame and the American case read against it. The single most important thing the author should know is Elliott's boundary sentence (PDF p. 91, "in Castile at least"): the Spanish evidence supports the mechanism inside the fisc and refutes the claim that decisive force required completing the settlement across the polity — which narrows the DEFEND-tier claim from precondition to condition of endurance.
