# Michael Howard, *War in European History* (OUP, 1976) — assessment against *The Artillery State*

Text read: /home/claude/books/Howard.txt, whole book (~52k words). Pins give "PDF p. N / printed p. N" from the running heads. **OCR warning, serious:** most recto (odd) printed pages are cropped at the left margin and lose the first words of each line; printed pp. 15, 19, 27, 31, 37, 55, 137–139 are only partly recoverable and are quoted only where the surviving words are unambiguous. Worse, printed p. 14 — where Howard treats the artillery of 1449–53 — has a block of lines dropped entirely (see §3). The PDF/printed offset is 6 to PDF p. 99 (blank) and 7 thereafter.

## 1. The book in two paragraphs

Howard's Radcliffe Lectures (Warwick, 1975), expanded: an institutional history of European war from the Carolingians to the 1970s, organised by who fought and who paid. Each military form is the product and then the producer of a social and fiscal order, and the state emerges as the only entity able to sustain the escalating cost of decisive force: "So there began a process of expensive escalation all too familiar in our own day" (PDF p. 9 / printed p. 3). On the end of the medieval order: "It was not the French chivalry who finally bundled the English back into their island, effective as the work of Joan of Arc no doubt was in rousing them. It was another professional group who enjoyed no social status whatever and were barely accorded even the humble status of soldiers: the gunners" (PDF p. 19 / printed p. 13).

The sixteenth-century chapter states the mechanism in almost the book's own terms: "For by the sixteenth century the price of independence was becoming very high indeed. It was not only the spectacular artillery trains which had to be paid for but the no less expensive and necessary professional infantry. So had fortifica[tions]… [only the prince who could extract taxes] from his subjects, or the credit to raise loans from the new banking houses like the Fugger and the Welser, could put armies into the field which steam-rollered their lesser opponents into oblivion" (PDF p. 28 / printed p. 22; bracketed words lost to the scan). The professionals chapter carries Howard's flywheel, with a note to Finer in Tilly: the professional army, "itself made possible [by the] control acquired by the state over the [resources of the] community, enabled the state to acquire yet [more control] over those resources by serving as an instrument, [not only of external] defence but of internal compulsion" (PDF p. 61 / printed p. 55, cropped; n. 1, PDF p. 153 / printed p. 145, cites Finer in Tilly, ed., *The Formation of National States in Western Europe*, 1975).

## 2. What it CONFIRMS

- **Chapter 6 §VI, the gunners' low status.** Manuscript: "the man who chose the ground was an accountant of guns" (Basin's *vir plebeius*). Howard: the gunners "enjoyed no social status whatever" (PDF p. 19 / printed p. 13); cannon and hand gun "degraded war, putting as they did the noble man-at-arms at the mercy of the vile and base-born" (PDF p. 20 / printed p. 14).
- **Chapter 6 §VI, the honest weighting of Formigny.** Manuscript: "Formigny… is not the battle field artillery won." Howard: "The most striking innovation of the late Middle Ages, fire-arms, were slow to take effect on the battlefield, and this was particularly true of artillery. In siege warfare the great guns were indeed to have profound and lasting effects, but as auxiliaries to the armies of the day their effectiveness was [limited by] the problems involved in deploying them on the battlefield" (PDF p. 36 / printed p. 30). Howard is the siege-not-field witness the chapter wants — see §3 for what that does to the attribution.
- **Chapter 10 §II–III, the Estates and the fisc.** "Parliaments, Estates, assemblies representative of the non-military, non-noble sections of the community, began to figure largely in the capacity of the prince to make war" (PDF p. 23 / printed p. 17); "[the] original grant was made by the Estates to Charles VII of France in 1439. In 1444 he issued *ordonnances* whereby a number of the mercenary bands pullulating about the country were taken into the royal service on a permanent basis and used to forcibly disband the rest" (PDF p. 24 / printed p. 18) — ch3 §II's absorb-then-destroy sequence, at T3.
- **Chapter 10 §VI, the Swiss as control group.** Manuscript: "The Swiss won the battles and sold the instrument." Howard: "war was for them a nationalized [indus]try" (PDF p. 33 / printed p. 27, cropped); "the Swiss pike phalanxes became left behind like dinosaurs unable to adapt to a new environment, as much of a curiosity in the history of infantry as the English bowman" (PDF p. 34 / printed p. 28). Also: the "revival of infantry was due far more [to economic] and thus social factors than to any technical [innovation]" (PDF p. 21 / printed p. 15, badly cropped — verify on the page image).
- **Chapter 10 §VI, Burgundy.** Cropped: Charles the Bold came "[to] grief at the hands of the Swiss and the French [and prevented] Burgundy from establishing itself, as it quite [well might have] done, as one of the great nations of Europe" (PDF p. 25 / printed p. 19). Weak pin.
- **Ch6 §II / ch10 §V, Charles VIII.** "Charles VIII had to besiege very few Italian fortresses in 1494; the mere reputation of his artillery brought them to terms" (PDF p. 41 / printed p. 35).
- **Chapter 8, private war.** "The consolidation of princely power put an end to what medieval jurists called 'private war'" (PDF p. 29 / printed p. 23), with Bonet: "nowadays every man wishes to have the right of making war, even simple knights, and by law this cannot be" (ibid.; n. 1: Coopland's 1949 edition). The manuscript does not use Bonet; it should.
- **Chapter 4, the condottieri.** "Machiavelli's scornful accusation that they fought bloodless battles is not borne out by the facts" (PDF p. 32 / printed p. 26); and the sequel to the closed Sforza route: "none of them succeeded in transforming their military effectiveness into political power. Wallenstein came nearest… the peace settlement in 1648… confirmed as *de jure* sovereign powers only those that had existed *de facto*" (PDF p. 36 / printed p. 30).

## 3. What it CORRECTS

**The Castillon attribution is, on this evidence, wrong or at best unverifiable.** Manuscript (Chapter 6 §VI) says "Michael Howard judged flatly that Castillon was in no sense a victory for field artillery." The words "Castillon", "Talbot", "Formigny" and "1453" occur nowhere in the OCR text, and the index (PDF pp. 161–70) runs "Castille… Castles" with no "Castillon" between; no entry for Talbot or Bureau. Howard's only treatment of 1449–53 is printed p. 14 (PDF p. 20), where the recoverable text reads: "Both weapons were integrated into the revived French armies of the fifteenth century and used effectively against an England where a combination of military nostalgia and political confusion held back any comparable innovations. On the battlefield a few artillery discharges at the beginning shattered the ranks of the archers and enabled the men-at-arms to close for hand-to-hand fighting on equal terms. More [—lines lost—] looked on as a historical curiosity." The lost block is where the siege point sits (index: "Castles, siege artillery and, 14"; ch. 1 n. 6, PDF p. 152 / printed p. 144, cites Oman ii. 384 and Lot, *L'Art militaire* ii. 8–15 — Lot's Formigny–Castillon pages). Two consequences. (i) The sentence cannot be pinned from this scan; physical p. 14 must be checked before Howard is named. (ii) What *is* recoverable runs the other way: Howard credits field artillery with shattering the archers' ranks — a stronger claim than the manuscript's careful "the guns did not break the line." My judgement: the formulation is probably a misremembered Bert Hall (*Weapons and Warfare in Renaissance Europe*, 1997) or Contamine — a lead, not a finding. Until pinned, stage the dissent without Howard's name, or with Howard cast correctly: the historian who thought field artillery marginal *in general* before Gustavus (printed p. 30) while holding the gunners decisive *in siege* in 1449–53 (printed pp. 13–14). That is a subtler and more useful dissent than the one the draft stages.

**Do not import Howard's dates.** He gives the *ordonnances* as 1444 (printed p. 18) where the manuscript's Contamine-pinned 26 May 1445 is right; "the ending of the Hundred Years War… in 1361" (printed p. 25) for Brétigny 1360; the Visconti as condottieri dynasts beside the Sforza (printed p. 26), which they were not. His "original grant… in 1439" is the shorthand the spine's 2 September ruling on §8(a) retires. The stirrup-to-feudalism opening (printed p. 2, from Lynn White) is a contested 1976 commonplace. Nothing in Howard corrects a manuscript figure.

## 4. What it ADDS

1. **Closes the Belloc GAP in ch6 §VI.** The draft says "the Belloc 1898 text is not in corpus and is not quoted here." Howard quotes it, in a footnote: "The confident reassurance of Hilaire Belloc's Captain [Blood]… 'Whatever happens, we have got / The Maxim gun, and they have not' was not always valid, as the British survivors of the Zulu victory at Isandhlwana in 1879 and the Italian survivors of the Ethiopian victory at Adowa in 1896 would have been able to testify. Even superior weapons, if deployed without tactical skill and used against forces superior in leadership and courage, did not necessarily guarantee victory" (PDF p. 128 / printed p. 121 n.; the name after "Captain" is OCR-lost). A T3 carrier for the couplet with the weapon-not-process caveat attached. Freedman is still wanted for the genealogy.
2. **The Swiss doctrine of overrunning the guns — for the Talbot conjecture (spine §8(e)4).** "[the] immediate objective of the Swiss was always to overrun the enemy guns before they could do any serious harm. But with [the] low rate of fire and the inaccuracy which resulted from [the com]bination of the recoil and of the 'windage'… they often did no harm at all" (PDF p. 37 / printed p. 31, cropped). This gives the conjecture what it lacks — a documented contemporary tactical rationale for charging a gun-park early, from the century's best infantry. It stays conjecture for Talbot, but it moves the early-attack reading from "lazy" to one of two live professional doctrines. Ch6 §VI, beside the Formigny seizure.
3. **The Brandenburg ratchet, 1653 — a second worked case for §8(a).** "the Estates were prevailed upon to agree to the introduction of royal officials throughout the land to assess and levy the tax… the *Generalkriegskommissariat*. So they forfeited, in this essential particular, their traditional right—the real guarantee of their independence—to tax themselves. They lived to regret it" (PDF p. 73 / printed p. 67; the sequel at PDF p. 74 / printed p. 68). The 1439–46 settlement, with no Estates General until 1484, has here its Prussian rhyme; ch10 §VIII or ch11.
4. **The longbow/cannon dichotomy stated in Bull's year.** "By the 1970s the development of, in particular, precision-guided missiles whose launchers were capable of rapid movement on the battlefield and even portable by infantry, were beginning to cast doubt on… the dominance over the battlefield of manned aircraft, and the dominance on the battlefield of the heavy battle tank"; yet "such weapons—missiles, supersonic aircraft, nuclear-powered submarines, and anti-submarine frigates—were beyond the capacity of all but the most technologically advanced countries to manufacture" (PDF p. 146 / printed p. 138). Intro or ch12 §I, as the register's pedigree.
5. **The trace italienne, which the manuscript never names.** "Fire could only be countered by fire… This led to 'the bastioned trace'… Fortifications of this kind, at first improvised ad hoc by the Italian cities in the last decade of the fifteenth century, spread all over Europe during the next fifty years, a matter as much of civic prestige as of military necessity" (PDF p. 41 / printed p. 35). Ch6 §VII engages Rogers and Curry but not Parker's fortification counter-turn; a paragraph is owed, and Howard is its T3 entry point.
6. **Davenant, 1695**, for ch10 or ch11: "that prince who can best find money to feed, cloath and pay his army, not he that has the most valiant troops, is surest of success and conquest" (PDF p. 54 / printed p. 48; n. 10: *Essay upon Ways and Means*, 1695, p. 16). Also "pas d'argent, pas de Suisses" (PDF p. 44 / printed p. 38); and Guibert's 1772 prophecy of a people "not reduced to laying down its arms through financial need" (PDF p. 80 / printed p. 74) as the counter-mechanism, if ch12 wants it.

## 5. What it CONTRADICTS

Two passages cut against the spine, and both are DEFEND-tier (§6: "the mechanism — cannon → fiscal-administrative discipline → consolidation; and its present-day operation").

**(a) Institutions before tools.** "Developments in weapon technology alone do not provide an adequate explanation of the change which came over European warfare between the time of Gustavus Adolphus and that of Frederick the Great. The really significant changes took place, not in the tools with which the armies operated, but in the structure of the armies themselves and of the states which employed them. Indeed it is doubtful whether the improvements in weapons would have taken place at all, much less have been fully exploited, if… full-time state officials had not been in a position to take decisions about their development, arrange for their manufacture and supply, and pay for them" (PDF p. 68 / printed p. 62). And for 1494–1648: "these developments were due, not to any striking technological or scientific advances, but rather to a process of trial and error… within a very stable and limited technological framework" (PDF p. 36 / printed p. 30). Howard's causal arrow, for the early modern state, points from administration to weapon.

**(b) Technical superiority is not decisive between peers.** "In a society economically so close-knit as that of Europe, any advantage given by the possession of technically superior weapons was temporary and unlikely in itself to be decisive. Numbers were what mattered" (PDF p. 128 / printed p. 121); the naval race is the *exception*, "where it looked as if it might provide an advantage sufficient not just to win a battle but to win a war and to establish a new kind of political dominance" (PDF p. 129 / printed p. 122). This bears directly on spine §4 ("the cannon of today… will be decisive on the battlefield") and the ch12 wager; Howard's nuclear-age close — "It would be premature even to attempt to answer the questions posed" (PDF p. 144 / printed p. 136) — is Bull's temperament.

**The book's best answer.** To (a): spine §8(a) already concedes co-evolution and claims only the ratchet, and Howard's *own* fifteenth-century chapter is technology-forward (the gunners "bundled the English back"; "the price of independence was becoming very high"). His institutions-first passage is about 1630–1740, not the book's hinge, and his Brandenburg case shows the army-as-tax-collector ratchet operating as §8(a) describes. Good, but it costs something: the book must say openly that the ratchet is a claim about *one* window, 1440–1500, and that later Howard's arrow may run the other way. To (b): the mechanism is about the *price threshold* of decisive force, not a technical lead — Howard's exceptions (Portuguese guns in the Indian Ocean, printed p. 121; the Dreadnought race) are cases where the capital threshold rose faster than diffusion. Adequate but not complete: "numbers were what mattered" is a real finding for 1870–1945, and the AI-stack wager depends on the present resembling 1449 more than 1914. Ch12 should meet this by name. Neither contradiction reaches a §8 ruling as written; both bear on the DEFEND-tier mechanism's scope.

Minor: Howard's Formigny — "a few artillery discharges at the beginning shattered the ranks of the archers" (printed p. 14) — overstates against the manuscript's Berry Herald account. The manuscript is better sourced; note, do not adopt.

## 6. Placement

- **Chapter 6 §VI**: engaged BY NAME, but recast — not as author of a Castillon verdict he does not appear to have given, but as T3 witness for field artillery's general weakness before 1630 (printed p. 30) held together with his siege-decisive gunners (printed pp. 13–14); the Swiss overrun-the-guns doctrine (printed p. 31) beside the Talbot conjecture; the Belloc footnote (printed p. 121 n.) closing that GAP. Role: T3, ally with reservations.
- **Chapter 6 §VII**: footnote, the missing trace-italienne paragraph; Howard as entry to Parker.
- **Chapter 4 §V–VI**: footnote, Wallenstein/1648 (printed p. 30); Machiavelli corrected (printed p. 26). **Chapter 8**: Bonet via Howard, footnote, pending Coopland.
- **Chapter 10 §II–III, §VI**: footnotes — "price of independence" (printed p. 22); Swiss dinosaurs (printed p. 28); Burgundy (printed p. 19, weak pin). §VIII or ch11: Brandenburg 1653 by name as the ratchet's second worked case; Finer/Tilly through Howard's n. 1 (printed p. 55) where the book differentiates itself from Tilly.
- **Chapter 12 §I or Intro**: Howard 1976 by name for the PGM/platform dichotomy (printed p. 138) and, as rival, for "numbers were what mattered" (printed p. 121). Appendix A: one line — not a neomedievalist, but the military historian writing in Bull's year with Bull's caution.

## 7. Verdict

Howard changes neither the book's direction nor its register; it changes its weight, and it removes one prop — the Castillon sentence attributed to him is not in this text and the recoverable page 14 says something closer to the opposite, so the staged "dissent" must be re-sourced or re-cast before print. What the book gains is larger: a 1976 authority, contemporaneous with Bull, who states the gunners-not-chivalry thesis, the "price of independence", the Swiss control group, the Brandenburg tax ratchet, the Belloc couplet and the cheap-missile/costly-platform dichotomy in his own words. The single most important thing the author should know is that Howard's causal arrow runs institutions-to-weapons for 1630–1740 (printed p. 62) but weapons-to-institutions for 1449–53 (printed p. 13) — a gift, because it is the spine's own §8(a) claim that the ratchet belongs to one window, said by the historian the draft was about to quote against itself.
