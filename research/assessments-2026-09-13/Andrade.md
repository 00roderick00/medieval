# Assessment — Tonio Andrade, *The Gunpowder Age: China, Military Innovation, and the Rise of the West in World History* (Princeton, 2016)

*Text read: /home/claude/books/Andrade.txt (EPUB conversion, no form feeds, no printed page numbers). Pins are by chapter title and section heading. Read in full: Introduction; Part I (chs 1–4); Part II (chs 5–9); Part III (chs 10–15); Conclusions; Appendix 2; the endnotes for chs 6, 8 and 18. Part IV (chs 16–18) was read at the opening of ch 16 and the closing pages of ch 18 (the Qing fiscal passage), the rest by targeted search only — it concerns 1839–1900 and does not bear on the manuscript's period. The text is clean; the only garble is Andrade's own — see §3.*

## 1. The book in two paragraphs

Andrade's argument is that the gunpowder age is a millennium long and Chinese for its first half, and that the divergence between China and Europe is not cultural but rhythmic — innovation follows the frequency of warfare. "The unifying theme is gunpowder warfare" (Introduction, "The Military Pattern of the Chinese Past"); China led "from the 900s through the mid-1400s"; a "first divergence, or the little divergence" opens 1450–1550 when "China engaged in fewer and less intense wars, and military innovation slowed" while Europe's wars intensified; an "Age of Parity (1550–1700)" follows in which "Whenever trained military forces from East Asia met those of Europe, the former won decisively"; and the "Great Military Divergence" of the Opium War is the product of the "Great Qing Peace" (1760–1839) plus European experimental science. The engine throughout is "the 'challenge-response dynamic'" (Introduction), which he takes from Parker and turns against the "competitive state system" paradigm by showing China had its own "Song Warring States Period" and a century of existential war around the Yuan–Ming transition.

For this manuscript the decisive chapters are 6 and 8. Chapter 6 asks "Why Western Europe and Not China Developed Gunpowder Artillery" and answers with walls: "Is it possible that China's massive walls were one of the key reasons guns developed differently in China than in Europe?"; "In China artillery would not have repaid the heavy investments … the massive tamped-earth walls of China acted as a deterrent to the development of gunpowder artillery" (ch 6, "European and Chinese Walls"). Chapter 8 then turns on the gunpowder-revolution thesis itself: "Perhaps guns hastened the end of Europe's decentralized system, but feudalism was probably doomed in any case" and "Defeudalization would likely have occurred without guns" (ch 8, opening section). The Conclusions summarise the walls point flatly: "it made sense for European leaders to allocate the huge sums necessary to manufacture, maintain, and deploy artillery, whereas in China large guns were simply not worth the investment" (Conclusions, "A New Warring States Period?").

## 2. What it CONFIRMS

**The price of the train and who could pay it (Chapter 6 §VII; Chapter 10 §VII).** The manuscript: "a first-rate siege train cost more than a government's entire traditional arsenal, and the cost recurred." Andrade, ch 6, "European and Chinese Walls": "Large guns were enormously expensive to make, to transport, and even to fire … a shot from a sixteenth-century cannon cost the equivalent of a month's wages for an infantry soldier. The kings and dukes of Europe paid these exorbitant sums because it was worth it." The Rogers cycle is restated as the received view: "the more control a state managed to achieve, the more revenue it could raise, the more guns it could buy … It's a widely held notion, nearly ubiquitous" (ch 8, opening) — useful precisely because Andrade then attacks it (§5).

**The tempo shift and the délai de secours (Chapter 6 §I–§IV).** Andrade, ch 6, "Big Guns": Pierre DuBois c. 1300, "a 'castle can hardly be taken within a year …'. A century later, European walls were falling with alarming regularity"; Gibbon quoted on "the proportion of the attack and defence was suspended"; and the list of Henry V's Norman towns with the caveat the manuscript already carries — "Not all these walls were destroyed by guns — many citizens found it expedient to surrender before the first shots were fired." The surrender-not-siege discipline of Chapter 6 has an independent T2 witness.

**Constantinople as a fiscal, not merely technical, event (Chapter 5 §IV; Chapter 11 §IV).** "The idea that technology destroyed Constantinople is overstated … The Byzantines were vastly outspent. The cannon maker Urban wasn't the only one who felt he was poorly paid" (ch 6, "Big Guns"). And on the Ottoman apparatus: "Ottoman armies were relatively centralized from an early date, had their own specialized firearms bureaus by the 1390s" (ch 11, "Drill and the Classical Heritage, East and West"). Both go straight into the Ottoman limb.

**Burgundy and the mortal node (Chapter 10 §VI).** "The dukes of Burgundy also 'supported "research and development" of all aspects of gunpowder weaponry technology' … the map of Europe might look very different today if it hadn't been for the disastrous leadership (and sonlessness) of the final Duke of Burgundy, Charles the Bold" (ch 6, "Big Guns"). The Depreter refinement — apparatus without a survivable dynastic centre — is stated by a historian with no stake in it.

**The militia principle fails; the payroll wins (Chapter 10 §II).** Andrade's citation of Morillo: "'In effect, strong infantry depends on strong government'" (ch 11, "Drill and the Classical Heritage"). This also cuts the other way — see §5.

**The negative case for the present (Chapter 12 §IV–V; spine §5).** Andrade on the Qing in the 1880s–90s: "Qing central governmental revenues probably came to around two percent of China's GDP … less than half the average of other late nineteenth-century states"; "the Qing lacked a fiscal state, so unlike Japan it had little capacity to borrow money for military investments"; and the rule, "Military power depends closely on state power, especially in the modern age" (ch 18, closing section). The Russia paragraph's logic applied to nineteenth-century China: weapons at parity, decision impossible without the fisc.

**The gates, not the walls (Chapter 6 §V; capitulation-without-siege).** "the most common way the armies of China got through walls was by walking calmly through their gates … two-thirds of the cities that he [Zheng Chenggong] took were opened to him from within by surrenderers or conspirers … Bombardment decided sieges only 6 percent of the time" (ch 15, "The Significance of the Renaissance Fortress").

## 3. What it CORRECTS

Nothing in the manuscript is shown wrong by Andrade on a date, figure or name. Three precision points:

1. Manuscript (Chapter 6 §IV) says the French crown "possessed, uniquely in Latin Europe at this scale, all three elements": paid specialists, a central park of guns, non-lapsing revenue. The qualifier "in Latin Europe" is essential and must not be lost at renovation, because Andrade, ch 4, "Great Martiality": by 1380 Ming policy "stipulated that gunners should comprise 10 percent of soldiers … there were more gunners in early Ming China than knights, soldiers, and pages in France, England, and Burgundy combined," and "His Bureau of Armaments (軍器局) was required to produce, every three years, three thousand bowl-mouth bronze guns, three thousand handheld bronze guns, and three thousand signal cannons." The triad existed in China seventy years before 1445.

2. Manuscript (Chapter 11 §V) says "only polities that could license, mandate and administer the new medium at national scale could exploit it," and stages print as Europe's second capital-intensive medium. Andrade, Conclusions: "Between 1550 and 1644, Chinese presses put out at least 1,127 military manuals … Historians of Europe tend to portray Europe's print revolution as unique and unprecedented, and maybe it was, but we certainly need to be aware of the rich history of East Asian printing." The chapter's claim survives (it is about the state's harness, not print's novelty) but should say China had both print and a licensing state for centuries.

3. A correction *to Andrade*, so the author does not inherit it: ch 6, "Big Guns", reads "the reforms of the French king Charles V (1422–1461)". The dates are Charles VII's; the name is a slip. Do not cite the sentence without the correction.

## 4. What it ADDS

**(a) Closes the Hussite GAP (Chapter 10 §VIII).** The draft flags "[the Hussite prehistory of the cheap hand-gun is asserted nowhere in this book — GAP]". Andrade supplies a deeper prehistory: "In 1163, a Song commander named Wei Sheng prepared several hundred 'at-your-desire-war-carts' (如意戰車), each of which contained fire lances … Historians have made much of the use of armored mobile firearm platforms among the Hussites in the early 1400s, the Muscovites in the late 1400s, and the Chinese in the mid-1500s. The armored fire lance carts of the Song period were their forerunners" (ch 2, "The Song-Jin Wars"). And the diffusion figure for the cheap layer: "By the 1430s and 1440s, it reached 20 percent. By 1466, it had risen to 30 percent. In Europe … it wasn't until the mid-1500s that gunners made up 30 percent" (ch 4, opening). The two-layer argument of §VIII — cheap projectile beneath, expensive apparatus above — gains a hemispheric control: China ran the cheap layer at three times Europe's density and built no siege-train monopoly at all.

**(b) The mechanism operating inside China (Chapter 10 §VII; Coda §I).** Andrade's own counter-evidence to his scepticism, and the best single find for the book: "Before the Ming period, the central government ruled over peripheral minority peoples by appointing local chieftains. It seems, however, that this chieftain (土司) system ended during the Ming and early Qing periods (1300s–1600s), morphing into a structure of direct rule … Historian Zhang Wen argues that guns gave the central government a decisive edge over local powers, allowing it to assume direct control over aboriginal areas that had for centuries — millennia in some cases — been governed indirectly. There is a clear parallel here with the gunpowder revolution model for Europe" (ch 8, opening; n. 12, Zhang Wen, "Huo qi"). Where China had intermediate authorities, the guns suppressed them. That is the chain's last link, attested in the one place in China where there was a dispersal to end.

**(c) The fourth counter-case for Chapter 10 §VI.** Ming China is the case the triad table lacks: capability, finance and administration all present, and no ratchet. Andrade's explanation is ready-made: "Given that even industrial age artillery had trouble overcoming traditional Chinese walls" (ch 4, "The Siege of Suzhou, 1366"); Suzhou's walls "over seven meters high, eleven meters thick at the base"; "Even Mehmed's monster would barely have dented Suzhou's walls" (ch 6). The mechanism idled not because a leg was missing but because the decisive capability was not decisive against the defence it faced — which sharpens Coda §I's "mechanism under conditions" into a statable condition: the ratchet needs a breakable defence.

**(d) The pluralising brake (Chapter 10 §VII; Chapter 12).** The spine asks why artillery produced four or five European states rather than one. Andrade endorses Parker on the fortress: "They were designed to resist artillery, and they worked. Artillery, which once breached walls regularly, was countered, and sieges became drawn-out affairs" (ch 6); "The Dutch case strongly supports the idea that the Renaissance fortress did indeed act as an 'engine of European expansion'" (ch 15). The train priced decisive force at state scale; the bastion, a generation later, priced the defence at the same scale — consolidation ran to the size of the unit that could pay for both, and stopped: "Europe was at the beginning of a long warring states period, one that would last until 1945" (ch 7, "Explanations for the First Divergence"). Material for a paragraph the manuscript does not yet have.

**(e) Colour.** The Florentine diplomat of the early 1490s: "'the French claim their artillery is capable of creating a breach in a wall of eight feet in thickness' … 'the French are braggarts by nature'" (ch 6). The Kangxi Emperor's logistics for Albazin — "detailed instructions about the sizes of transport boats, the construction of granaries, the staffing of post stations" (ch 15).

**(f) For Chapter 12 §IV and spine §5 (China).** Andrade's Conclusions offer a rival present-tense frame the chapter should meet by name: "some Chinese thinkers today suggest that their country is entering a new warring states period, this time on a global scale … the situation today is perhaps less like the original Warring States Period … than like the period I have called the Song Warring States Period." His datum on Qing fragility — "Decentralization was baked into the Qing state, the result of a 'carefully devised system of checks and balances'" (ch 18) — is the historical antecedent of the two-body question the manuscript poses about a settlement "executed as personal rule".

**(g) Datasets.** Appendix 2's warfare-frequency tabulations are a crude but reproducible way of scoring Coda §I's third condition, "exogenous pressure sufficient to force the mobilisation", which currently has no metric.

## 5. What it CONTRADICTS

At full strength, in Andrade's words.

**(i) The arrow runs the other way, and China proves it.** "Someone who knows these rich data is Peter Lorge, who suggests that the explanation is perhaps more straightforward: Europe needed to become more like China before it could fully use guns, which is to say that it first needed centralized states with permanent standing armies. Lorge is speaking delicately, but one could put it more boldly: China was in certain ways more advanced than Europe" (ch 8, opening). Then Crone: "'to the non-European historian they simply indicate that Europe had finally joined the club.' Thus, we might rather look at Europe as an aberration, a bit slow on the uptake. Perhaps guns hastened the end of Europe's decentralized system, but feudalism was probably doomed in any case." And Hale, via DeVries: centralization "'began before cannon were effective or readily transportable and can be explained without reference to gunpowder weapons.' Others have argued similarly, and China adds weight to the case." This is Curry's reversal (Chapter 10 §VII) armed with a second hemisphere, and it reaches the DEFEND-tier mechanism (spine §6) and ruling §8(a): the artillery ratchet is, on this reading, an epiphenomenon of a Eurasian trend toward "fewer but increasingly centralized political units" (Lieberman, cited ch 8 n. 10).

**(ii) The price shift was an artefact of European walls.** "In China artillery would not have repaid the heavy investments" (ch 6). If the capital-intensification of decisive force depended on the accident that "Late medieval French walls were almost always 2 meters or less in thickness" while Chinese walls were "ten and twenty meters wide at the base", then the mechanism is not "technology-specific" (CLAUDE.md §1) but defence-specific — and Part III must show that the present defence is thin.

**(iii) Parity without the fiscal-military state.** "The forces of China were effective not just because they'd achieved technological parity, but also because of statecraft, which made possible large standing armies, intensive drilling practices, and sophisticated logistics" (Conclusions) — a polity that fielded standing armies, central arsenals and world-class logistics for three centuries while, by Andrade's own later account, lacking "a fiscal state". The chain's second link — capability compels permanent finance — did not run in the largest gunpowder polity on earth.

**(iv) Warfare frequency, not weapon price, is the variable.** "dynamism followed the pulse of warfare" (Conclusions); the 1450–1550 divergence is explained by "the frequency of warfare", and the whole book is built to show that "levels of geopolitical instability … help explain military aspects of the rise of the West" (Introduction). The manuscript's motor is a threshold price; Andrade's is a rate of challenge.

**The book's best answer.** To (i) and (iii): the manuscript already concedes the fisc predates the guns (spine §8(a); Chapter 10 §III, "the gradualism is the finding") and claims only the ratchet — "what the guns of 1449–53 made irreversible was … everyone else's ability to reverse it". Andrade's China is not a counter-example to a ratchet; it is a polity with nothing left to ratchet: the intermediate authorities had been suppressed a millennium earlier, and where they survived — the *tusi* peripheries — his own cited evidence (Zhang Wen) shows the guns doing exactly what the manuscript says guns do. Lorge's "Europe needed to become more like China" is therefore true and harmless: the book is about how a dispersed order was ended, and China had ended its own by other means at another price. That answer is good, and Andrade hands over its evidence. To (ii): adopt the objection rather than resist it — state the condition (the ratchet requires a decisive capability the existing defence cannot cheaply absorb), then discharge it for the present in Chapter 12 from the Chapter 2–3 evidence: the platforms' walls are being breached by licence, export control and revocable authorisation at a price only blocs can pay. Adequate, but it converts a claim into a scoreable condition, and Appendix C should carry it. To (iv): Coda §I already names "exogenous pressure sufficient" as a condition; Andrade's variable is a condition of the mechanism, not a rival to it, and the draft should say so. On the spine's "why four or five, not one": Andrade does not answer; the fortress brake (§4(d)) is Parker's, endorsed by Andrade, and the manuscript will be resting on Parker for the brake and on Andrade only for confirmation that it worked.

## 7. Verdict

Andrade changes the manuscript's weight and its register, not its direction: the mechanism survives, but only in the conditional form the Coda already states, and Chapter 10 must now carry China as the counter-case that had every leg of the triad and no ratchet. The single most important thing the author should know is that the triad is not the mechanism — the ratchet is — and the ratchet needs two things China lacked in 1445 and Europe had: a dispersal to end and a wall the guns could break; Andrade's own cited evidence (the *tusi* peripheries) shows the guns consolidating exactly where China still had the first. The price he exacts is that Part III must show, and Appendix C must score, that the present defence is thin.
