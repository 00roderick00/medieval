# Assessment — Cemal Kafadar, *Between Two Worlds: The Construction of the Ottoman State* (University of California Press, 1995; pbk 1996)

*Read in full (main text and the relevant endnotes; bibliography and index skimmed). OCR is clean apart from "o f" spacing, "IPs" for "II's", mangled Turkish diacritics, "Baissa" for Bursa (p. 43) and letter-spaced lines on p. 145; nothing load-bearing is garbled. Pins give printed page; PDF page = printed + 20 throughout (p. 146 = PDF p. 166).*

## 1. The book in two paragraphs

Kafadar's book is a historiographical essay on how the rise of the Ottoman state has been explained since Gibbons, Köprülü and Wittek, and a selective reconstruction of that rise as a *process* of coalition-building and coalition-devouring. Against Wittek's "gaza thesis" — "the thesis that assigned a crucial role to the spirit of gazâ ('Holy War ideology' in his unfortunate translation)" which "soon became textbook orthodoxy" (p. xi–xii / PDF pp. 11–12) — and equally against its 1980s critics (Lindner, Jennings, Káldy-Nagy), he argues that both camps "subscribed to the essentialism of earlier historiography", perpetuating "a dichotomous analysis that wishes to see the early Ottoman conquerors and state builders as fundamentally Turkish, tribal, and driven by pragmatism and plunder or fundamentally Muslim and driven by zeal for holy war" (p. 12 / PDF p. 32). The gaza ethos was real, contested and elastic, and a "motive force", never a "sufficient cause" (pp. 57–58 / PDF pp. 77–78).

The construction of the state is then told as the dynasty's "centralizing logic" defeating, one by one, the partners of its own frontier coalition: gazi warlords, dervish colonisers, the ulema-origin Çandarlı vizierial dynasty. The thesis sentence: "the rise of the Ottoman state as a process rather than as a mechanical relationship between a particular cause and an outcome" (p. 120 / PDF p. 140). The instruments are institutional — unigeniture, the kul army created "during or right after the crisis of the 1370s", the pençik booty-tax, the kadıasker — and the sealing moment is 1453: "the conquest of Constantinople, when the triumph of centralized absolutism was sealed" (p. 14 / PDF p. 34). "It was the peculiar success of the House of Osman to pursue its own dream of absolute power to the exclusion or subjugation of other dreamers" (p. 152 / PDF p. 172).

## 2. What it CONFIRMS

- **Ch. 5 §IV** — manuscript: "in 1446 it ended … a Janissary rising, the Buçuktepe revolt, engineered by the grand vizier Çandarlı Halil Pasha, which returned Murad to the throne". Kafadar: the war party's 1444 victory was "a precarious victory that was reversed within two years by a Çandarlı-instigated revolt of the kul army" (p. 146 / PDF p. 166); chronology: "1446 A Janissary revolt culminates in Murâd II's return to the throne" (p. xix / PDF p. 19).
- **Ch. 5 §IV** — manuscript: the devşirme/kul answer was "already mature by mid-century". Kafadar's chronology: "1383–87 Suggested as the latest date by which point the imposition of devshirme had been initiated"; "1395? Sermon by the archbishop of Thessaloniki that includes the earliest known reference to the devshirme (which indicates that it had been practiced for some time)" (p. xix / PDF p. 19). This closes the dating half of the ch. 5 [GAP].
- **Ch. 5 §IV / Ch. 11 §IV** — the elite owing "nothing to any hereditary interest": "an institution of artificial kinship … that functioned as an extension of the royal household" (p. 17 / PDF p. 37).
- **Ch. 10** — "the Ottoman janissaries predate the ordonnance companies by two generations". Kafadar dates the corps to "that conjuncture, during or right after the crisis of the 1370s" (p. 139 / PDF p. 159): seven decades before 1445.
- **Ch. 11 §IV** — the execution of Çandarlı Halil and the fiscal move. Kafadar: "Some of the losers appeared as soon as the day after the grand conquest, when Çandarli Halïl, grandson of the scion of this vezirial dynasty, was arrested and soon executed" (p. 97 / PDF p. 117); and "toward the end of Mehmed's reign when he confiscated more than a thousand villages that were held, as freehold or endowment, by descendants of early colonizers, mostly dervishes" (p. 97). Bayezid II "was forced to appease the losers of Mehmed's confiscation drive by rerecognizing their entitlements" (p. 97). Kafadar carries confiscation flatly; the Özel caveat post-dates him.
- **Ch. 11 §IV** — the kanunname's fratricide clause as Mehmed's. Kafadar treats it as settled: "Mehmed II (r. 1451–81) finally legislating fratricide as the culmination of a centralist logic whose goal was to eliminate all tendencies toward fragmentation" (p. 137 / PDF p. 157); Ibn Kemâl "attributes to Osman the same reasoning that is advanced in Mehmed II's code to legitimize the legislation of fratricide: 'damage to an individual is preferable to damage to the public'" (p. 108 / PDF p. 128). He does not stage the Dilger–Berki dispute; he is a witness that the 1995 consensus assumed authenticity, not an argument for it.

## 3. What it CORRECTS

1. **Manuscript (Ch. 11 §IV) says Çandarlı Halil was "the last of the old aristocratic vizieral dynasty"; Kafadar p. 111 (PDF p. 131) says the Çandarlı were "of ulema background", a family that "nearly monopolized the top juridical and administrative offices in the Ottoman state from the midfourteenth to the midfifteenth century".** Kafadar's structural point is that the frontier polity "developed a general reluctance to recognize an aristocracy" (p. 141 / PDF p. 161). Write "ulema-origin vizierial dynasty". He also notes vezirs "(perhaps three) before Çandarlı Kara Halil" (p. 140).
2. **Manuscript (Ch. 11 §IV) says "Mehmed II manufactured his service class from raw material"; Kafadar pp. 139, 148 (PDF pp. 159, 168) date the manufacture to the 1370s and the political sealing of kul ascendancy to 1481–82.** Mehmed inherited corps and levy and eliminated their rivals; the sealing came when "the kapıkulu army … managed to enthrone Bäyezld. Thereafter, the ascendancy of a kul-based administration was sealed" (p. 148). Mehmed completed, not created.
3. **Manuscript (Ch. 5 §IV) reads the 1444 abdication as a weary king retiring and the crisis as "a personalised sovereignty briefly detached from a person capable of executing it"; Kafadar p. 146 (PDF p. 166) reads 1444 as a factional victory.** The uc begleri "appear as the leading members of the 'war party' that stood once again in opposition to the Çandarlı family, who led the party that stood for accommodation with the imperial government of Byzantium … they appeared to have won the upper hand with the abdication of Murâd II and the first enthronement of Mehmed II in 1444". The child sultan was a faction's candidate and the 1446 kul revolt the central administration's instrument for reversing a policy. "Weary of the throne" has no support in Kafadar; source it elsewhere (his authority is İnalcık, *Fatih Devri*, n. 58, p. 190 / PDF p. 210).
4. **Manuscript (Ch. 11 §I, §V) places the Ottoman variant "between roughly 1470 and 1530"; Kafadar p. 96 (PDF p. 116) places the decisive phase earlier: "The three decades from the conquest of Constantinople to the victories of Kilia-Akkirman in 1484 witnessed the most intensive phase in the development of the Ottoman political technology that we now call the classical Ottoman system."** Subordination of great subjects runs 1370s–1453; codification to the mid-sixteenth century (p. 153). Flag the Ottoman case as running on its own clock.
5. Not carried by Kafadar, so left on their present footing: the 1 June date, "first grand vizier to be executed", Zaganos Pasha, the Janissary strength figure and the one-in-forty ratio. The ch. 11 per-levy [GAP] is not closed here.

## 4. What it ADDS

- **The fiscal origin of the standing army (Ch. 10 §on permanence; Ch. 11 §IV).** The manuscript never says how the Janissaries were paid for. Kafadar: "one-fifth (hums) of the slaves captured in the raids, like other kinds of booty, ought to be taken by the state treasury … That, of course, is the beginning of a new army under the direct control of the House of Osman, yeni çeri (Janissary), that was evidently staffed at first through this tax called pençik (one-fifth). The tax is obviously exacted from the gazis" (pp. 112–13 / PDF pp. 132–33). A permanent household force built by a fiscal claim on the intermediate power it was made to subordinate — the manuscript's chain in a fourteenth-century key, with the disanalogy that the base is booty, not the peasantry.
- **The frontier warriors' own understanding of what consolidation meant (Ch. 5 §IV or Ch. 11 §IV, as the chapter's one primary-source line).** 1457, the Belgrade assault: "'If Belgrade is conquered,' they said, according to Apz, 'we will have to plow the land.' These gazis were obviously aware of where the central state's policy was leading … they were increasingly reduced to provincial fief-holders, that is, agriculturalists" (p. 147 / PDF p. 167). And Nihâlî on Mihaloğlu Ali Beg, forbidden to raid after Süleyman's Habsburg treaty: "like giving me the judgeship of Galata and telling me not to drink" (p. 150 / PDF p. 170).
- **The chroniclers' resentment of fiscal institutionalisation (Ch. 11 §IV; Ch. 7's Praguerie-type material).** The anonymous chronicle: "the rulers … did not know what a treasury was. But when [Çandarlı] Hayreddin Pasha came to the Gate [of government] greedy scholars became the companions of the rulers … 'He who is a ruler must have a treasury,' they said … Until then nothing was known of keeping account books" (p. 111 / PDF p. 131). The losers' account of the administrative state — the Ottoman twin of ch. 6 §VI's point that the defeated misread the fiscal apparatus.
- **Both factions decapitated (Ch. 11 §IV).** Kafadar: Mehmed "eliminated the leaders of both factions as well as the significance of both types of forces in Ottoman politics" (pp. 18–19 / PDF pp. 38–39); "Two prominent frontier warlords, leaders of the anti-Çandarlı party, were also executed in a couple of years" (p. 97).
- **Unigeniture as the pre-artillery anti-fragmentation device (Ch. 5 §IV; Ch. 9 on the two bodies).** "the Ottomans came up with unigeniture again and again, even though it was systematized only with Mehmed II's codification of fratricide" (p. 136 / PDF p. 156); Mehmed I to Shahrukh: the Ottoman sultans "refused to accept partnership in government" (p. 137). The anonymous chronicles' editorial "brothers consulted each other then; they did not kill one another" (p. 106 / PDF p. 126) is T1 evidence that fratricide was contested at codification — for the [^11-6] note.
- **The devşirme disanalogy, re-grounded (Ch. 11 §IV; Ch. 12 §on legibility).** The manuscript grounds it in "imperial-household logic"; Kafadar in frontier fluidity and fitra: "A system like the devshirme … could be conceivable only in a state born of those frontier conditions" (p. 141 / PDF p. 161); "Only thus could the kul system flourish … once shown the true path of Islam, which accords with the innermost nature, the fitra, of every human being, they would follow it" (pp. 83–84 / PDF pp. 103–04). The levy breached the dhimma yet "does not seem to have invited more than minimal debate" (p. 153). Both make "no Latin monarchy could have adopted it" more specific.
- **The Ottoman fourth move — canonical assertion — which Ch. 11 §IV currently lacks.** The Seyyid Gazi shrine dervishes "unacceptable to the Ottoman state, which now was conscious of its role as the defender of Sunni orthodoxy" (p. 92 / PDF p. 112); Ebüssuûd calling Sarı Saltuk "a Christian ascetic" (p. 154 / PDF p. 174); the ahi brotherhoods "turned into guilds much more strictly controlled by the government" (p. 141) — with the limit: "the insufficiency of the technologies of control available to a pre-modern state" (p. 154).
- **A fifth-move equivalent (Ch. 11 §V).** Bayezid II in 1484 "ordered the recording of what thus far had been mostly oral traditions about the founding fathers" (p. 97). The record, not the press — one sentence.
- **Renaissance comparative politics did admit the Ottomans (Ch. 11 §I).** Knolles: "the way laid open for every common person, be he never so meanly borne, to aspire unto the greatest honours" (p. 31 / PDF p. 51). Green excluded them; Bodin and Knolles did not — and Knolles is an Elizabethan T1 gloss on the kul meritocracy.
- **Appendix B row.** Kafadar supplies the Ottoman row: intermediate authorities (uc begleri, dervish freeholders, Çandarlı, ahi corporations); instruments (kul army 1370s; pençik; kadıasker and uc begleri offices under Murad I, pp. 142–43; fratricide code; confiscations; the Istanbul capital); sealing dates 1453 and 1481–82; the Edirne-versus-Istanbul axis as the losers' politics (pp. 148–49).
- **Method (Coda).** The "motive force"/"sufficient cause" distinction (pp. 57–58) and the warning against "confusing an epistemological itinerary with an ontological one" (p. 58) support spine §8(a)'s ratchet-not-motor ruling.

## 5. What it CONTRADICTS

**At full strength.** Kafadar's Ottoman state is built without artillery in the causal chain. Its decisive instruments — unigeniture, the kul army of the 1370s, the pençik, the reduction of gazi begs "first to unequal partnership, then to vassalage, and then to incorporation as removable appointees" (p. 17 / PDF p. 37) — are in place before any gun mattered; the 1446 counter-revolution is made by household infantry, not cannon finance; and 1453 seals centralism because the sultan "rode on the tremendous prestige of that feat" (p. 18 / PDF p. 38), not because walls fell. Kafadar's own programme is explicitly anti-mechanistic: the Ottoman polity is "the historically contingent product of a culturally complex, socially differentiated, and politically competitive environment rather than as the necessary result of a unitary line of developmental logic" (p. 14 / PDF p. 34); "The question of the 'cause(s) of Ottoman success' cannot be expected to yield a unitary answer for the whole period one needs to consider" (p. 120 / PDF p. 140). And he brackets the very variable the manuscript rests on: "one also would like to know much more about Ottoman military strategies and use of technology since these undoubtedly played an important role in our story. Detailed studies are needed, from techniques of cannon casting to allocation of firearms" (p. 145 / PDF p. 165). Against this, Ch. 11 §V's closing sentence — "each move spends the fiscal-military surplus the artillery state generated, and none was executable without it" — is, for the Ottoman member of the four, refuted: the surplus that built the kul army was booty, and the moves were executed. This reaches the DEFEND-tier mechanism (spine §6) wherever the Ottoman case is presented as a variant of the mechanism rather than a comparator on a different sequence; it touches §8(a) too, since Kafadar attributes the 1453 sealing to prestige, not to wall-breaking proof.

Lesser: his periodisation runs the Ottoman "new monarchy" a century early and the codification a generation late, so the 1470–1530 synchrony is, for one of the four, a convenience.

**The book's best answer.** The manuscript already refuses to make the Ottomans a mechanism case: ch. 11 §IV places them "not as an option the West declined but as the demonstration of the pattern's outer bound", and ch. 5 §IV uses them as the crisis-decade comparator. The honest restatement: the Ottoman state supplies the *requirement*, not the *causation* — a polity that had built the fiscal-administrative household by pre-artillery means was thereby the polity able to cast and feed the guns of 1453, and 1453 removed everyone else's ability to reverse the settlement (Kafadar's "sealed"; the Belgrade gazis; the executions of both factions inside three years). That is §8(a)'s ratchet in a different sequence: intention fixed by the 1370s and 1413, reversibility removed 1453–84. The disanalogy must be stated: the Ottoman base for permanent force was booty and slaves, the instrument a manufactured household. Is the answer good? Adequate, on condition that ch. 11 §V's closing sentence is rewritten to exempt the Ottoman case, and that the variant is labelled as one where the administrative state preceded the artillery and the artillery locked it. Kept as written, the sentence is refuted.

## 6. Placement

- **Ch. 5 §IV — T2 canonical scholarship, evidence and (on the 1444 accession) rival to the draft's reading.** Engage BY NAME for the war-party/peace-party framing (p. 146); footnote the 1446 revolt and devşirme dating (pp. xix, 139). The "Ibn Khaldunian predicament" (pp. 16–17) and the 1370s crisis (p. 139) can replace the second-hand İnalcık formulation and close that [GAP] at T2; İnalcık's *Fatih Devri* (n. 58) stays on the retrieval list.
- **Ch. 10 — footnote** on the Janissaries' date and the pençik origin (pp. 112–13, 139), as the calibrating caveat to "first permanent, regularly paid royal army in Latin Christendom".
- **Ch. 11 §IV — T2 ally on the institutional description; rival on the mechanism. Engage BY NAME** for the "ulema background" correction, the both-factions point, the confiscation figure, the fitra/frontier grounding of the disanalogy, and the sixteenth-century Sunni canonical move (pp. 92, 153–54). The gaza-thesis critique (chs 1–2) is not this book's quarrel; it enters as a one-sentence footnote: the Ottoman literature has its own Wittek-versus-critics debate, shaped like Elton/Rogers, and Kafadar's resolution (contested ethos; motive force, not sufficient cause) is adopted.
- **Ch. 11 §V and closing — engage BY NAME** in the sentence that exempts the Ottoman case from "none was executable without it".
- **Ch. 12 (legibility) — footnote** on the devşirme as a dhimma-breaching instrument (p. 153).
- **Coda §II and Appendix B — evidence.** The Appendix B Ottoman row should be built from Kafadar's chronology and epilogue (pp. xvii–xix, 151–54).
- **Not for** chs 1–4, 6–9, Appendix C.

## 7. Verdict

Kafadar changes the Ottoman comparator's register, not the book's direction: the standard account of how the Ottomans consolidated is institutional, contingent and pre-artillery, so the Ottoman case must be carried as the demonstration of the requirement and the ratchet, never as a fourth instance of the causation. Its weight is considerable — it corrects "aristocratic" to "ulema-origin", reframes 1444 as a faction's coup rather than a vacancy, dates the devşirme and the kul army, and hands the book its best Ottoman primary line ("we will have to plow the land"). The single thing the author must know: ch. 11 §V's "none was executable without it" is false for the Ottomans on Kafadar's account, and the Ottoman disanalogy is stronger, and differently grounded, than the draft states.
