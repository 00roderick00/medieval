# Assessment — Walter Scheidel, *Escape from Rome: The Failure of Empire and the Road to Prosperity* (Princeton, 2019)

*Read: Introduction (pp. 1–27), Chapter 1 (pp. 31–48), Chapters 5–6 in full (pp. 127–215, the eight junctures), Chapter 7 pp. 232–240 (fiscal section; Tilly), Chapter 10 in full (pp. 337–419: institutions, war, taxes, Hoffman, China), Chapter 12 pp. 479–496, Epilogue pp. 503–516. Not read: Chapters 2–4, 8–9, 11, the rest of 7 and 12, Epilogue pp. 517–528, apparatus. Text clean apart from soft-hyphen residue and scrambled figure axes (captions and Table 1.1 intact). Pins: printed page, PDF in brackets; PDF = printed + 21. Caution: Scheidel's German-empire pages carry slips not to be imported — "Henry V" for Henry VI, "Conrad II" for Conrad IV, "dukes of Aquitaine and Swabia" for the rival kings of 1198 (all p. 166 [187]), "The Great Schism of the thirteenth century" (p. 348 [369]). T2 for structure and measurement, not a name-check source. Role: RIVAL FRAME at the macro scale, ALLY at the domestic scale.*

## 1. The book in two paragraphs

Scheidel asks why Europe, alone among the Old World's macro-regions, never rebuilt hegemonic empire after Rome, and why that mattered. "I argue that a single condition was essential in making the initial breakthroughs possible: competitive fragmentation of power" (p. 9 [PDF 30]); "Yet in contrast to China, nothing like the Roman empire ever returned to Europe" (p. 9); the axiom: "without polycentrism, no modernity" (p. 15 [36]). Chapter 1 measures the anomaly by population: Rome held four-fifths of Europeans, and thereafter "a 20 percent ceiling became the norm in post-Roman Europe" (p. 45 [66]) — in nineteen of twenty-six fifty-year intervals since 650 the largest European polity fell short of a fifth, against nought in East Asia. Chapters 5–6 run eight counterfactual junctures — Justinian, the Umayyads, Charlemagne, the German emperors, the Mongols, the Habsburgs, the Ottomans, Louis XIV to Napoleon — under a "minimal-rewrite rule" (p. 24 [45]) and find each blocked: "I conclude that post-Roman polycentrism in Europe was a perennially robust phenomenon" (p. 12 [33]).

The mechanism runs through revenue. Germanic land grants "removed the single most important function of taxation: ensuring control over organized violence" (p. 236 [257]); fiscal atrophy, aristocratic autonomy, castles and a monarchical papacy produced a "multiple acephalous federation" (Mann, quoted p. 360 [381]) that no one could scale up. Part V argues that every explanation of the Great Divergence "critically depend[s] on the absence of Roman-scale empire" (p. 14 [35]). China is the foil: the "capstone" state, and Mokyr's line that under monopoly "the government can flip the switch off" (p. 479 [500]).

## 2. What it CONFIRMS

**Ch. 1 §II — authority "nowhere exclusive", and durable.** "treating medieval France or Germany as single imperial entities is generous at the best of times. A more hard-nosed assessment of political realities would push the population share of dominant polities far below the 20 percent mark" (p. 43 [64]); "Different legal traditions existed side by side: canon, urban, feudal, and manorial law… Power became constitutionalized and thereby subject to negotiation" (p. 361 [382]). The manuscript's "no internal tendency… toward the exclusive territorial state" is his "perennially robust".

**Ch. 10 §§I–III and Ch. 11 §V — the arc, in one sentence each.** "In the Middle Ages, the dispersion of power within polities constrained the intensity of interstate competition by depriving rulers of the means to engage in sustained conflict. In the early modern period, these conditions were reversed" (p. 371 [392]). The five moves compressed: "rulers for the most part managed to regain control over the concentrated means of violence, co-opt economic powerholders, and incorporate the church(es) into emergent national structures" (p. 340 [361]). "Overcoming prolonged fiscal weakness, the reconstitution of centralized governmental power created fiscal-military states. Military demand for funding was systemic" (pp. 368–69 [389–90]).

**Ch. 10 §VII — the fifteenth century as the hinge.** The tournament's conditions were "arguably unique to Western Europe from the fifteenth century onward", and "the painful transition from weak and fragmented medieval polities to more centralized and increasingly capable early modern states was in large part driven by warfare" (p. 368 [389]).

**Ch. 10 §VI — the fisc as the missing leg.** Charles V: "In the absence of an effective fiscal bureaucracy, warfare on this scale was simply unsustainable" (p. 199 [220]). Metz "cost him more than 3 million ducats" (p. 198 [219]); loans rose from "46 percent" of war funds in 1529–41 to "72 percent" in 1543–52; armies "from 15,000 men… in 1529 to 80,000 by 1552" (p. 199 [220]).

**Ch. 1 §IV and Ch. 12 §II — Cerny's absent pressure.** "the Hundred Years' War helped restore royal wealth and power in France" (p. 357 [378]); and the negative case: between 955 and 1241 "the German empire did not face any serious threats that might have precipitated centralization" (p. 168 [189]).

**Coda §I — the Empire's failure mode.** "Sporadic attempts under the Salians to impose direct taxes remained unsuccessful" (p. 170 [191]); and the Tilly-inversion: "there were no big wars that made stronger states able to make more war, only small wars that helped a weak state stay alive" (p. 172 [193]).

**Ch. 12 §IV and Coda §V — consolidation inside units, plurality between them.** "as the states of early modern Europe gradually improved their capabilities in these categories, they did so in concert with their peers. As a result, no one state enjoyed the unique advantages of ancient Rome" (p. 214 [235]) — why the settlement stops at the bloc boundary, from the historian of why it stopped at the kingdom.

## 3. What it CORRECTS

1. **Manuscript (Chapter 1 §II) says** "after Frederick II's death in 1250, the Great Interregnum — a quarter-century"; **Scheidel p. 166 [187] says** the interregnum began with Conrad's death: "The latter's death in 1254 led to a prolonged interregnum without any emperor at all", ending "When the Habsburg Rudolf I of Austria claimed the imperial mantle in 1273". The vacancy is 1254–73, nineteen years; if the sentence is to count from 1250 it must say the dynasty's collapse, not the interregnum. (Scheidel's "Conrad II" is Conrad IV.)

2. **Manuscript (Chapter 11 §V) says** "the Habsburg composite followed its own path a generation later"; **Scheidel pp. 195–99 [216–20] says** the composite executed no settlement: it resembled "a multinational corporation more than a state" (Pagden, p. 195), "lacked both a capital and a unified administration" (p. 199), and "Upon Charles's death in 1558, the Habsburg domains were more fragmented than they had been before" (p. 198). Castile executed the moves at kingdom scale; the composite failed them at composite scale — the manuscript's own EU distinction, and it should be said.

3. **Manuscript (Coda §VI) says** the first medieval order "ended within two generations of that price becoming payable"; **Scheidel pp. 192–93 [213–14] and 370 [391] say** the rebuilding took four centuries — "state power was slowly but surely rebuilt: rulers bargained more successfully for tax revenue, and armies grew in size", and "In the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, European states became less diverse as state power grew at the expense of local elites". Under spine §8(a) the verb is *became irreversible* within two generations, not *ended*.

4. **Manuscript (Chapter 10 §II) says** the compagnies were "the first standing force in Christendom's memory" bar Charles V's and the janissaries; **Scheidel p. 239 [260] says** Alfred "and his successors… establish[ed] a permanent army. Sustained by men who rotated between their homes and the field army". The calibrated claim ("permanent, *regularly paid*") survives; the qualifier must now visibly do the work against Wessex.

Nothing else in the chapters read is contradicted on fact.

## 4. What it ADDS

**Ch. 1 §II — a pinned count for c. 1200, closing the chapter's own caution.** The draft withholds the "ninety ecclesiastical territories" because they describe 1450. Scheidel supplies the Staufer-era figure: "No fewer than seventeen secular princes and forty-seven bishops or archbishops with de facto ducal powers emerged in the process" (p. 169 [190]); and the continental count, "from about three dozen at the end of late antiquity to more than a hundred by 1300" (p. 48 [69]). Table 1.1 (p. 45 [66]): post-Roman Europe 20 per cent, East Asia 77.

**Ch. 6 and Ch. 10 §VII — the castle as the physical substrate of the dispersal.** "During the High Middle Ages, Western Europe filled up with castles… progress in defensive capabilities coincided with stagnation in offensive capacities" (p. 169 [190]); and the sentence the siege chapters should quote and reverse, from the Mongol pages: "There was no central government to offer surrender: these positions would have had to be reduced one by one" (p. 187 [208]). What the Bureau train changed in 1449–50 is exactly this — reduction one by one became feasible, so garrisons surrendered before it. Rogers's surrender-not-siege caveat and Scheidel's Mongol condition are one fact seen from either side of the gun.

**Ch. 10 §§III and VII — the Reich as the threshold demonstration.** "small wars that helped a weak state stay alive" (p. 172 [193]) names, from outside the artillery debate, the threshold the spine's differentiation from Tilly requires: war did not make the state until war had a price only the state could pay. Scheidel does not say what crossed the threshold; his account of 1250–1500 is the one sentence quoted at correction 3. That is the manuscript's opening, and the book should say so by name.

**Ch. 12 §IV (EU) — the failure mode is the Reich, not only Burgundy.** "the real question is not why the empire did not expand but why it did not collapse… Given a shared elite preference for weak rulers, there was always enough political will to preserve the empire in a format that did not encroach too much on elite interests" (p. 172 [193]). And the composite's fiscal geometry under Charles V: "only a minute fraction of the costs of that war were borne by the empire's principalities" (p. 195 [216]). Depreter names apparatus without a centre; Scheidel names a framework its members keep because it is weak. The EU section should carry both.

**Ch. 12 §II — primary material for "compelled, not accomplished".** The Great Elector: "The military preparations of all our neighbors compel us to follow their example" (p. 369 [390]; his n. 64). With the multiples: 1500 to the 1780s tax yield per capita rose "to almost five times the previous level in Spain, to fifteen times in France, and to thirty times in England in silver terms… some 70–90 percent of state budgets was committed to the military" (p. 369).

**Ch. 10 §VI — the timing mechanism for England versus France.** "In polities where [sustained interstate competition] commenced on a significant scale before the fifteenth century, rulers were routinely compelled to make concessions to key constituents… Elsewhere, delays… allowed rulers… to set up administrative apparatuses" (p. 366 [387]) — the Downing–Ertman argument at T2, and why 1439–46 could dispense with the Estates where England could not.

**Ch. 11 — the Ottoman fisc.** "By 1600, Ottoman tax revenue in per capita terms lagged behind that of Spain, Venice, the Netherlands, France, and England… From the sixteenth through the eighteenth centuries, Ottoman tax income measured in silver remained flat" (p. 206 [227]). The "most radical case" was also the case that stopped compounding.

**Ch. 12 §IV (China) — the capstone warning.** The hegemonic ideal type: "maintenance orientation", "delegational rule", "conservatism", "Malthusian caps" (Figure 10.1, p. 339 [360]); "public revenue declined from the Song to the Qing periods just as it rose in Europe" (p. 395 [416]). See §5(iv).

**Coda §I** — the minimal-rewrite question, "how much would have had to go differently at a certain point to bring about change on a large scale" (p. 25 [46]), is the historian's falsifiability clause; a footnote.

## 5. What it CONTRADICTS

**(i) The state as "episode".** The manuscript (Ch. 1 §VI; Coda §VI) calls the sovereign territorial state "an episode" interrupting Europe's plural norm. Scheidel: "European polycentrism hardened over time" (p. 214 [235]); "State consolidation strengthened the polycentric state system of Latin Europe: the remaining parties were fewer in number but more capable and resilient than before" (p. 193 [214]). For him the state system is not a break in polycentrism but its mature form; dispersion within polities and fragmentation between them moved in opposite directions (p. 371 [392]).

*The book's best answer:* the unit of analysis. The manuscript's plural order is overlapping authority *within* a territory; Scheidel's is the count of polities *across* it. He concedes the first was reversed (p. 340) and the manuscript predicts the second persists. Good — the two books describe one shape — but only if Ch. 1 §VI and Coda §VI stop saying the plural order "ended" and say the *overlapping* order was consolidated into units while *inter-unit* plurality hardened. As drafted, Table 1.1 refutes the sentence.

**(ii) Causal priority of technology.** For Scheidel weapons are an output of competition, not its driver: "by channeling ever more resources into warfare, this competition sustained innovation in weapons technology and public organization" (pp. 367–68 [388–89]); and among the forces that stopped Charles V he lists "the soaring cost of war, driven by mercenaries, gunpowder, and intricate fortifications" (p. 200 [221]) — at his scale the gunpowder price rise is a brake on empire, not a ratchet for the state.

*The book's best answer:* scale, honestly stated. The ratchet consolidates inside the unit that can pay; the price floor Scheidel observes is the same mechanism between units, fixing how many can pay. The manuscript must concede it cannot claim the gun made the state *system*; Scheidel's conditions explain why there was a tournament to enter, and the manuscript supplies the irreversibility point he leaves blank. The concession costs nothing at DEFEND tier.

**(iii) The European bloc.** The sharpest cut; it reaches spine §5 and the Depreter limb of the wager. "polycentrism as the defining characteristic of Europe's political order was never under any serious threat" (p. 204 [225]). "By the early nineteenth century, the European state system—forged of centuries of inconclusive warfare and self-strengthening reforms—had become too deeply entrenched, too large (thanks to Russia), and too capable of drawing on external resources (thanks to Britain) for any one party in Latin Europe to overpower and control all the others" (p. 210 [231]). Even Goldstone's rewrite yields "hegemonic primacy among allied states", not a polity (p. 209 [230]). And the aside aimed at this manuscript's reader: "French rulers who assembled a proto-European Union 'plus' (with Russia and without Brexit)" belong "In a counterfactual universe far, far away from an even moderately defensible rewrite of history" (p. 215 [236]).

*The book's best answer:* all eight junctures are empire *by conquest or dynasty against a balancing system*; a treaty polity assembled by its own members under external pressure has no entry in his frame, and his own tournament requires one to appear if the price of decisive force has moved beyond the members' reach. Only partly good. His Reich pages predict exactly what a consent-based European framework does: it persists, weak, because its constituents prefer it so — the pessimistic branch the manuscript already draws. The honest register is that a consolidated European bloc would be the first exception to the most robust pattern in a book of fifteen centuries, and Ch. 12 §IV should say so.

**(iv) The valence of consolidation, and China.** Part V argues that monopoly stifles innovation: under hegemonic empire "the government can flip the switch off" (Mokyr, p. 479 [500]); "the absence of interstate competition removed incentives for monopolistic patrons to encourage or adopt innovation" (p. 481 [502]); China's asymmetric wars "retarded Chinese firearms technology" (p. 400 [421]). The manuscript's off-switch is the state's instrument of consolidation; Scheidel's is why consolidated empires lose the tournament. "Closest to Tudor norms" (HOLD ON BALANCE, spine §6) reads in his vocabulary as "closest to the capstone".

*The book's best answer:* the settlement is compelled, not blessed (Coda §V), and runs inside blocs that remain in tournament — four polities are his polycentrism scaled up, not his monopoly. Good as far as it goes. But he adds a condition the manuscript does not carry: a bloc whose absorption of the stack becomes maintenance-oriented forfeits the innovation the stack runs on, and loses to the bloc that keeps Mokyr's "disconnect between the size of the political unit and the intellectual community" (p. 516 [537]). This does not touch the DEFEND-tier mechanism, but it belongs in Appendix C as a scoreable sub-wager: consolidation that closes the switch on its own research base is self-defeating within the horizon.

## 7. Verdict

Scheidel does not change the book's direction — his fifteen centuries of "consolidation within, plurality between" are the manuscript's predicted shape run once already at kingdom scale — but he changes its register at three joints: the "episode" sentence, the "ended within two generations" tempo, and the European conditional, which after him must be stated as the first exception to his pattern rather than an open coin. His weight is considerable because he arrives at the same map from the opposite premise, and because his account of 1250–1500 is a single sentence into which the artillery state fits exactly. The single most important thing: Scheidel supplies the frame the bloc prediction has lacked — units improve "in concert with their peers", so the price of decisive force fixes the number of survivors and not the survivor — while his Reich pages hand the EU section its true failure mode, a framework its members keep weak on purpose.
