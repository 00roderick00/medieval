## PART A — Trevor Dean, *Crime and Justice in Late Medieval Italy* (Cambridge, 2007)

## 1. The book in two paragraphs

Dean's book is a source-critical synthesis of criminal justice across the Italian peninsula from the late thirteenth century to 1500, written to fill a gap he names on the first pages: "for Italy there is no such survey: this is one effect of the enduring localism of Italian historiography. This book attempts to fill that gap" (PDF 14 / p. 2). Its method is to take five classes of document in turn — trial records, chronicles, fiction, statute law, consilia — before turning to the crimes themselves, and its deliberate decentring is from "the historiographical duopoly exercised by Florence and Venice" (PDF 18 / p. 6) towards Bologna, Lucca, Mantua, Savona and the South. The stated aim is modest in one register and pointed in another: "not to argue for a new categorisation of crimes, or to attempt to offer new explanations of crime, but to write about crime in a new way, focusing on attitudes, representations and constructions" (PDF 22 / p. 10).

The findings that matter to this project are two. First, against the "processual" school (Vallerani, Zorzi, Sbriccoli) which holds that trials were mere phases in out-of-court conflicts and that "The law, in any case, did not prohibit or prosecute vengeance, but recognised its positive value in limiting violence" (Dean's summary of that view, PDF 32 / p. 20), Dean argues that "jurists did not legitimate feud or vendetta" (PDF 141 / p. 129) and that "it is not accurate to say that the law did not prohibit or prosecute vengeance" (PDF 142 / p. 130). Second, that there was no uniform Italian trajectory: "the character of justice – inquisitorial, accusatorial, negotiated, repressive – varies both between cities and across time" (PDF 212 / p. 200), with the one directional finding that "justice in the principality looks more repressive" (PDF 63 / p. 51).

## 2. What it CONFIRMS

**(a) Ch. 8 §IV — the vendetta was "law-entangled and restrained beside its literary image".** Dean carries this at T2 and with more bite than the draft gives it. Statutes penalised "secondary vendetta" against the aggressor's kin "with death if it involved killing, or with double, treble or quadruple penalties" and in Camerino and Spoleto even "primary vendetta against the 'principal aggressor' was to be punished with the ordinary penalty" (PDF 141 / p. 129); Palermo's custom ran "normally no one may avenge themselves on their own authority, but should prosecute their case in the ordinary courts" (PDF 142 / p. 130). Trial records show prosecution "in four different situations" — the threat of revenge, outsiders recruited to a revenge group, escalation, and false accusation as revenge (PDF 142–143 / pp. 130–31).

**(b) Ch. 8 §IV — enmity "managed — priced, timed, settled".** Dean confirms the management, but dates it: thirteenth-century criminal law was "that of composition" and "public execution was thus conceived of as revenge... 'public revenge', in Gandino's phrase"; then "from early in the fourteenth century, legal vocabulary changes: now the offender is 'to be punished', or 'condemned'; execution is now 'capital punishment'; and concord is declared to be of no avail ('pax non prosit')" (PDF 137 / p. 125). The Bolognese government in 1492–93 refused to cancel homicide trials during peace negotiations lest subjects, seeing that "justice does not take its course... set to taking revenge (vendecta)... in place of that which belongs to the Podestà" (PDF 137 / p. 125). This is the clean primary-source statement of the state claiming punishment as its own.

**(c) Ch. 8 §I–II — plurality as operative law, with rules and professionals.** Dean's Italy is the same keyring of jurisdictions the Paston file shows for England: accusation, denunciation, ex officio inquisition, episcopal "inhibition" of cases against clerics, governors' clemency bought with contributions to "a new fountain, or the governor's new palace" (PDF 55 / p. 43), and jurists who "could only reconcile statute law and regional law by using the concept of fiction" when a lord's dominion spanned several cities' bans (PDF 117 / p. 105).

**(d) Ch. 8 §VII and Coda §II — the direction of travel is towards repressive, official, princely justice.** "The repressive forces of the state grew in number and changed in quality: increased numbers of police officers, the militarisation of policing, and greater staging of execution as a spectacle" (PDF 23 / p. 11); the crimes whose treatment hardened "share a greater sense of the need for and value of public, official intervention, to contain, cleanse and protect" (PDF 213 / p. 201). The Forlì chronicler's line of 1455 could stand as an epigraph for Part II: "nowadays, lords are no longer feared unless they have severe, pitiless justice" (PDF 62 / p. 50).

## 3. What it CORRECTS

**(i) Manuscript (Ch. 8 §IV) says "Dean's reading of Bolognese and Florentine cases finds the practice law-entangled and restrained".** Dean's cases are Bolognese and Lucchese (with Perugia, Modena, Reggio, Mantua, Savona); Florence is the case he argues *against*. His revenge section opens by setting "significant differences in law and practice across Italy" against "this view from Florence" and "the Florentine assumption of kin ownership of revenge" (PDF 136 / p. 124). Change "Bolognese and Florentine" to "Bolognese and Lucchese, set against the Florentine picture".

**(ii) Manuscript (Ch. 8 §IV) presents Zorzi as concordant with Dean ("Zorzi treats it as a practice regulated within communal legal culture — timing, targets and settlement all rule-bound").** Dean presents Zorzi as the position to be revised: Zorzi "has argued for the existence of a 'culture of vengeance' in Italy" with "a positive value attributed to vengeance by almost all the great thinkers" (PDF 136 / p. 124), and Dean objects both to Zorzi's word *faida* — "rarely, if ever, used by medieval writers" — and to his reading of the Parma statutes, which "Zorzi misreads... as a legal restriction of vendetta" (PDF 137 n. 62 / p. 125). The draft may keep both names, but as a disagreement, not a chorus, and the "timing, targets and settlement rule-bound" gloss is closer to Dean's finding than to Zorzi's.

**(iii) Manuscript (Ch. 8 §IV) treats the Italian vocabulary as "vendetta" and, by implication in §III, feud.** Dean: "there is no word corresponding to 'feud' or 'blood-feud', and... medieval writers instead use the terms 'enmity', 'hatred' or 'war' (inimicitia, odium, guerra)" (PDF 136 / p. 124). "Vendetta" survives as the actors' word for the act; "feud" as a structure should be confined to the German *Fehde*, which the draft already treats separately.

Nothing in the draft's dates or figures for Italy is contradicted by Dean, because the draft carries few; the 526 Florentine peace instruments (Jansen) and the Udine material (Muir) lie outside Dean's range and are not touched.

## 4. What it ADDS

**(a) The sequencing evidence Ch. 8 §VII lacks for Italy — and it is dated to the hinge decade.** Mantua: "local denunciations disappear in 1446, the Capitano del Divieto appears in 1447... This seems to represent a shift in the administration of justice, from reliance on unpaid local officials (men of the district) to hired ducal officials (men recruited abroad)" (PDF 61 / p. 49); by 1462 "eighteen men were condemned to death for an armed assembly that prevented him from performing his office" and executions rose — "greater harshness and severity were marks of princely rule in Mantua after the middle of the fifteenth century" (PDF 63 / p. 51). Bologna 1450: "the Podestà's discretionary powers were enlarged, enforcement was militarised with the appointment of a Bargello" (PDF 52 / p. 40). Naples under Alfonso "from the 1430s": "the introduction of Spanish-style police officials, who could not just arrest criminals, but try and execute them as well" (PDF 24 / p. 12). These are salaried, foreign, executive officers replacing unpaid local ones in the 1440s–60s — the administrative-capacity step of the mechanism, at city scale, on Dean's own archival evidence. Go to Ch. 8 §VII's dated sequence (Italian sub-entry) and Ch. 11's Italian comparator; and to Appendix B row 8.

**(b) "The militarisation of political power seems to have been accompanied by a disarming of the citizenry" (PDF 182 / p. 170).** In Bologna 1351 and Reggio 1373–74 under Visconti rule, citizens "threw punches, used sticks or stones", while "It was the soldiers, Germans and Lombards mostly, who struck with swords and knives" (PDF 181–182 / pp. 169–70). This is the monopoly of violence observed from the trial register — the sword literally passing from citizen to hired man. Ch. 8 §III or §VII; it also answers the "age of violence" cliché the draft should not lean on: "the use of weapons was not a first resort" (PDF 213 / p. 201).

**(c) The impunity clause — what the state left to private jurisdiction, and why.** "This broad swathe of violence was not penalised because it was itself seen as punitive, and punitive of those who had crossed boundaries policed by families, neighbourhoods and their patriarchies, not by the agencies of the state" (PDF 187 / p. 175). It gives Ch. 8 a precise statement of the residual private jurisdiction inside the communal order — the medieval equivalent of the "interstitial spaces" Part III maps.

**(d) The duel as the nobility's exit from the state's courts.** Dean, following Cavina: "a new type of duel, in which gentlemen and soldiers could resolve their quarrels beyond state intervention"; lords "granting permits ('field' in duelling jargon)" from the 1370s; Bartolus advising that "the emperor could not grant permission for the duel", but fifteenth-century consultants softening (PDF 190–192 / pp. 178–80). Dean's conclusion: "Participants were not seeking revenge, but proof" (PDF 193 / p. 181). Ch. 8 §III–IV: the licensed duel is the princely state *managing* rather than abolishing noble violence — the hierarchical plurality the chapter names.

**(e) The rationale of statute law as a state ideology.** "'Lest crimes go unpunished' (ne crimina remaneant impunita) was the great mobilising rationale of much medieval criminal law" (PDF 99 / p. 87); Galeazzo Visconti "deprecated the way that this [reduction of penalty on peace-making] made men 'ready to commit crimes'"; the king of Naples in 1462, "'impunity gives incentive to criminality'"; his predecessor in 1330 "forbad the composition of serious crimes" (PDF 101 / p. 89); "One final argument, used by monarchies and principalities but not by republics, announced the ruler's personal detestation of crime as a sufficient justification for new criminal law" (PDF 100 / p. 88). Judges saw themselves as "cleaners and weeders... digging out pernicious tares from the garden of state" (PDF 102 / p. 90). Ch. 8 §IV or §VII, and Ch. 9 (the personal executive).

**(f) Memorable primary material.** The Bargello of Rome, 1483, who "hanged two robbers who happened to be servants of Antonello Savelli; and in response Savelli later killed him, along with three of his men" (PDF 66 / p. 54); Giangaleazzo Visconti banning recourse to learned consultants in criminal trials "because recourse to consultants led to the avoidance or reduction of penalties" (PDF 120 / p. 108); the Ranieri vendetta at Perugia 1442–45 ending with the Podestà's "symbolic, punitive act of seizing all his belongings, and burning them in the piazza" (PDF 143 / p. 131) — "vendetta dies with him" (PDF 144 / p. 132).

**(g) A T2 comparator for the "control case" argument.** Dean's Bologna–Modena exchange of the 1490s, where Modenese officials banished thirteen Bolognesi "not 'out of zeal for justice, but only to accomplish some tacit vendetta'" (PDF 33 / p. 21), shows *states* conducting vendetta through justice across a border — a useful Italian pendant to the Villach killing in Ch. 8 §IV.

## 5. What it CONTRADICTS

**The chronology of the sequencing claim.** Ch. 8 §I states "the sixteenth century's assertion of royal justice as monopolist — was a *consequence* of the fiscal-military consolidation, not its precondition. The courts followed the companies and the taille." Dean's Italy, at full strength: the criminalisation of vendetta, the eclipse of composition by punishment, ex officio inquisition, official denunciation and the Bargello are *thirteenth- and fourteenth-century communal* developments. "In the fourteenth or fifteenth centuries, the judicial power of the state made further advances, as mechanisms for anonymous denunciation were further developed, the law became more punitive, torture was increasingly used, and the composition – settlement by private negotiation – of crimes was forbidden" (PDF 30 / p. 18); the vocabulary turned from composition to punishment "from early in the fourteenth century" (PDF 137 / p. 125); Florence hired a Bargello with power "to punish de facto and summarily" in 1315 and again in 1335 (PDF 65–66 / pp. 53–54). None of this waited on gunpowder or a permanent tax. Worse for the chapter's tidiness, Dean denies any single direction at all: "What is striking, however, is the absence of linear evolution" (PDF 63 / p. 51), and Savona in 1455 looks as it did in 1355, with "only two weapons in their 'arsenal of repression': fines and banishment" (PDF 49 / p. 37).

**The book's best answer.** Three moves, and together they are adequate. First, Dean's own qualification: the communal machine "failed to deliver much publicly visible justice: few cases were concluded, and corporal and capital punishments were relatively rarely inflicted"; justice was "'fierce', but 'inefficient'" (PDF 30 / p. 18) — assertion without enforcement, the same shape as the Empire's 1495 Landfriede, which the chapter already uses as its control case. Second, where Dean does find enforcement hardening — Mantua after 1446–47, Bologna 1450, Naples from the 1430s — it is princely, salaried and dated to the decades the mechanism predicts, and he explains the Mantuan change by "the administrative changes associated with Ludovico Gonzaga (1444–78)" (PDF 62 / p. 50). Third, the Italian communes are the precocious small-scale case the book already concedes in Chapter 4 (the condottieri) and Chapter 11: a city could build an inquisitorial apparatus on customs and gabelles without a siege train. The honest reformulation is that the *legislative* claim to monopoly long predates the fiscal-military settlement in Italy, and that what the settlement supplied was enforcement. Ch. 8 §I's sentence "the law did not tame the sword until the exchequer could pay for both" survives; "the courts followed the companies" does not, for Italy, and should be rewritten as "the courts *bit* only when the exchequer paid the men who staffed them". No spine ruling (§8(a)–(f)) is reached; the DEFEND-tier mechanism is not touched, because Dean's evidence is about courts, not about who could afford decisive force.

**A second, smaller contradiction.** The draft's "the communes and the princely regimes that followed them governed through the management of enmity as much as through its abolition" is exactly right for the thirteenth century and increasingly wrong after 1300 on Dean's account; the sentence needs a tense.

## 6. Placement

- **Ch. 8 §IV** — T2, ally and corrective, engaged by name: Dean is now the standard synthesis and should displace the "scholarly apparatus" wording; Zorzi enters as the position Dean revises. The Bologna 1492–93 letter (T1 via Dean) belongs in the text.
- **Ch. 8 §VII** — T2 evidence, by name, for the Italian sub-sequence (Naples 1430s; Mantua 1446–47; Bologna 1450; Mantua 1459–63), and for the Cherubini "fierce but inefficient" line as the Italian statement of assertion-without-enforcement.
- **Ch. 8 §III** — footnote: Dean on the duel as licensed exit; on "no word corresponding to 'feud'" in Italian usage.
- **Ch. 11 (Italian variant)** — footnote: the chronicler of Forlì, 1455.
- **Appendix B row 8** — cells: intermediaries (families and their clienteles; the noble duel; episcopal inhibitions); instruments (ex officio inquisition, Bargello, hired ducal officials 1446–47, "ne crimina remaneant impunita"); dates (composition → punishment c. 1300–1340; princely severity 1450s–60s); residual private jurisdiction (the impunity clauses, PDF 186–187 / pp. 174–75).
- Not for Ch. 7; not for Part III.

## 7. Verdict

Dean changes neither the direction nor the register of Chapter 8, but he changes its chronology and its footing: the Italian criminalisation of revenge is a communal achievement of c. 1300, not a sixteenth-century consequence of the fisc, and the chapter's sequencing sentence must be re-cut to say that the settlement bought enforcement, not law. His weight is considerable — a T2 synthesis that carries the "law-entangled vendetta" claim, supplies the dated administrative moves of the 1440s–60s that the chapter's Italian column lacks, and gives the manuscript its best single line on the monopoly of violence seen from below ("the militarisation of political power... accompanied by a disarming of the citizenry"). The one thing the author should know: the draft has Dean and Zorzi singing together and Dean's cases as Florentine; both are wrong, and the fix is easy.

## PART B — Tom Scott and Bob Scribner (eds), *The German Peasants' War: A History in Documents* (1991)

## 1. The book in two paragraphs

This is a translated documentary reader — 162 documents, mostly from Franz's *Quellen* and the *Akten* volumes — prefaced by a sixty-four-page editorial introduction that is itself a compact interpretative essay. The editors' opening claim sets the scale: "The German Peasants' War of 1524–6 was one of the greatest popular uprisings in European history, whose significance can be compared with that of the English Civil War or the French and Russian Revolutions. Unlike these, however, it was unsuccessful" (PDF 15 / p. 1), and "seems, therefore, to stand at the crossroads between medieval and modern patterns of rebellion" (PDF 15 / p. 1). Their position on causes is the one the manuscript needs: "Many peasant grievances can indeed be taken as a reaction against the emergence of an incipient bureaucracy in fledgling territorial states, whose rulers sought greater control over their subjects" (PDF 20 / p. 6), and "A major political cause of the conflict has been seen in the development of the early modern territorial state" (PDF 24 / p. 10).

Their position on the outcome is double-edged. The rebels were defeated "almost everywhere with bloodthirsty ruthlessness" (PDF 17 / p. 3), yet "it cannot be claimed that the peasants were defeated simply because of their military inadequacy. The mischief of historical accident played more than a passing role" (PDF 78 / p. 64); and on the aftermath, "It was once claimed that the defeat of the peasants' rebellion put an end to the recalcitrance of the common man and paved the way for an unrestrained control of the lords over their subjects. Both assessments have been shown by recent scholarship to be wrong" (PDF 327 / p. 314). The documents are chosen to let the reader see all of this happen.

## 2. What it CONFIRMS

**(a) Ch. 7 §V — the fiscal-administrative trigger.** The editors' account of the territorial state is the manuscript's mechanism stated by specialists in 1991: "Effective territorial control was established by replacing autonomous communal self-government by the rule of district officials or bailiffs... This process was often accompanied by replacing local customary law with Roman Law... The most obvious way of financing these developments was to levy new territorial taxes... The levying of excises on beer, wine, milling, and sometimes on slaughtering financially underpinned these fledgling territorial states, but at the same time increased the costs to their subjects" (PDF 24–25 / pp. 10–11). Bamberg "levied an investiture tax in 1501, 1503, 1505, and 1522, while five other extraordinary taxes were levied during the same period" (PDF 25 / p. 11). Burden figures: "Feudal dues or rents often accounted for as much as forty percent of production, along with ten percent in tithe. Taxation was often levied at the rate of five to ten percent of assessed wealth" (PDF 23 / p. 9).

**(b) Ch. 7 §V — the Bundschuh series and its programme.** "Bundschuh rebellions from the mid-fifteenth century onwards on the south-western fringes of the Holy Roman Empire, where the failure of the local lords to fulfil their elementary responsibility of protecting their subjects against marauding mercenaries and the Burgundian invasion led to the demand that all lordship except that of the Pope and Emperor be abolished" (PDF 24 / p. 10); Alsace "intermittent revolts between 1439 and 1445, a Bundschuh conspiracy in 1493, and an aborted general uprising in 1517" (PDF 58 / p. 44); "the Bundschuh revolts of 1513 in Lehen in the Breisgau and of 1517 on the upper Rhine" (PDF 34 / p. 20). The 1493, 1513 and 1517 dates in the draft are carried; and the draft's "restorationist paradox" is confirmed at the level of the banners: in 1525 the Sundgau flags showed "the imperial eagle, an invocation perhaps of the old Bundschuh programme of having no lords other than God and the Emperor" (PDF 59 / p. 45).

**(c) Ch. 7 §I and §V — the organisational capital of the commune.** The village commune "included all male householders, those in possession of land (Grund und Boden) who occupied a 'house and farm' (Haus und Hof)" (PDF 28 / p. 14); military organisation "probably owed much to that of the village militia, and it is no coincidence that the revolt seized those areas where the peasantry had customarily been allowed to bear arms" (PDF 30 / p. 16). This is Bohna's Kent argument (the hundred-muster turned against its operators) in its German form, and the draft should say so.

**(d) Ch. 7 §VII — "shaped, not prevented".** The Diet of Speyer's Great Committee, August 1526, conceded that new small tithes "not of ancient tradition... should be abolished", that "Those who were hitherto free or free to move may not be made serfs anymore", that "no one who shoots or catches game should henceforth be punished corporally" and that "no one should be punished by life and limb outside the law" (PDF 327–330 / pp. 314–17); the editors call these concessions "certainly minimal", omitted from the final recess (PDF 327 / p. 314). The recess itself is the consolidating instrument: "if the subjects of any secular or spiritual authority... should arouse fresh disturbance or rebellion the nearest neighboring electors, princes, counts, and other authorities shall... immediately go to its aid with horse and foot", at their own cost "for a whole month" (PDF 331 / p. 318, §§9–10). A revolt that produced an inter-princely security compact is the chapter's thesis in one document.

## 3. What it CORRECTS

**(i) Manuscript (Ch. 7 §V) gives the Bundschuh programme as "no lord but the Emperor".** The editors give it as "all lordship except that of the Pope and Emperor" (PDF 24 / p. 10) and, for the 1525 banners, "no lords other than God and the Emperor" (PDF 59 / p. 45). The Pope, or God, is dropped in the draft; it should be restored, because the ecclesiastical half is exactly what makes the demand "restorationist".

**(ii) Manuscript (Ch. 7 §V) says the Bundschuh conspiracies were "each... betrayed and suppressed before rising".** True of 1502, 1513 and 1517; the editors describe the 1493 Alsatian episode only as "a Bundschuh conspiracy" and the 1517 rising as "uncovered while it was still in preparation" (PDF 58 / p. 44), so no correction there — but the volume does not mention Untergrombach, Joss Fritz or 1502 at all, and Sélestat only as a place. The draft's 1502 sentence therefore still rests on the "DHI/BNU institutional accounts" of note [^7-5], which this volume does not upgrade.

**(iii) Manuscript (Ch. 7 §V) says "the same ending" for the German series.** The 1525 ending was not absorption. Count Philipp von Solms after Frankenhausen: "Over five thousand peasants were slain and left for dead" (PDF 304 / p. 291); margrave Casimir at Kitzingen had "sixty-two of them" blinded, and his executioner's invoice lists "80 beheaded / 69 eyes put out or fingers cut off — 114½ fl." (PDF 312–314 / pp. 299–301); the Bernese chronicler Anshelm, a chronicler's figure the editors print without endorsing, "more than 130,000 peasants slain in Upper Germany alone" (PDF 315 / p. 302). The draft says 1525 "lies just beyond this chapter's frame, and Chapter 11 will meet its aftermath"; Chapter 11 in the current assembly does not mention 1525 at all. Either the promise is honoured or the sentence goes.

**(iv) Manuscript (Ch. 7 §V) on "Armer Konrad" and Tübingen 1514.** The volume confirms only that the name was live in 1525 (Feuerbacher "had opposed the Poor Conrad rebellion in 1514", PDF 155 / p. 141; the tract's "even before your own door in the Poor Conrad [of 1514]", PDF 287 / p. 274). The Tübingen detail in the draft is untouched — neither confirmed nor corrected — and note [^7-6] stays as it is.

## 4. What it ADDS

**(a) The fiscal grievance in the rebels' own mouths — the chapter's missing T1.** Feuerbacher's reply to the Württemberg Estates' offer of a diet, from his trial: "We want no diet, don't mention anything about diets, for when one returns home from the diet it is always, 'Give money! give money!' We want to give no more money, justice is what we want" (PDF 155 / p. 141); and in the ring at Degerloch: "duke Ulrich had nothing to do with him; the Emperor is our lord, it's him we want to have; we are here because of the Word of God" (PDF 156 / p. 142). Composition, fiscal grievance and restorationist idiom in two sentences of a witness deposition. Ch. 7 §V, in the text.

**(b) The Twelve Articles as a fiscal-military counter-proposal.** Article 2 would have the tithe surplus "retained against the need to provide military service in defense of the country, which should be paid for from this surplus, so that no territorial tax will be laid upon the poor man" (PDF 267 / p. 254). The commons proposing their own settlement of the tax-for-defence question is a striking exhibit for a chapter about who pays for force. Article 9 supplies the restorationist idiom on justice — "punishment should be imposed according to the old written penalties" (PDF 269 / p. 256) — and the editors' headnote supplies the composition and provenance (Lotzer's condensation of "over three hundred articles", 27 February–1 March 1525, "printed within three weeks", PDF 265 / p. 252).

**(c) The Swabian League's fiscal substrate — the repression as a funded operation.** "There was a further problem of finance: a loan of 10,000 florins from the Fuggers in March solved the temporary problem of how to pay the troops, but not the long-term problem of how to underwrite an extended campaign" (PDF 71 / p. 57); the territorial clerk at Ravensburg, 5 May: "the Estates of the league complain bitterly about money, and are trying to raise money everywhere... if the league were not seen to act firmly and quickly to raise three armies it would not be able to resist the peasants" (PDF 170 / p. 156). Four thousand of Frundsberg's returning mercenaries "refused to fight against the peasants" (PDF 71 / p. 57). This closes a gap the chapter does not yet state: the "harvest of heads" required credit and a standing league constituted in 1488 (PDF 70 / p. 56). Ch. 7 §V and §VII; also Ch. 10 as a German instance of finance beneath force.

**(d) The disarmament clauses as the settlement's text.** Stühlingen, 12 July 1525: "Henceforth they shall have no brotherhood or hold any commune contrary to their authorities... on pain of loss of life"; "Since the peasants sounded the alarm with the great bell, this shall be removed from the tower"; "Each village shall pay his Highness six florins from each house" (PDF 316–317 / pp. 303–04). The League's penal articles: "hand over all weapons, armor, and firearms to the authorities, and no one is to possess or carry them without knowledge of the authorities" — and the Fürstenberg peasants' reply, "Beg that this be suspended and things left as of old" (PDF 317 / p. 304). Anshelm's summary: "all the articles which the peasants had initially demanded were not only rejected, but they were enforced with strict prohibitions and relentless punishments" (PDF 316 / p. 303). Ch. 7 §VII, in the text: the bell taken from the tower is the chapter's image if it wants one.

**(e) Götz von Berlichingen conscripted by the commons — Ch. 8 §III's feud-noble seen from 1525.** "'You must be their captain.' Then I said, 'Oh God, not me. This is the devil's work'"; "They took my horse by the bridle, and I had to dismount and stand in their ring"; "the captaincy lasted no more than a week" (PDF 217–218 / pp. 203–04). Doc. 88, a Franconian noble's memorandum of August 1525: "one must seize clerical property" to fund compensation, and the nobles must "relieve them of immoderate and burdensome hearth taxes, poll taxes, labor services, rents, tithes, and other imposed innovations" (PDF 223 / p. 209). The Empire's over-mighty subject, a generation after the Landfriede, in the ring of his own peasants: a footnote in Ch. 8 §III, a paragraph in Ch. 11 if the German variant is expanded.

**(f) The sociology, honestly sampled.** The editors: "Officials, urban notables, members of the nobility, rural artisans, substantial farmers, as well as those from the marginal classes and the urban lumpenproletariat took part in the revolt" (PDF 229 / p. 215). The Ebern cellarer's inventory of July 1525 lists rebels of "roughly fifteen florins' wealth", a cottage "bought for four florins", a wife who "does not have enough bread to eat" beside a steward and an innkeeper (PDF 233–234 / pp. 219–20). Leaders were innkeepers (Metzler, Feuerbacher), a former Hohenlohe chancery secretary (Hipler), a Lindau notable "placed himself at the head of the band in order to contain the rebellion" (Hurlewagen, PDF 40 / p. 26), a journeyman furrier (Lotzer), a former mercenary (Müller). In Württemberg "Thirty town mayors are said to have sat in its council" (PDF 42 / p. 28). This is the two-biased-windows problem the draft states for Kent, now available for Germany at T1.

**(g) The revolutionary tract, for balance.** *To the Assembly of Common Peasantry*: "That a territory or a commune has the power to depose harmful lords I will prove with thirteen sayings from divine laws" (PDF 284 / p. 271); "Be proud, my dear Sibyl, of your Bundschuh" (PDF 287 / p. 274). The editors call it "the most powerful revolutionary tract of the 1520s" (PDF 264 / p. 251). The draft's claim that the main body was restorationist can stand only if this voice is named as the exception, as the Merfolds are for Kent.

## 5. What it CONTRADICTS

**(i) The form's definition — "ending, not in victory or annihilation, but in absorption" (Ch. 7 §I).** At full strength, 1525 ended in annihilation followed by re-subjection, and the editors insist it was a near thing either way. The Truchsess's own assessment of Weingarten: "Only his army stood between the rebels and their complete domination of Upper Germany: had he been defeated at Weingarten, the peasants would certainly have regained their confidence" (PDF 72 / p. 58); the Ravensburg clerk: "if the league's army is once defeated, the whole land will fall and the peasants will be the lords" (PDF 170 / p. 156). The editors: "It was the short-lived nature of the revolt and not the inherent weakness of these political forms that destroyed their chances of practical implementation" (PDF 33 / p. 19); and "The success of revolutions often turns on such contingencies" (PDF 78 / p. 64). The draft's "appeared on schedule... and met the same fate" is precisely the determinism the editors refuse.

**(ii) The idiom — "restorationist to its bones".** The editors read the Twelve Articles as "a blueprint for a reform of agrarian society which could not have been implemented without undermining the foundations of the existing feudal political and social structures" (PDF 265 / p. 252); the sworn brotherhood "introduced a different principle of social organization which could easily subvert the hierarchical principles on which feudal domination rested" (PDF 30 / p. 16); the Christian Union "assumed the air of a sovereign authority" (PDF 32 / p. 18). Godly law is a new benchmark, not the old constitution: the Klettgauers would "negotiate in no other way than according to the only benchmark for justice, that is, according to the Word of God" (PDF 265 / p. 252). Article 3: "Christ has redeemed and bought us all by the shedding of his precious blood... we are free and wish to be free" (PDF 267 / pp. 254–55).

**(iii) The composition — "middling leadership... not the destitute".** See §4(f): the editors expressly include "the marginal classes and the urban lumpenproletariat", and Upper Swabia's stratification ("thirty percent fell below this minimum and were forced into by-employment", PDF 22 / p. 8) means the commune of householders sat on a large landless margin that marched.

**(iv) Spine §4 and the DEFEND-tier mechanism — who had the guns.** The commons had cannon. At Königshofen "their front ranks protected by a row of forty-seven cannon"; at Böblingen the Truchsess "was matched in artillery"; at Leubas "the peasant army gave as good as it got" (PDF 75 / p. 61). What decided the field was cavalry ("roughly one horseman for every five foot in Truchsess Georg's army", PDF 74 / p. 60) and the fortress: "effective political and military control did not always depend solely on the ability to win battles in the open field, but on the possession of strategic fortified places" (PDF 75 / p. 61); the Marienberg "perhaps the best-fortified stronghold in Germany" held (PDF 77 / p. 63). If artillery is the people's weapon too, the longbow/cannon metaphor is in trouble.

**The book's best answer.** To (i)–(iii): concede the family resemblance is looser than "the same ending", and re-scope. The propertied-anxious form describes the *first phase* the editors themselves describe — "The initial stages of revolt were usually very peaceful and resembled a strike rather than open rebellion", aimed at "an amicable settlement" (PDF 29, 34 / pp. 15, 20), led by householders and mayors — and 1525 is the case where the form broke its own banks into godly-law revolution and was then annihilated; the absorption came a year later, minimal, at Speyer, and the recess's mutual-aid clause is the consolidation the chapter predicts. That answer is good, but only if the chapter stops claiming 1525 as an instance and starts claiming it as the boundary case on the other side from the Ciompi — the form exceeded upward rather than undershot from below. The editors' insistence on contingency is harder: the chapter's "on schedule" must become "under a probability the state's fiscal reach kept raising", which is calibrated and still falsifiable. To (iv): the answer is the mechanism itself, and it is strong. The peasants' guns were "able to seize or requisition artillery from castles or towns" (PDF 75 / p. 61) — captured, not cast, fed or paid for; Götz reported the band before Horneck "had not a single cannon with which they could dislodge a single stone from the wall" (PDF 216 / p. 202); the League fought on a Fugger loan and a constitution of 1488, and the rebels' rotation system "robbed them of the chance to build up expertise and discipline" (PDF 73 / p. 59) because no one could pay them to stay. The decisive asymmetry was not the gun but the siege train plus the treasury plus the standing structure — which is spine §4's cannon correctly restated (the cannon is "the full-stack... state", not the barrel). Chapter 7 should say this in a sentence, because it is the best short demonstration in the corpus that a captured weapon is not a decisive one. No spine ruling under §8 is reached; the DEFEND claim is not damaged but must be worded to survive the fact that the commons had cannon.

## 7. Verdict

Scott and Scribner do not change the book's direction — their account of the territorial state's bureaucracy, Roman law and excises is the manuscript's mechanism from the peasant's side — but they change Chapter 7's register: "on schedule" and "the same ending" cannot survive an edition whose editors argue that 1525 was contingent, nearly won, and ended in slaughter rather than absorption. Their weight is large and mostly T1: the chapter gains the rebels' own fiscal grievance in a deposition, the Twelve Articles' tax-for-defence clause, the disarmament and mutual-aid texts that are the settlement itself, and the League's Fugger loan as the substrate of repression. The one thing the author should know: the commons of 1525 had forty-seven cannon at Königshofen and still lost — which, correctly stated, is the strongest short proof in the corpus that a captured gun is not a decisive one, and, incorrectly stated, is the objection to spine §4 that a hostile reader will raise first.

## JOINT VERDICT

Neither book redirects the manuscript; both correct its chronology in the same direction, and the correction is the same lesson twice. Dean shows that Italian communes claimed the monopoly of punishment around 1300 and could not enforce it evenly until salaried princely officers arrived in the 1440s–60s; Scott and Scribner show that the Empire's lords could enforce nothing in April 1525 until a Fugger loan and a league constituted in 1488 put an army in the field — assertion is cheap, enforcement is bought, and the fiscal-administrative step is where both stories turn. The register of Chapter 7 must loosen ("shaped, not prevented" survives; "on schedule... the same fate" does not), the sequencing sentence of Chapter 8 must be re-cut to say the settlement bought enforcement rather than law, and Appendix B rows 7 and 8 can now be filled at T1/T2. The single most important thing across both: the manuscript's German and Italian comparators currently rest on institutional web accounts and a mis-attributed Dean; these two volumes replace that footing entirely, and in doing so hand the author the two best short exhibits for the mechanism — the disarmed citizen of Visconti Bologna and the captured cannon of Königshofen.
