## 1. The book in two paragraphs

An anthology, so the "argument" is the debate's shape as Rogers frames it. Roberts's 1955 Belfast inaugural (published 1956) held that tactical reform under Maurice and Gustavus drove army growth and army growth drove the state: "The transformation in the scale of war led inevitably to an increase in the authority of the state… Only the state, now, could supply the administrative, technical and financial resources required for large-scale hostilities. And the state was concerned to make its military monopoly absolute" (Roberts, p. 20 / PDF p. 33). Parker (1976) kept the state conclusion, dropped the tactical cause, moved the start back to the 1430s–1530s and substituted the trace italienne; Rogers (1993) moved it back to the Hundred Years War and dissolved the single revolution into "punctuated equilibrium evolution" — Infantry (1302–46), Artillery (1420s–40s), Artillery-Fortress (1520s–40s), Roberts's administrative revolution — "each an attempt to reverse a disequilibrium introduced by the previous one" (p. 57 / PDF p. 70). Black places the decisive change after 1660 and reverses the arrow; Thompson, on Spain, asks whether "war=taxes=state" is "an inversion of the proper syntax, state=taxes=war" (p. 273 / PDF p. 286).

Parker's rejoinder is the volume's synthesis: "clearly the two developments fed upon each other, and indeed required each other… Rather than trying to establish a simple model of causation, we should perhaps envisage something like the 'double helix' structure of the DNA molecule, with two complex spirals interacting at various discrete points over time" (pp. 340–41 / PDF pp. 353–54). Rogers's thesis sentence, which the manuscript carries at second hand, is here at first hand: "Artillery was expensive. As early as 1442, the French government was spending more than twice as much on its artillery train as it spent on more traditional war materiel—arrows, lances, bows, etc. The central governments of large states could afford to acquire and maintain large siege trains: their subjects and smaller neighbors, in general, could not" (p. 74 / PDF p. 87; n. 142 citing Contamine, *Guerre*, 666).

## 2. What it CONFIRMS

**Intro §IV — Roberts's "great divide."** Quoted in the manuscript without a note. Pinned: Roberts, p. 13 / PDF p. 26: "It stands like a great divide separating mediaeval society from the modern world." Provenance (Credits, p. xi): "a revision of an inaugural lecture delivered before The Queen's University of Belfast on 21 January 1955; it was first published in its current form in Michael Roberts, *Essays in Swedish History* (Minneapolis, 1967), pp. 195–225"; Parker (p. 37) and Lynn (n. 1) cite the Belfast 1956 pamphlet. Cite as Roberts 1956 [1967], in Rogers (ed.) 1995, p. 13.

**Ch06 §VII / ch10 §VII — the Rogers cycle, first-hand.** Ch10 pins it "at DeVries 1998: 128." The passage: "central governments of large states could afford artillery trains and large armies. The artillery trains counteracted centrifugal forces and enabled the central governments to increase their control over outlying areas of their realms, or to expand at the expense of their weaker neighbors. This increased their tax revenues, enabling them to support bigger artillery trains and armies… and so on" (p. 75 / PDF p. 88; n. 151 ties it to Finer's coercion-extraction cycle). Also p. 74: "Regional interests lost their ability to defy central authorities; small states and semi-independent regions were gobbled up by their larger neighbors."

**Ch06 §VII — "new orthodoxy."** [^6-61] Rogers 1993: 242 = reprint p. 55 / PDF p. 68: "The idea of the military revolution rapidly became the 'new orthodoxy'… passing almost unchallenged until 1976."

**Ch06 §§I, IV — the Somerset list, Harfleur, Guienne.** "A contemporary English document lists one-hundred strongpoints taken by the French in 1450, including Chateau Gaillard, St.-Sauveur-le-Vicomte, Cherbourg, Roche-Guyon, and Rouen, all of which had earlier required long sieges to capture. In 1451, all of Guienne fell rapidly to the French despite the deeply ingrained pro-English sympathies of the inhabitants of the duchy" (p. 67 / PDF p. 80; n. 110: most "were actually never assaulted; they surrendered before then"). Harfleur "made terms after a seventeen-day bombardment in 1449" (n. 108: sixteen large bombards, Chartier 2:178–79); Bayeux sixteen days; Blaye "only five days" (p. 67). T2 support for spine §8(e)5.

**Ch10 §VII — Parker.** "The English possessions in France were likewise reconquered… largely by Charles VII's artillery; at Castillon in 1453, the big guns even won a battle… by 1500 every major European state possessed a powerful artillery park for use against its neighbours or against its dissident subjects" (1976, p. 42 / PDF p. 55). Caution: Parker's "in the 1430s and 1440s" is his imprecision; do not import it.

**Spine §8(a).** Parker p. 340: "The emergence of the 'Renaissance State'… constituted an essential precondition for the important military changes of the late fifteenth and early sixteenth centuries; while, conversely, the need to mobilize resources for war could enhance governments' power over their subjects." Roberts, p. 22: "the income for maintaining standing armies was taken out of the control of the Estates" — the ratchet, a century on.

**Ch10 §II.** Lynn p. 121 / PDF p. 134: "Charles VII… created the first permanent standard units, the compagnies d'ordonnance, in 1445. These 15 companies each contained 100 lances, at a time when a lance included six mounted men, of whom only four qualified as combatants. Therefore, 1,500 lances produced 6,000 combatants, not the 9,000 often cited" (citing Contamine, *Guerre*, 278–83, 286).

## 3. What it CORRECTS

1. **Intro §IV and ch06 §VII attribute a ranking Rogers does not make.** Manuscript: "Rogers, ranking the infantry revolution above the artillery revolution"; "In his reconstruction, the deeper break came a century earlier." Rogers, p. 56 / PDF p. 69: "twice over the course of the Hundred Years War new developments revolutionized the conduct of war in Europe, in each case with consequences as significant for the history of the world as those which took place during Parker's Military Revolution… Each… truly deserves to be termed a 'military revolution' in itself." He ranks neither; his point (pp. 76–77) is that they are sequential punctuations of equal status. The contest ch06 stages ("the objection comes, inconveniently, from the historian this chapter has cited more than any other") is partly manufactured. What Rogers does give is the dispersive social reading of the Infantry Revolution (pp. 61–62), which the chapter's answer already uses. Rewrite: Rogers does not rank; he refuses a single hinge.

2. **Ch06 §VII understates 1442.** Manuscript: "a first-rate siege train cost more than a government's entire traditional arsenal." Rogers p. 74: "more than twice as much." Ch06 §III and ch10 §VIII already say "twice"; align.

3. **Ch10 §VII provenance.** [^10-36] "Rogers's cycle, at DeVries 1998: 128" and [^10-35] "Porter, at DeVries 1998: 128": Rogers is in corpus (reprint p. 75); Porter, *War and the Rise of the State* (1994), ch. 3, is named at Rogers n. 33 (p. 10) but not in corpus — pin Rogers first-hand, leave Porter as retrieval.

4. **Ch06 §II.** "The business of this chapter is the decade in which the curve turned." Rogers: "a revolution occurred in the art of war around the 1420–30s" (p. 67), "around 1430" (p. 75); 1449–53 is the demonstration at scale. Spine §8(a) already dates the revolution to the 1420s–30s; the running text should not say the curve turned in 1449–50.

5. **Intro §IV.** "in the tradition of the military-revolution historians — Roberts's 'great divide'…" borrows Roberts's authority for a chronology (1560–1660) and a mechanism (drill, army size) he did not share with this book; say so or cut.

## 4. What it ADDS

- **Ch06 §II — replace the English Heritage [GAP: T3].** Pierre Dubois, early fourteenth century (p. 74; n. 143, Verbruggen 273): "A castle can hardly be taken within a year, and even if it does fall, it means more expenses for the king's purse and for his subjects than the conquest is worth… leaders are apt to come to agreements which are unfavorable to the stronger party." Rogers's ledger, p. 66 / PDF p. 79: Rouen "nearly six months"; "Cherbourg was starved out after seven months in 1418; Melun after eighteen weeks in 1420; Meaux after seven months in 1421; Montaguillon after six months in 1423. The strong castle of Chateau Gaillard… held out for six months, until the cords the garrison used to draw up drinking water wore out." The c. 1420 German treatise: the defender "will be able to hold off the enemy… until he is relieved" (p. 65) — the délai de secours before the guns, in period words.

- **Ch06 §II — the tipping-point [GAP].** Rogers pp. 68–73: barrel length from 1–1.5 ball-diameters in 1400 to "3:1 or more" by 1430; hooped-stave construction "shortly before 1420"; limestone flux; corned powder "in almost universal use by 1420"; gun prices "fell significantly (about a third)" (p. 72); "between 1400 and 1430, a whole series of interconnected innovations synergistically improved the power and efficiency of gunpowder artillery" (p. 73). Closes the GAP at T2 pending DeVries/Hall.

- **Ch10 §VII — name Parker's "double helix"** as the T2 form of spine §8(a); and Lynn's test (p. 117): "for military necessity to have brought on government reform, the growth of the army must have predated that reform, not the other way around" — which the book passes only via the ratchet. Also p. 75: "tax revenues of central governments in Western Europe doubled in real, per capita terms between 1450 and 1500" (n. 152: McNeill citing Bean 1973 — carry at T3).

- **Ch07 (Cade).** Rogers pp. 61–62: the 1381 rebels "were generally armed with longbows, and their leader, Wat Tyler, had seen service in France"; Charles VI dropped universal bow training because the archers "if they had been gathered together, would have been more powerful than the princes and nobles"; the 40-shilling franchise was "the same amount which legally obliged him to own a bow."

- **Ch04.** Parker p. 45: "in all Europe, only Oliver Cromwell managed to… wrest political power from his civilian employers"; "War departments proliferated in every country, squeezing out military entrepreneurs and other middlemen."

- **Ch11 §III — Granada as the Iberian run.** Parker via Rogers p. 74: "a siege-train of some 180 guns" reduced Granada "within ten years (1482–1492)"; Thompson p. 274: armies "increased from 20,000 to 60,000 within ten years"; Bernaldez in Parker p. 345: "Great towns, which once would have held out a year against all foes but hunger, now fell within a month." Ch11's Spanish section has the Hermandad and the Orders but not the guns. Also Parker p. 344 for the Elton engagement: the Tudor Revolution in Government coincided with unprecedented military spending, "inconceivable without the former. But that is not to say that the one caused the other."

- **Ottoman disanalogy (ch05/ch11).** Guilmartin pp. 319–20: "an elaborate and remarkably efficient system of irregular taxes in kind… a decentralized and largely self-sustaining bureaucracy… The thrust of the system was to minimize cash payments from the center."

- **Appendix A §VII.** The anchor entry the section says the literature lacked — Roberts, Parker, Rogers, Parker's rejoinder and a ten-page historiography in one volume.

## 5. What it CONTRADICTS

**(i) Thompson — guns were marginal and not a monopoly.** Pp. 279–80 / PDF pp. 292–93: "the entire annual expenditure on the artillery account hardly ever represented more than about 4 or 5 percent of Castile's total domestic military budget (a figure that was again reasonably constant from the 1490s)." "If gunpowder technology added only marginally to the cost of war to the state, neither was it a monopoly of the state. Evidence survives of large aristocratic arsenals even in the seventeenth century. The marquis of Las Navas… had twenty-five pieces of artillery which he donated to the king in lieu of taxes; they were worth 30,000 ducats… some 50 tons, as much as the entire siege train on the Spanish Armada. Though private arsenals of this size were a rarity, they were in decline clearly not because they could not be afforded, but because they were not needed." On the chain (p. 290): "War, therefore, did not necessarily have a centralizing effect, and Schumpeter's 'common exigency' may have inhibited the development of the state as much as it inspired it… In neither instance is the notion of an ascending cycle of coercion and extraction a particularly useful explanatory model of state building." Spain ended "with great theoretical authority but limited effective power" through "the devolution and privatization of coercive-extractive and military-administrative functions" (p. 291). This reaches the DEFEND-tier mechanism (spine §6) and §8(a).

*The book's best answer.* The mechanism claims an entry threshold in 1440–1500, not a persistent budget share. Thompson's 4–5 per cent is a share of a treasury whose spending "multiplied at least twenty-fold" (p. 274); the denominator is the standing establishment the threshold created, and a constant small share of a vastly larger budget is what a capability looks like once it is routine. Rogers's ratio (guns to traditional matériel, 1442) and Thompson's (guns to total military spend, post-1490) measure different things. Las Navas's park is fortress metal, not a train with powder, carriage, gunners and payroll — and Thompson's "not needed" is the suppression the book describes. Parker's rejoinder (p. 353) adds that fortification costs were devolved to communities, so central-treasury shares understate gunpowder's fiscal reach. Is the answer good? On the threshold, yes. On Thompson's deeper point — that redistribution of costs between state and society, contracting and local fiscality could run *against* centralisation for 150 years in the most heavily armed monarchy in Europe — no. Ch10 §VII must concede that the chain's later links (finance → administration → suppression) are not automatic, and that Spain is a case where the first link fired and the chain stalled.

**(ii) Black — the arrow runs the other way, and later.** "the major changes took place after 1660" (p. 97); the 1980s redefined absolutism "away from an emphasis on coercion and, instead, towards one on a greater measure of consensus… The extent to which more effective military forces reflected not more autocratic states but rather crown-elite co-operation is more apparent" (p. 95). *Best answer:* Black himself credits "c. 1470–c. 1530" with revolutionary status (p. 110) and concedes "Military strength was linked to centralization and state expansion" (p. 109); the consensus point is met by the ratchet — elite cooperation *after* the guns removed the option of refusal. Adequate if ch10 §VII names Black beside Curry.

**(iii) Arnold and Rogers — the mechanism reversed within eighty years.** Rogers's cycle runs "at least until the advent of the Artillery Fortress Revolution of the early sixteenth century" (p. 75). Arnold: "the trace italienne took a weakly held, barely sovereign state—Gonzaga Montferrat—and made it politically viable and nearly invulnerable militarily. That is a revolution indeed" (p. 220); the fortification race "had narrowed the power gaps between city-states and princely states, and between princely states and the great European kingdoms" (p. 221); the Huguenot bastions "held off the forces of the French crown for sixty years" (p. 221). *Best answer:* the fortress restored small states and confessional cities, not castle-holding magnates — it froze the map between polities after consolidation inside them, and Parker shows it drove costs and garrisons up (40 per cent of Louis XIV's army, p. 353). But the Huguenot case is a reversal inside a kingdom, and the lesson for Part III is that a defensive counter-technology can re-open the gap for two generations. Appendix C's timetable must allow for it. Good enough only if stated.

## 7. Verdict

The volume changes the manuscript's weight and register, not its direction: Roberts, the Rogers cycle and the 1442 sentence can now be pinned at first hand, the co-evolution ruling acquires a T2 name (Parker's "double helix," p. 341), and the sceptics-concede arc becomes honest by admitting the sceptic who does not concede. The single most important thing the author should know is Thompson: in Spain after 1490 the artillery account never exceeded 4–5 per cent of the domestic military budget, aristocratic arsenals survived into the seventeenth century, and "war… did not necessarily have a centralizing effect" (pp. 280, 290) — the mechanism's first link is secure, its later links stalled for 150 years in Europe's most heavily armed state, and ch10 §VII must say so rather than assemble the chain "almost entirely in the words of the scholars who resist" it. Secondary: stop casting Rogers as a rival who ranks the infantry revolution above the artillery revolution; he does not, and the book's strongest witness is an ally who merely refuses a single hinge.
