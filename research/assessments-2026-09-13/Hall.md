# Assessment — Bert S. Hall, *Weapons and Warfare in Renaissance Europe: Gunpowder, Technology, and Tactics* (Johns Hopkins, 1997)

Read whole (introduction, chs 1–7, notes to chs 2, 4, 6, 7). Printed page = PDF page − 18; pins give printed page with PDF page in brackets. OCR is serviceable but Italianate ("piace", "carne"); running heads garbled on about one page in six; figures, tables and the ch. 3 equations (p. 74) are lost. Nothing load-bearing is illegible.

## 1. The book in two paragraphs

Hall is a historian of technology, asking of gunpowder what one asks of any tool: how it was made, at what cost, and what it could not do. His finding is that the gun's history is the history of its fuel — saltpetre plantations from the 1380s broke the price bottleneck; fifteenth-century "Knollen" corning was a storage fix that suited small arms and endangered great guns; only sixteenth-century controlled-grain corning produced the "early modern ordnance synthesis" that then stood still for three centuries. On the 1440s his verdict is organisational: "Better management, organization, and logistics seem to have played more of a role in most cases than did better ballistics" (p. 106 [PDF 124]); "Nothing in the sources from either side suggests that the French guns were qualitatively different or technologically 'advanced'" (p. 120 [PDF 138]).

The last chapter engages Roberts and Parker sceptically. "Most of the technical changes in gunpowder … served to create only a brief moment of superiority for the cannon. Almost as suddenly as it began, the reign of the gun came to a close" (p. 211 [PDF 229]); the *trace italienne* "represented a restoration of the status quo ante with regard to siege warfare" (p. 162 [PDF 180]); "The 'military revolution' was preceded by a 'military restoration'" (p. 212 [PDF 230]). And his causal arrow runs against the manuscript's: army enlargement "was less driven by technical or tactical necessities than drawn by the new ability of the early modern state to raise revenues that it could spend as it saw fit" (p. 224 [PDF 242]).

## 2. What it CONFIRMS

**"The protagonist of this chapter is not a gadget. It is a payroll" (ch. 6 §III).** The Bureaux presided over "the replacement of older and somewhat haphazard methods of procuring ordnance … by a true 'royal artillery' consisting of an organized system of personnel, arsenals, and magazines" (p. 115 [PDF 133]); "The French probably built their victory more on administrative and logistical efforts than on technological advances. Jean Bureau was the fiscal and administrative genius, whereas Gaspard seemed to have been more of a technical expert" (p. 120 [PDF 138]).

**"Walls and minds" (ch. 6 §IV–V).** Guns' "greatest contribution was to convince the defending garrison that further resistance was pointless and to make the defenders willing to surrender on terms" (p. 66 [PDF 84]) — though Hall dates this to 1375–1419.

**Castillon and Bureau (ch. 6 §VI; spine §8(e)4).** "Jean Bureau himself had insisted on besieging Castillon, and he supervised the laying out of the French lines and encampment; indeed, he may have laid out the camp specifically to trap Talbot" (p. 117 [PDF 135]); "Castillon clearly involved siege guns pressed into service as field pieces … the layout of the French camp made the guns into something like wall-defense weapons" (p. 131 [PDF 149]).

**Formigny drawn small (ch. 6 §VI).** "it seems likely that the French had [no field artillery], despite the victories attributed (correctly) to cannon at Formigny and Castillon" (p. 131).

**Siege as the king's business (ch. 10 §VII).** Master gunners in Castilian royal service "from only 4 … in 1479" to "65 by late 1482 and 91 in 1485"; "some 1,500 carts just for the artillery train" by 1485; "siege operations become more and more the preserve of the king's own forces, while the *tala*, raiding and burning …, was left to the nobles and their private armies" (pp. 124–25 [PDF 142–43]). A second-comparator confirmation of the threshold step, hedged (§5).

**The Swiss control group (ch. 10 §VI).** Pikes "did not, of course, depend on any technological innovation … Their effective use on the battlefield depended entirely on recruitment, training, and discipline" (p. 211 [PDF 229]); Charles the Bold's defeat "was a victory for pikes over guns" (p. 132 [PDF 150]).

**The two layers (ch. 10 §VIII).** "The cleavage between artillery and small arms is a matter of physics … no generalization about early modern firearms that seeks to comprise both small arms and artillery is likely to be applicable to both" (p. 106 [PDF 124]).

**Fisc before guns, made in practice (spine §8(a)).** "from the 1430s onward the administration of Charles VII imposed taxes in proportion to the size of the royal army without the consent of the Estates-General" (p. 224 [PDF 242], citing Wolfe, *Fiscal*, 25–52) — the Wolfe pages ch. 10 §III's [GAP] awaits.

## 3. What it CORRECTS

1. **Ch. 6 §III: Jean Bureau "after 1453, mayor of Bordeaux."** Hall pp. 116–17 [PDF 134–35]: "On 30 June 1451 Bordeaux welcomed her new masters, and Jean Bureau himself became the city's new mayor"; in 1452–53 "Jean Bureau, no longer mayor of Bordeaux, reverted to his old job". The mayoralty begins in 1451.

2. **Ch. 6 §II: walls "out-ranged the trebuchet's flat trajectories."** Hall p. 21 [PDF 39]: the counterweight trebuchet, "the wall-breaking machine par excellence", shot "at a 40- to 45-degree angle", so "the trajectory at impact will probably be about 30 to 40 degrees above the horizon, and the energy at impact can be absorbed by a suitably reinforced masonry curtain wall"; p. 14 [PDF 32], walls "were heightened and thickened". The flat trajectory is the gun's. The tall-thin contrast survives; its premise is wrong.

3. **Ch. 6 §III: corned powder "burned uniformly."** Hall p. 73 [PDF 91]: "the problem of atmospheric moisture absorption was what stimulated the earliest attempts at corning"; fifteenth-century crumb powder "exhibits the worst features of any corned powder: shape irregularities, variability of size, and surface roughness" (p. 86 [PDF 104]). Uniform burn belongs to sixteenth-century controlled-grain corning (pp. 101–3). Say "kept and burned better", or drop the ballistic claim.

4. **Ch. 6 §III: "their great guns threw stone" in the Bureau era.** True to 1451. Hall p. 119 [PDF 137], citing Dubled: "Jean Bureau is said to have begun to employ 'a new type of powder' only at the beginning of 1453. The same date seems to apply to his adoption of cast iron shot. Innovations in production, such as casting in bronze, came somewhat earlier, in 1440–45." The 1453 campaign is the first with both.

5. **Ch. 6 §VI, Cadillac.** To the Vale (17 Sept execution) / Escouchy (storm 19 Sept) discrepancy Hall p. 118 [PDF 136] adds a third account: "a heavy artillery bombardment that lasted some eight days and ended in complete capitulation on 27 September. Charles VII personally ordered the garrison captain's execution" (citing Labarge; Basin). Capitulation, not storm; resolve at the sources before "the only storming in the record" goes to print.

6. **Ch. 6 §V: "before December … not one documented fall is bombardment-driven."** Hall p. 115 [PDF 133]: at Rouen "the townspeople rose against their English garrison after only three days of bombardment" (Griffiths, *Henry VI*, 516–17). Compatible with the reputational reading, but check Griffiths.

7. **Ch. 6 §VI: Formigny's culverins "light wheeled pieces."** Hall p. 116 [PDF 134]: "probably heavier guns". Carry both readings.

## 4. What it ADDS

**The tipping-point GAP (ch. 6 §II), closed with prices.** Powder ~10 sous/lb in the 1370s; "By the 1420s in France they were half what they had been in the 1380s, about 5 sous tournois per pound"; Frankfurt saltpetre 41 florins/cwt (1381–83) → 16 (1416) → 9–10 (1439–40); "by the last quarter of the fifteenth century French gunpowder prices were less than 20 percent of what they had been a century earlier, a level at which they came to be fixed by law" (p. 58 [PDF 76]). The 1442 programme's 2,200 l.t. for 20,000 lb (2.2 sous/lb) sits on Hall's curve — an independent check on Contamine's document. Strategic dating: the trend "plays a significant strategic role in such campaigns as the Lancastrian conquest of Normandy between 1410 and 1425" (p. 66 [PDF 84]); Henry V's Normandy powder bill "would have been considered ruinously expensive by the standards of an earlier age" (p. 64 [PDF 82]). Cheap powder by c. 1420, organised train by the 1440s: the sequence the chapter needs.

**Replacing the English Heritage T3 line (ch. 6 §II).** "Treachery, especially involving bribery, and trickery remained the best methods of inducing a surrender, although simple starvation seems to have been the strongest weapon an attacker could hope to employ" (p. 14 [PDF 32]); n. 23 (p. 238): "Contamine, *War*, 101, gives anecdotal accounts of eight thirteenth-century sieges lasting from five months to one year." For the coda: "the average French siege between 1500 and 1600 lasted about 65 days" (p. 211).

**The Hussite GAP (ch. 10 §VIII).** John the Fearless "some 4,000 handgunners" in 1411; Nuremberg "501 Handbüchsen but only 607 crossbows" in 1430 (p. 95 [PDF 113]); "the experiments with crumb powder that culminated in the arquebus … had their genesis in attempts to foil the heretics' successes" (p. 114 [PDF 132]); but "Small arms seem to have played a fairly minor part in the overall Hussite mix" (p. 112). And the T2 sentence for the two layers: "smaller scales of ambition correlate with smaller scales of arms and arms production. The German cities had no need for arms to conduct major sieges, but they wanted to be able to defend themselves against sieges" (p. 100 [PDF 118]).

**An Empire comparator (ch. 10 §VI).** In 1422 the cities "agreed to an unprecedented 1 percent tax on all governments in Germany" against the Hussites (p. 114) — a gunpowder-driven tax that built no state.

**Primary colour (ch. 6).** Cherbourg: batteries "on the tidal flats … 'Three bombards and one cannon' are said to have burst" (p. 116 [PDF 134]; Chartier ii. 231–32). Bordeaux 1453: "the city was forced to pay for two citadels to ensure its future obedience … the walls were allowed to remain intact" (p. 118) — §VI's constitutional demotion made concrete. Berry Herald, Stevenson's translation: "no one can remember a Christian king ever having such great artillery" (p. 118; Stevenson 1863 in corpus).

**Talbot (ch. 6 §VI).** "commanders could not rely on sieges to consume the attackers' time and money. Resisting the attacker in open battle became, on balance, a better option than before" (pp. 210–11 [PDF 228–29]) — T2 ground for the seize-the-train conjecture. Hall himself sides with temerity (Talbot "unwisely attacked", p. 117) and belongs in the dissent list beside Howard.

**Ch. 10 §II:** "by the end of 1441, some 15,000 men had already been mustered" under crown control (p. 115). **Ch. 10 §VIII:** finished guns "three to one, bronze over iron", iron guns by mid-century "one-fourth" of 1430s prices (p. 93 [PDF 111]); "Whereas once warring princes had sought to recruit skills, now they could recruit raw manpower" (p. 148 [PDF 166]).

## 5. What it CONTRADICTS

**(i) "Above what any subject could pay" — the census "with one entry" (ch. 6 §VII; ch. 10 §VII; DEFEND-tier mechanism).** "The king of France did not enjoy a monopoly on artillery in this period. There were private siege trains, some of sufficient size to challenge the royal prerogatives Charles VII sought to assert, and there were ambitious nobles who were more than willing to use these to their own advantage, especially the old enemies of the crown, the dukes of Burgundy" (p. 120 [PDF 138]); "private siege trains persisted in Spain, as they did in France" (p. 125 [PDF 143]); "Burgundy's resources were not inferior to the king's"; Dinant fell in 1466 "after only a week's work with his guns, even though Dinant had successfully resisted seventeen previous siege attempts" (p. 121 [PDF 139]). And guns and powder got *cheaper* across the window — powder to a fifth, iron guns to a quarter — while cities bought them freely (Ghent's "200 carts" in 1382, p. 49). The 2:1 budget line is a unit-price argument in a century of falling unit prices.

*The book's best answer.* The threshold was scale, recurrence and organisation, not unit price: "To make such tactics work required immense administrative ability, attention to details of logistics and transport" (p. 130 [PDF 148]), and Hall's own Granada figures show siege drifting to the crown as trains grew. Rewrite the 1442 passage so the 2:1 reads as the *establishment* absorbing the budget, and re-grade "no subject could pay" to "no subject could sustain it as a standing, provincial-scale instrument". Good — but it concedes: Burgundy was a rival, yet Liège's princes and the German cities were subjects. The mechanism survives at that grade; the aphorism does not.

**(ii) The military restoration (spine §8(a) "irreversible"; §2 "sieges … over in weeks").** "the tilt created by successful siege artillery was quite short-lived … fairly inexpensive earthworks could be constructed to modify existing walls" — Pisa 1500, Padua 1509 (p. 161 [PDF 179]); the *trace italienne* "was actually the culmination of a reactive process … after a brief interlude when the attacker's guns held the advantage, the defenders reasserted their customary privilege" (pp. 162–63 [PDF 180–81]); the English were already building *boulevards* in 1449 (p. 119). If walls regained their time value within two generations, what did the guns make irreversible?

*The book's best answer.* Hall supplies it: "The new fortifications were fearsomely expensive to build … Papal Rome planned a belt of eighteen bastions but gave up after spending more than 44,000 ducats for just one"; Siena bankrupted itself on seventeen sites and fell in 1555; "The trace italienne could provide defense against conquest by cannon, but only for those able to afford the protection it offered" (p. 164 [PDF 182]). The fiscal threshold migrated from attacker to defender, and the migration consolidates further: in the restored equilibrium only a fisc can hold a wall. The ratchet's object is untouched. A strong answer the manuscript has not yet made; ch. 10 §IV stops at 1494.

**(iii) The causal arrow (spine §6 DEFEND).** Charles VII's 1430s taxes were "what made the strong royal armies of later decades possible, not vice versa" (p. 224); and, against the competitive-adaptation logic Part III rests on, "states seem not to have regarded any particular type of weapon as critical to their survival … technological change was not necessarily sought for the sake of competitive advantage; it may well have been resisted because of the costs" (p. 4 [PDF 22]).

*The book's best answer.* Partial. On the 1430s–40s sequence the 2 September ruling already concedes what Hall asserts — he is an ally misread as a rival. On competitive adaptation Hall exempts the case the book needs: "The most extensive development of artillery took place in France, propelled by the French crown's wish to reverse the tides of war … This political purpose probably shaped the destiny of firearms more than any other single policy in the century" (p. 130); artillery in the fifteenth century is one of his two "model cases of how technology can alter longstanding habits in fairly short order" (p. 215 [PDF 233]). But his seventeenth-century evidence — real army growth came 150 years later and was fiscal in origin — warns against letting the mechanism drift past 1500.

**(iv) The longbow as "the people's weapon" (spine §4).** The longbow "depended on an entire peasant culture to sustain it" (p. 20 [PDF 38]); "None but the king of England could seriously hope to employ masses of archers in pitched battles" (p. 38 [PDF 56]). The democratised, learn-in-weeks weapon was the crossbow and then the arquebus (pp. 20, 148).

*The book's best answer.* The metaphor is about decisiveness, not accessibility, and the Agincourt caveat already refuses the longbow-alone story. Adequate for that half; but "democratised, cheap, effective en masse" is the pike's or hand-gun's property, and ch. 6 §VII's "raised by anybody with a valley or a town" is true of the Swiss and false of the English retinue.

## 7. Verdict

Hall changes the manuscript's register, not its direction: "above what any subject could pay" cannot survive his private-siege-trains passage and falling price series in aphoristic form and must be restated as a threshold of scale and permanence — which is what the chapter already argues everywhere except in that sentence. The single most important thing to take from him is that the sceptic's own evidence hands the book its answer to the *trace italienne* objection it has not yet faced: the gun's superiority lasted two generations, but the fortress that ended it cost more than any subject could build, so the fiscal threshold migrated to the defender and the consolidation ratcheted a second time. On the fisc-first sequence he is an ally the 2 September ruling has already met; on the seventeenth century he is a standing warning not to let the mechanism drift past 1500.
