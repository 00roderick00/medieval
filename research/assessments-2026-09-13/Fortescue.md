# Fortescue, *The Governance of England*, ed. Charles Plummer (Oxford, 1885) — assessment against *The Artillery State*

## 1. The book in two paragraphs

Plummer's volume is two books. Fortescue's treatise (1471–76 by Plummer's table, p. xxiii, PDF 27) is a Lancastrian chief justice's programme for Edward IV: the king is poor and his greatest subjects richer than he; the remedy is not to destroy the lords but to out-endow the crown — resume the alienated demesne and amortise it so that it "mey neuer be alyened þerfro, without þe assent off his parlement" (p. 154, PDF 182); give every office to sworn king's men, one office each; veto the marriages and purchases by which lordships accumulate; replace the baronial council with twenty-four salaried professionals who "shall take no ffee, nor clothynge, nor no rewardes off any man, except only off þe kynge" (p. 146, PDF 174). The thesis is stated as a law of nature: "when a subget hath hade also gret livelod as his prince, he hath anon aspired to þe estate of his prince" (p. 128, PDF 156); "Ffor the peple will go with hym þat best mey susteyne and rewarde ham" (p. 129, PDF 157); "For certanly ther mey no grettir perell growe to a prince, than to haue a subgett equepolent to hym selff" (p. 130, PDF 158).

Plummer's Introduction (1885) supplies the frame in which the treatise has been read since. It coins "bastard feudalism" (p. 15, PDF 43), puts "over-mighty subjects" into quotation marks as the name of Fortescue's obsession (p. 14, PDF 42), and — against Stubbs — reads the treatise as the germ of what followed: "In this, and in his proposals for permanently endowing the crown and reducing the power of the nobles, he certainly prepares the way, however unconsciously, for what it is the fashion to call the New Monarchy" (p. 87, PDF 115). The volume is both a T1 primary ("the earliest constitutional treatise written in the English language", p. 86, PDF 114) and a dated T2 commentary whose French chronology the spine has overruled.

## 2. What it CONFIRMS

**Chapter 5 §I — the heading as quoted.** The manuscript quotes Fortescue's chapter heading "the perellis that mey come to the kyng by ouer myghtye subgettes" with a flag "wording to be verified against the page images". Verified: the printed heading of ch. IX, p. 127 (PDF 155), is "HERE HE SHEWITH THE PERELLIS THAT MEY COME TO THE KYNG BY OUER MYGHTYE SUBGETTES." The OCR gives "GUER"; the Critical Notes (p. 162, PDF 190) record variants only for "Here he shewith", and Plummer's gloss is "the perils of over-mighty subjects" (p. 66, PDF 94). Close the flag against p. 127. The spelling "ovur mighty subgiettes" in the project's task notes is *not* Plummer's — "subgiettes" occurs only in the Yelverton Articles of 1470 (App. B, p. 349, PDF 377), never with "myghty".

**Chapter 5 §VI — "The remedy that works is the one that changes what the centre can afford that its subjects cannot."** Fortescue says exactly this, and it is the treatise's spine: "yff þe kynge myght haue is livelod ffor the sustenance off his estate in grete lordshippes, manors, ffee ffermys, and such other demaynes, his people not charged, he shulde kepe to hym hollych thair hertes, excede in lordshippes all the lordes off his reaume, and ther shulde non off hem growe to be like vnto hym, wich thynge is most to be fered off all þe worlde" (p. 133, PDF 161). The manuscript's sentence can carry a T1 pin.

**Chapter 5 §VI — power follows the ability to pay (DEFEND tier, in a fifteenth-century voice).** "his subgettes woll rather goo with a lorde þat is riche, and mey pay thair wages and expenses, then with thair kynge þat hath noght in his purse" (p. 119, PDF 147). Plummer on Henry IV: "weak most of all through his want of money" (p. 5, PDF 33); and the song he prints twice, "So pore a kyng was never seene, / Nor richere lordes all bydene" (p. 17, PDF 45; p. 293, PDF 321).

**Chapter 5 §I — hollow sovereignty as authority without executive capacity.** Plummer, quoting Stubbs: "'Constitutional progress had outrun administrative order'" (p. 3, PDF 31).

**Chapter 3 §II — Plummer coined "bastard feudalism" in 1885, as a slur.** The reign of Edward III "saw the beginning of that bastard feudalism, which, in place of the primitive relation of a lord to his tenants, surrounded the great man with a horde of retainers, who wore his livery and fought his battles" (p. 15, PDF 43); the whole order is "a sort of ignoble caricature of the feudal system … a hierarchy of corruption" (p. 25, PDF 53).

**Chapter 11 §II — the Tudor machine "inherited and enlarged, not invented".** On Edward IV and Henry VII: "Common to both kings were the desire to be financially independent of parliament, and many of the means which they took to secure that object" (p. 38, PDF 66); the people "attained the wish they had so often expressed, that the king should live of his own; with the result … that the voice of the nation was silenced" (p. 39, PDF 67). Crown lands "largely increased under Edward IV by the Lancastrian forfeitures … to a still greater extent under the Tudors" (p. 277, PDF 305); four Yorkist resumption acts, 1461–83, that of 1467 "ut rex haberet unde viveret" (p. 281, PDF 309). Dated T2; Wolffe supersedes on figures.

**Chapter 11 §II — the council as the machine's organ.** "Of the character of the council under Edward IV we know, owing to the absence of documents, hardly anything; and therefore we cannot say how far he acted on Fortescue's advice … It is when we come to Tudor times that we see Fortescue's system in full working order … The increase of the power of the Crown was precisely the result which Fortescue most probably intended" (pp. 295–6, PDF 323–4). Offices: "in Henry the VIII's time the system which Fortescue wished to see established of direct appointment to all offices by the Crown had become a reality" (p. 329, PDF 357).

## 3. What it CORRECTS

Nothing in the manuscript is wrong by this book's account. Three precisions:

- Manuscript (Chapter 5 §I) dates the treatise "in the 1470s"; Plummer's table gives "1471–1476" (p. xxiii, PDF 27), arguing (pp. 94–6, PDF 122–4) from ch. X's "this lande" that it was written in England after the pardon of October 1471, with "kyng Edward the iiij" (p. 155, PDF 183) the original reading. "c. 1471–75" in the project notes should read "c. 1471–76".
- Manuscript (Chapter 5 §I) has McFarlane inverting "Fortescue's warning". The intermediate link is Plummer: the quotation-marked modern phrase "'over-mighty subjects'" is his (p. 14, PDF 42), sixty years before McFarlane. Fortescue's heading → Plummer's gloss → McFarlane's inversion; §I should say so.
- Manuscript (Chapter 3 §II, Chapter 11 §I) presents Henry VII's 1504 licence-to-retain as "the sequence's destination". Plummer (p. 337, PDF 365) shows the formula fifty years earlier: York as Protector could "geve the king's liverey of colers to iiijxx gentillmen … they and everich of thaim to be sworn to be afeed with no man but with the king withoute his speciall licence" (P.P.C. vi. 209, 1454–5). The 1504 statute generalised an existing instrument; the paperwork is Lancastrian.

## 4. What it ADDS

**(a) Ch05 §I / ch12 §IV — the criterion behind the coinage.** The "over-mighty citizen" should hang on "a subgett equepolent to hym selff" (p. 130, PDF 158): equipollence, not wealth, is Fortescue's test — great lords may grow "off livelod and poiar like a kyng; wich shalbe right god ffor the lande while thai aspire to non hygher estate" (p. 130). Wealth tolerated, parity of power not: ch12's "title left, discretion severed" in its original.

**(b) Ch12 §IV — Fortescue's remedy is regulation of the switch, not seizure of the property.** He never proposes confiscation. His instruments are: resumption of *the king's own* grants (chs. XI, XIV, XIX); a crown monopoly of the lever of local power — "Ffor the myght off þe lande, aftir the myght off þe grete lordes þeroff, stondith most in þe kynges officers" (pp. 150–1, PDF 178–9), so that "nor ij. the gretteste lordes off Englond mey make so gret a myghte as þe kynge mey haue only off his officers, yff thai were holliche and only is seruantes" (p. 152, PDF 180); an oath severing dual allegiance — "þat no man haue any office of þe kynges yefte, but he be ffirst sworne that he is seruant to non oþer man" (p. 153, PDF 181); and a licence over accumulation — "Ner thai myght growe soche be mariages, but yff the kyng wolde hit … ther shall non off his tenantes aliene livelod with owt is licence" (pp. 133–4, PDF 161–2). Plummer's summary is at pp. 30–1 (PDF 58–9). This is the medieval parallel ch12 reaches for when it says the American method leaves the fortune and severs the switch; it is a parallel of *instrument*, to be marked as illustration, not mechanism.

**(c) Ch11 §II — Fortescue as the programme, stated with Plummer's own caution.** Use p. 87 ("prepares the way, however unconsciously") and pp. 295–6 as the T2 bridge; use Fortescue's "newe ffundacion of is crowne" (p. 154, PDF 182) — the inalienable endowment as "a collage, in whiche shul syng and pray for euermore al the men of Ingland" (p. 155, PDF 183) — as the primary. Plummer's "the Crown should gradually absorb the great lordships, as was being done on a larger scale by Louis XI in France" (p. 270, PDF 298) gives ch11 its cross-Channel sentence. Limit: no document shows Edward IV acting on the treatise; the claim is that Yorkist–Tudor practice matches it, not that it was executed from it.

**(d) Ch06 §VI / ch10 — the English post-mortem has no artillery in it.** The treatise budgets the king's ordinary charges in full — household, wardrobe, officers and courts, the marches, Calais, the works, and "grete and myghty vessels, ffor the brekynge off an armye" at sea (pp. 121–3, PDF 149–51) — and the extraordinary charges (ch. VII). There is no line for guns; "gonnes", "ordenaunce" and "artillery" do not occur in Fortescue's text. The only cannon in the volume is in Plummer's note, killing James II "by the bursting of a cannon at the siege of Roxburgh" in 1460 (p. 257, PDF 285). Twenty years after Castillon the most systematic English analysis of the crown's weakness is a treatise on land revenue and council reform. This serves spine §8(e)3 and (e)6 with a primary of the first rank — and sharpens it, see §5.

**(e) Ch10 §III — a hostile English witness to the French fiscal-military state.** The French king "toke vpon hym to sett tayles and oþer imposicions vpon the commons with owt the assent of the iij estates … the Ffrench kynges haue yerely sithyn sette such charges vpon them, and so augmented the same charges, as the same commons be so impouerysshid and distroyed, þat thai mowe vnneth leve" (p. 114, PDF 142); the crown "is compellid to make his armeys and retenues for the defence of his lande of straungers, as Scottes, Spaynardes, Arrogoners, men of Almeyn … for he hath no defence of his owne except is castels and fortresses. Lo this is the frute of his jus regale" (p. 115, PDF 143). Set beside "the universal spider": Commynes from inside, Fortescue from the shore. Plummer's figures (taille 1,800,000 livres and 1,700 men-at-arms at Charles VII's death; 4,700,000 livres, 5,000 men-at-arms and 25,000 permanent infantry at Louis XI's; francs-archers abolished after Guinegate 1479 — p. 197, PDF 225) are T3-grade, corroboration only.

**(f) Ch07 §III — Fortescue on rising.** "nothyng mey make is people to arise, but lakke off gode, or lakke off justice. But yet sertanly when thay lakke gode thai woll aryse, sayng that thai lakke justice" (p. 140, PDF 168); Plummer pairs it with Gascoigne on Cade, "quia justicia defecit … item, propter penuriam magnam" (p. 284, PDF 312).

**(g) Ch11 §II** — "the Tudor axe had more to do with the extinction of the old nobility than Plantagenet dissensions" (Oliphant, cited p. 36 n., PDF 64); a pointer only, to be re-pinned to modern work.

## 5. What it CONTRADICTS

**(i) Fortescue is the longbow theorist, and he denies the mechanism.** The man who saw the taille and the standing army judged them the *destruction* of French power: "al be it that the Ffrench kynges reuenues ben by suche meanes moche gratter than be the revenues wich þe kynge owre souerayn lorde hath off vs, yet thai ben not goodly taken, and the myght of his reaume is nerehande distroyed therby" (p. 117, PDF 145). England's strength "stondith most vppon archers, wich be no ryche men" (p. 137, PDF 165); making the commons poor "shalbe þe distruccion of the grettest myght off owre reaume" (p. 138, PDF 166); the French king "hath no defence of his owne except is castels and fortresses" (p. 115). This is not the ignorance spine §8(e)6 posits ("they did not understand the hidden forces behind the artillery — the fiscal apparatus"). Fortescue understood the fiscal apparatus precisely and rejected it on constitutional and military grounds. What he did not see was the ratchet: the castles and fortresses he dismisses as no defence were exactly what the taille-funded train had just taken from him in Normandy and Gascony.

*The book's best answer:* Fortescue is the governing metaphor in person — the people's weapon, "democratised, cheap, effective en masse", proposed as decisive by the defeated side's chief justice; his instrument in French dress, the francs-archers, was abolished as useless in 1480 (Plummer, p. 197). Quote him as the strongest contemporary statement of the longbow fallacy and let 1449–53 refute him. The answer is good, but it costs (e)6 a rewording: the defeated did not fail to see the fiscal machine; they saw it, called it tyranny, and failed to see what it bought.

**(ii) Plummer's account of the English consolidation is contingent, not mechanical.** "Had the civil war been terminated by a conscientious adherence to the agreement made in the Parliament of 1460, there would not have been that weakening of the old nobility, that weariness of anarchy on the part of the people, which gave Edward IV and the Tudors the opportunity they enjoyed of establishing a despotism" (p. 36, PDF 64). The New Monarchy here is born of exhaustion, forfeiture and dynastic accident; no weapon enters. And Fortescue's own mechanism — out-endow the greatest subject — is a domestic law requiring no artillery; Plummer reads Louis XI's absorption of the fiefs the same way (p. 270). This reaches the DEFEND-tier claim as applied to England.

*The book's best answer:* spine §7 already casts England as the latecomer and ch05 §VII as "the control experiment"; the mechanism claims that a polity wishing to be a power after 1453 had no alternative to the settlement, not that every settlement was triggered by guns. Fortescue supplies the *endowment* half without the *artillery* half; the book adds the ratchet that made his endowment insufficient and the French version irreversible. The answer holds only if ch11 does not call Fortescue "the programme Henry VII executed" without saying that the programme was pre-artillery in conception and that the Tudor ordnance establishment is not in it.

**(iii) Plummer carries the "permanent taille at Orléans 1439" tradition that spine §8(a) has overruled.** "This refers to the establishment of the standing army and of the permanent taille at the Estates-general of Orleans in 1439 … the deliberate act of the Estates themselves, whereby they committed political suicide" (p. 195, PDF 223). Do not cite this note as authority; it is the older Picot–Martin line. Fortescue is nearer the ruling than his editor: permanence as practice — the king "toke vpon hym … and … yerely sithyn" (p. 114) — not as a grant.

**(iv) Register.** Plummer moralises (Edward IV "probably the worst king, and certainly the worst man, who had occupied the English throne since John", p. 40, PDF 68). Quote him for coinages and constitutional judgements, never for character.

## 7. Verdict

The book changes neither direction nor register; it adds weight and one correction of nuance. Fortescue supplies the mechanism's fifteenth-century voice — power goes "with hym þat best mey susteyne and rewarde ham" — and the exact instrument-set ch12 needs for regulation-not-seizure, while Plummer supplies the two coinages the manuscript already leans on with pins. The single most important thing to know: Fortescue is the longbow theorist, an Englishman who understood the French fiscal machine perfectly and called it the ruin of France in a treatise that never mentions a gun — so spine §8(e)6 should say the defeated *saw* the fiscal apparatus and rejected it, not that they failed to see it, and ch11 must not call his programme the one Henry VII executed without adding that the artillery, which made the endowment insufficient and the settlement irreversible, is the one thing it does not contain.
