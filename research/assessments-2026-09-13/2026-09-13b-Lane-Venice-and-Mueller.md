# Assessment — Lane, *Venice: A Maritime Republic* (1973) and Lane & Mueller, *Money and Banking in Medieval and Renaissance Venice*, vol. I: *Coins and Moneys of Account* (1985)

Pins: "PDF p. N / printed p. M". Lane-Venice: PDF = printed + 22 throughout. Lane-Mueller: PDF = printed + 20. Both are pdftotext OCR of scans. Lane-Venice is clean in body text, garbled in running heads, marginal section labels and map pages. Lane-Mueller is rougher ("moncy" for money, mangled footnote numerals, illustration pages as noise at PDF pp. 2–3, 21, 103, 236); body text is readable. Quotations are verbatim except for the correction of obvious OCR slips.

**What was read.** Lane-Venice: chs 9 (Serrata and the Council of Ten, printed pp. 109–17), 11 (finance and the public debt, pp. 146–52), 13–14 (the Genoese wars and Chioggia finance, pp. 172–201), 16–17 (the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, pp. 224–49), 18 (the constitution, pp. 250–73), 23 (Finance and Income from Power, pp. 322–35), 25 (The War Fleets and the Arsenal, pp. 354–75), plus pp. 98–99 and 162–64. Not read: chs 1–8, 10, 15, 19–22, 24, 26–30. Lane-Mueller: preface, ch. 7 (pp. 90–101), ch. 11 (Communal Revenue, pp. 186–203), ch. 12 §iv (pp. 233–35), ch. 21 (pp. 485–91), and pp. 397 and 410. Not read, as instructed: the numismatic chapters 8–10 and 13–20 and appendices A–G.

**A structural finding that governs the retrieval list.** The task asked for Lane & Mueller's chapters on the public debt, the Monte Vecchio and bank money. They are not in this volume. The preface says so: "Volume two developes the themes of banking and foreign exchange in Venice from the origins" (PDF p. 14 / printed p. xiv); the public debt appears in vol. I only in passing (e.g. PDF p. 417 / printed p. 397). The work the manuscript's [GAP] in Chapter 4 §VI actually needs is Mueller's vol. II, *The Venetian Money Market: Banks, Panics, and the Public Debt, 1200–1500* (1997), and behind it Luzzatto's *I prestiti della Repubblica di Venezia* (1929), cited by Lane & Mueller as PRV. Both belong on the ch04 and ch10 RETRIEVAL LISTs. What vol. I does supply — the fiscal weight of the mint, the governing institutions, the Ten's seizure of coinage in 1472 — is set out below.

## 1. The books in two paragraphs

Lane's *Venice* is a one-volume history whose declared centre is maritime and economic and whose governing theme is institutional survival: "Venice alone survived with independence while perpetuating republican institutions" (PDF p. 273 / printed p. 251). Its fifteenth-century argument is that the turn to the mainland committed a city-state to a contest it was not built to win: "much larger states — units of power with which a city-state such as Venice could not in the long run compete — were being solidified by Renaissance monarchs" (PDF p. 263 / printed p. 241); and on the cause, "clearly Venice failed to make the many institutional changes that were called for in order to exercise the power to which she aspired. One weakness was her dependence on the condottieri" (PDF pp. 270–71 / printed pp. 248–49). Lane is direct that the political form was the limit: "Administration by boards of elected officials limited Venice's development of bureaucratic structures like those employed by contemporary Renaissance monarchs" (PDF p. 288 / printed p. 266).

Lane & Mueller vol. I is a monetary history whose thesis, for this book's purposes, is the contrast between merchant-republican and feudal-monarchical public finance. Its epigraph is a Senate decree of 1472: "the moneys of our dominions are the sinews, nay even the soul, of this republic" (PDF p. 13 / printed p. xiii). Its fiscal chapter opens: "The contrast between its budget and that of a feudal monarch is extreme. King Philip the Fair (1285–1314), who extracted more monetary revenue from France than any previous ruler, 'made enormous profits from his mints, probably more than he made from general taxation.' The amount of revenue that the Venetian Commune received from seigniorage even when the mint was very active was a small part of the government's total income" (PDF p. 206 / printed p. 186). The republic financed itself by taxing turnover and, in war, by borrowing from its own ruling class.

## 2. What they CONFIRM

**Chapter 4 §II — Venice as "the most institutionally careful buyer"; the Carmagnola/Colleoni bracket.** Lane: "On the whole, Venice was one of the most successful employers of condottieri, but she had difficulties" (PDF p. 254 / printed p. 232). Carmagnola was invited "to Venice in March 1432, to explain more fully his ideas about the feasability of various operations in the coming campaign. Although a decisive step towards his condemnation had already been taken in the Senate, no word of it leaked out"; "A month later, after a formal hearing of evidence he was beheaded in a public ceremony between the two columns of the Piazzetta" (same page). Lane's verdict is the chapter's point: "Handling a somewhat similar situation with more legality and efficiency added much to Venice's prestige." The bronze: the government "commissioned the statue by Verrocchio and erected it, not in the Piazza San Marco, but in front of the hospital or Scuola di San Marco… such glorification of any individual person was not permitted at the center of government" (PDF p. 255 / printed p. 233) — a detail the draft should use.

**Chapter 4 §III — Sforza, from the buyer's side.** "The most successful of all the Italian condottieri was Francesco Sforza, who blocked Venice's ambitions by making himself Duke of Milan… he used these contracts to accumulate army, income, and lands so as to become himself a member of the state system" (PDF p. 256 / printed p. 234). Lane dates the "fiscally weak central authority" from Venice's own conduct: the alliance with the Milanese republic was "conditional on Milan's cession of some cities, Lodi and Piacenza… That enraged many Milanese" (same page).

**Chapter 4 §VI — the funded debt of 1262.** "Venice strengthened the state by its consolidation in 1262 of all outstanding loans into what was later called the Monte Vecchio, on which it paid only 5 percent… Venice was the first European state to fund its debt so that interest was regularly paid from one fund to all bondholders equally" (PDF p. 172 / printed p. 150). The stakeholder point is Lane's: "most of the Monte Vecchio belonged to Venice's upper class and the charities they endowed" (same page).

**Chapter 4 §VI — "the state had first to *see*."** The parish capi "made tax assessments for forced loans and registered all the adult males of their district" (PDF p. 121 / printed p. 99); "in 1463, assessments were made more equitable. A cadaster was drawn up, listing all of the city's real estate, building by building, and its value" (PDF p. 260 / printed p. 238).

**Chapter 4 §VI — the Arsenal.** "the largest industrial establishment of the age" (PDF p. 274 / printed p. 252); "the biggest industrial establishment in all Christendom, perhaps the biggest in the world" (PDF p. 384 / printed p. 362). The assembly-line claim is carried in calibrated form: "Some features of the Arsenal's organization anticipated such characteristics of modern industry as an assembly line, interchangeable parts, and vertical integration" (same page).

**Chapter 4 §VI–VII — consolidation as Lane's own frame.** "the instruments of state-building — administrative and judicial bureaucracies backed by armies and taxation — were in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries creating larger and more centralized units. The consolidation of all northern Italy became a distant possibility" (PDF p. 248 / printed p. 226).

**Chapter 8 §IV — the Ten as suppressor of private force.** "It was on the look-out to suppress not only any possibility of armed insurrection, but also any noble acting as if above the law… They permitted no organized opposition" (PDF p. 139 / printed p. 117); its first business was the exiles — "Partly by leniency but mainly by efficiency they eliminated Venetian fuorusciti" (same page).

**Chapter 10 §VIII / Chapter 12 §IV — the scale race lost.** "the fleets Venice put to sea in 1538 and 1571 were four or five times as large as those manned in the 1420's… Much as Venice had increased her own war fleets, she had not been able to keep up" (PDF p. 270 / printed p. 248).

## 3. What they CORRECT

1. **Manuscript (Chapter 4 §VI) says the Arsenal was "some forty-five hectares, a workforce around sixteen thousand at peak".** Lane p. 362 (PDF p. 384): "the Arsenal, which then covered 60 acres, employed on an average about 2,000 men within its wall — as many as 3,000 in emergencies, and hardly ever less than 1,000." Sixty acres is about twenty-four hectares; the sixteen thousand is not Lane's and should go unless another T2 source carries it. Replace the "galley-a-day" parenthesis with Lane's dated surge: "the Arsenal turned out 100 galleys within two months in the spring of 1570" (PDF p. 386 / printed p. 364).

2. **Manuscript (Chapter 4 §VI) says the 1262 debt paid "five per cent semi-annually".** Lane p. 150: five per cent a year, "its payment of 2-1/5 percent every half year" (OCR for 2½). Write "five per cent, paid half-yearly".

3. **Manuscript (Chapter 4 §VI) says "forced loans on the wealthy".** Lane p. 150: "All Venetians having a minimal amount of property were required to subscribe proportionally"; the 1379 list took in "those with known property worth more than 300 ducats… 2,128 persons… about one-eighth of all heads of households" (PDF p. 173 / printed p. 151). "The propertied" is exact.

4. **Manuscript (Chapter 4 §VI) says the Ten was "permanent within a generation".** Lane gives no date: the Ten "almost lapsed, but gradually it built a permanent place for itself" (PDF p. 139 / printed p. 117). The conventional 1334 must come from elsewhere or be dropped.

5. **Manuscript (Chapter 4 §VI): the bocche di leone and the destruction of anonymous denunciations.** Neither is anywhere in Lane (searched: denunciation, anonymous, bocca, lion). The [GAP] cannot be closed from Lane; re-point it to Chambers & Pullan or cut.

6. **Manuscript (Chapter 4 §II) says Carmagnola was "tried in secret".** Lane: the arrest was by deception, the trial "a formal hearing of evidence", the execution "a public ceremony" (PDF p. 254 / printed p. 232). Lane does not give 5 May.

7. **Manuscript (Chapter 4 §II) says Colleoni was "made captain-general for life (1455)".** Not in Lane; Lane carries instead the fact in §5(iv). Pin the date elsewhere or drop it.

## 4. What they ADD

**Chapter 10 §VI — the price of thirty years' war, in a republic's books.** Mocenigo, 1423: the debt, "After being raised to almost 10 million ducats by the wars at the beginning of the century… had been reduced during his dogeship to 6 million ducats"; and of Foscari, "if you make him doge, which God forbid, you will shortly be at war; and whoever has 10,000 ducats, you will find yourself with only 1,000" (PDF p. 251 / printed p. 229). The sequel: "In 1423, bonds of the Monte Vecchio had paid 4 percent and sold at 66… by 1474, they were paying only 1 percent irregularly and selling at 13"; "As the price of the bonds fell, the levies of forced loans became practically equivalent to direct property taxes. Those taxed refused to pay… The Senate was already gradually shifting to direct taxes when the Turkish conquest of Constantinople in 1453, in the midst of the Sforza war, created an atmosphere in which it could vote sweeping, drastic direct levies on income" (PDF p. 260 / printed p. 238). War cost → collapse of the old instrument → direct tax → cadaster (1463) → Monte Nuovo (1482), "bought voluntarily by both Venetian and foreign investors" (same page). The manuscript's chain, run in a second polity — with the disanalogy in §5(ii) stated.

**Chapter 10 / Appendix B — the budget of c. 1500.** Lane's table (PDF p. 259 / printed p. 237): receipts 1,150,000 ducats, of which mainland cities 330,000 and direct taxes 160,000; ordinary expenses 530,000, of which interest on bonds 155,000; "Available for extraordinary expenses (wars) 620,000". "Two of the largest items of receipts had been absent a century or two earlier: the payments from the mainland cities and the direct taxes collected in Venice" (same page). Lane & Mueller: revenue in 1469 "totaled about 1 million ducats, only a little more than half came from the empire and direct taxation combined"; the "8 dazi" yielded "about 235,000 ducats yearly"; the mint, in the 1490 budget, "only 2,700 ducats" (PDF p. 207 / printed p. 187).

**Chapter 12 §IV — the EU as the Venice pattern, in Lane's terms.** On the collegial executive: inner-circle terms were "so staggered that some member of the inner circle left it each month only to reenter it immediately a few months later… it was planned that way, so as to avoid concentrations of power in single individuals" (PDF p. 279 / printed p. 257). On its limit: the Savii and Chiefs "lacked the clear-cut powers of the chiefs of a bureaucracy. They had no ministries directly under them" (PDF p. 289 / printed p. 267). On the strategy the form dictates: "On sea as on land, she relied on playing the balance of power" (PDF p. 268 / printed p. 246). And the Venetian answer to the Depreter test: "She did not develop a hierarchy of real professionals supported by a naval bureaucracy… In a negative sense at least, changes within the internal structure of the republic were quite as crucial as any weavings of diplomacy or any vicissitudes of battle in determining Venice's future" (PDF p. 271 / printed p. 249). For the mainland domain: after Agnadello, "The complete lack of any national feeling within Venice's mainland domain was revealed by the way this one defeat caused its immediate collapse" (PDF p. 265 / printed p. 243).

**Chapter 10 §VI — a fourth counter-case, the inverse of Burgundy.** Burgundy had the apparatus and one mortal node. Venice had a centre nothing could kill — Foscari "was deposed in 1457 by vote of the Ducal Councillors and the Ten" and the system carried on (PDF p. 289 / printed p. 267); after Agnadello the Great Council reversed a defeated war tax, "700 noes to 650 in favor", to "864 to 494 noes" on the doge's plea (PDF p. 292 / printed p. 270) — and no consolidation followed, because "There was almost no bureaucracy, no especially trained officials named by superiors to perform specialized tasks" (PDF p. 120 / printed p. 98). Survivable centre without executive capacity: durable, not decisive. That completes the triad's proof by variation.

**Chapter 4 §V — the buyers' terms, from the sellers' side.** "There is a report of a conversation among several condottieri about their business interests. One leader urged that they all avoid winning victories for Venice because, if she became any stronger, she would be able to impose peace on Italy and put them all out of work" (PDF p. 256 / printed p. 234).

**Chapter 8 — plural enforcement inside one state.** Lane & Mueller: "A decree of 1338 restricting the sale of silver named twelve different magistracies as enforcement agencies eligible to receive a third of the fines they inflicted" (PDF p. 119 / printed p. 99); and a caution the chapter could adopt: "'was forbidden' should not be taken by the reader to mean that there is good evidence that the law was enforced" (PDF p. 120 / printed p. 100).

**Chapter 4 §VI / Chapter 11 §II — the Ten and the money.** "the Council of Ten in 1472 asserted unchallenged responsibility for regulating coinage and for administration of the mint" (Lane & Mueller, PDF p. 117 / printed p. 97). Venetian venality as the fiscal move in republican dress: in 1509 "To men whom it approved and who put down 2,000 ducats, the Council of Ten granted the title and toga of Senator" (PDF p. 285 / printed p. 263); lesser posts sold in 1510 for "eight to ten times their salaries" (PDF p. 288 / printed p. 266). Priuli on the Monte Nuovo at 40: the bonds "'are based on air,' he wrote in his diary, 'and are mere books, namely, paper and ink'" (PDF p. 347 / printed p. 325).

**Memorable material.** "uomo morto non fa guerra" — "dead men wage no wars" — on the strangling of the Carrara (PDF p. 249 / printed p. 227); the Turkish fleet of 1470, "a forest on the sea, when described incredible but when seen stupefying" (PDF p. 380 / printed p. 358).

## 5. What they CONTRADICT

**(i) "Venice became the proto-state itself" (Chapter 4 §VII) — at full strength, Lane denies it.** "those most nearly successful were, except for Venice, monarchies" (PDF p. 273 / printed p. 251). The Arsenal, mint, grain office and tax collection "were one after another assigned to elected committees of from three to six nobles who held office for only a few years at most and were not eligible to succeed themselves" (PDF p. 120 / printed p. 98). The secretaries "provided material for a bureaucracy, [but] they were not under centralized bureaucratic control" (PDF p. 288 / printed p. 266). The manuscript's §VI calls surveillance, state manufacturing and funded debt "every apparatus the modern literature treats as the signature of the twentieth-century state"; Lane's Venice has the instruments and refuses the form. The DEFEND-tier chain (spine §6) runs cannon → fiscal-administrative discipline → consolidation; Venice ran finance and industry without the administrative step and consolidated nothing beyond its own class.

*The book's best answer.* Drop "proto-state" and keep the narrower claim §VI makes — that the instruments arose in a republic out of commerce — then use Lane against the objection: Venice isolates the administrative leg of the triad. It had capital, credit and an arsenal, and by Lane's verdict "could not in the long run compete" because it "failed to make the many institutional changes that were called for". That is the mechanism confirmed by a polity that stopped two steps in. The answer is good and improves the chapter; it costs the §VII sentence.

**(ii) The Venetian fisc was driven by wars, not by cannon.** Nowhere in the chapters read does Lane attribute the fiscal transformation of 1423–63 to artillery. The cost that broke the Monte Vecchio was "thirty years of almost constant warfare in Lombardy, becoming every year more costly" (PDF p. 251 / printed p. 229), fought by hired companies; the direct tax was voted under the shock of 1453; and Lane's one sustained artillery passage makes the Turks the innovators — Mehmed "was ahead of the Venetians in developing artillery both on land and sea" (PDF p. 378 / printed p. 356). Priuli's complaint after 1509 located the drain in men: mercenary pay "was pure loss… soldiers took the money away, whereas what was paid out for the fleet came back because the recipients spent it in Venice" (PDF p. 270 / printed p. 248).

*The book's best answer.* This is the Swiss lesson from the other side: Venice paid a permanent price for force without a technology that compelled institution-building, and got the fisc without the ratchet — direct taxes "were still thought of as essentially war taxes and were lightly levied in peacetime", falling from "about one fourth of the government's income" c. 1500 to "only one tenth" by 1600 (PDF p. 347 / printed p. 325). Ruling §8(a) is untouched, since Venice is not the French case; but the chapter must not let Venice read as artillery-driven. Good, provided the disanalogy is written into the text.

**(iii) Venetian decisive force was limited by men, not capital.** "Not lack of ships but lack of men limited Venetian sea power… Her Arsenal gave her superiority in industrial organization, but it produced more galleys than Venice could find crews for. At Lepanto, the Venetian galleys were so inadequately manned that they accepted on board detachments of Spanish soldiers" (PDF p. 386 / printed p. 364). Chapter 10's thesis is that the binding constraint on decisive capability is what it costs; Lane's Venice could pay and could build, and could not man.

*The book's best answer.* Chapter 10 §VIII already concedes that the modern base cannot be bought by revenue alone; Venice sharpens it — the capital-intensive layer is necessary, and the labour and legitimacy layers can still fail it. Adequate rather than good: the price framing stands, but price is not the only constraint, and the chapter should say so.

**(iv) "Colleoni, the loyal one" (Chapter 4 §II and §V).** Lane: in 1467 Milan, Florence and Naples allied against Venice "because Venice refused to join them in curbing Venice's old condottiere, Bartolomeo Colleoni, who was no longer in Venetian pay but acting, Venice protested, on his own and with help from Florentine exiles… He tried to carve out a principality for himself in the Romagna. The hostile Italian coalition dissolved only after the 75-year-old Colleoni had been bought off" (PDF p. 258 / printed p. 236). At his death the republic seized much of his estate, "as soon as he was dead and his army and skill was no longer to be feared" (PDF p. 255 / printed p. 233). His loyalty, in Lane's exact phrase, was that he "had never treacherously broken a contract with the Venetian Republic" (same page).

*The book's best answer.* Used honestly this strengthens the chapter: "Colleoni's bargain" is contractual fidelity to the paymaster plus a licensed freelance ambition elsewhere, ending in sequestration and a statue — nearer the American venture-defence case than "tenure and bronze for the loyal" allows. Good answer; the draft is wrong as written.

**(v) Market discipline did not produce reliable force.** Agnadello: an army "which was the biggest and finest that had yet been collected by an Italian state… was crushed on May 14, 1509, and the whole Venetian army scattered in rout" (PDF p. 265 / printed p. 243); Priuli was, in Lane's paraphrase, as bitter as Machiavelli against mercenaries who took the money and then ran (PDF p. 266 / printed p. 244). §II's "domesticated" survives in its precise sense — the buyers were not devoured — but domestication is not reliability.

*The book's best answer.* Say exactly that, and hand reliability to Part II. Good answer.

None of these reaches a §8 ruling directly; (i) and (ii) bear on the DEFEND-tier mechanism by supplying a polity that ran part of it, and both are answered by naming Venice a control case rather than an exemplar.

## 7. Verdict

Neither book changes the argument's direction; together they change Venice's register within it — from an exemplar of state-form phenomena to a control case that isolates the administrative leg of the triad, and, for Chapter 12, from an analogy to a documented pattern for the collegial bloc. Their weight is considerable for Chapter 4 (three factual corrections, one large), Chapter 10 (a fourth counter-case with dated fiscal evidence) and Chapter 12 §IV (Lane's own sentences on why a rich, durable, collegial polity could not compete with fiscal-military monarchies). The single most important thing the author should know: Lane's Venice had the money, the Arsenal and the funded debt and, by Lane's explicit verdict, refused the bureaucracy and the executive — so "Venice became the proto-state itself" must go, and Venice belongs beside Burgundy and the Swiss as a polity that ran the mechanism two steps in and stopped.
