# Assessment — M. G. A. Vale, *Charles VII* (Berkeley and Los Angeles: University of California Press, 1974)

Text: /home/claude/books/CharlesVII.txt (pdftotext; ~123k words; read in full — preface, chs. 1–7, conclusion, table of events, glossary; bibliography and index skimmed). Printed page = PDF page minus 12 in ch. 1, rising by steps to minus 22 from ch. 6 as plates intervene; pins give printed page, then PDF page. Body text is clean; footnotes are heavily garbled and the genealogical chart (PDF 18–19) unreadable. This is the 1974 biography, not *English Gascony* (1970) nor the TRHS article the manuscript cites as "Vale 1969"; keep the three apart in the apparatus.

## 1. The book in two paragraphs

Vale writes a study of personal monarchy: "This book is not a conventional biography … It takes a contemporary assessment of Charles VII as both a king and as a man for its starting-point" (p. ix / PDF 9) — Chastellain's assessment: mutability, distrust, envy, and the craft of playing faction against faction. His governing claim is a check on the "new monarchy" tradition: "To attribute the creation of that form of government known subsequently as the *ancien régime* to the reign of Charles VII would give those forty years too great a burden to bear … the reforms and innovations in justice, taxation and the army were not totally unprecedented. The means had often been extant in the past, but the power had not. Hence a study of the reign of Charles VII must essentially be a study of power — its justification, its delegation, and its direct, personal exercise" (p. 12 / PDF 24). "The conventional picture of France as a nation destined for 'centralisation' under an absolute monarch is not a very convincing one" (p. 18 / PDF 30).

The conclusion is unsparing: "If he established peace, it was peace at a price. That price was taxation which was heavy, and which got heavier, war or no war" (p. 231 / PDF 255). The reign's tools, as Vale reconstructs them, are bribery, pensions, spies, treason trials and ceremony; the standing army is a cost and a hazard. The book declines to "re-tell the story" of the reconquest (p. 121 / PDF 139) and is almost silent on Castillon; its value here lies in the Praguerie, the Estates, the fisc under strain, and the court in which Jean Bureau sat.

## 2. What it CONFIRMS

**The 1439 ordonnance as the constitution of force, not of taxation (ch10 §III; spine §8(a) ruling of 2 September).** Manuscript: "What the ordonnance of 2 November 1439 … verifiably changed was the constitution of force." Vale: "the military *ordonnance* of 2 November, in which no one but the king was permitted to raise troops" (p. 76 / PDF 92). Vale carries no taille grant at Orléans; the Estates there appear only as the assembly Jean Juvenal complained the king ignored (p. 82 / PDF 98). The ruling survives Vale intact. Better, Vale prints the king's terms at Montferrand in May 1440 as "a restatement of the notions which had produced the military *ordonnance* of 1439": "all the war of the said kingdom belongs to the king and his officers, and to none other, and there is no one so great in the said kingdom that he can levy war nor retain troops, without the authority, commission and command of the king" (p. 80 / PDF 96; Escouchy iii, 18). The manuscript does not yet have this clause.

**Permanence made in practice, without the Estates (ch10 §III).** Manuscript: "It was simply not consulted, year after year." Vale: "In 1442 he told the Estates of Languedoc that he 'did not wish such assemblies to meet in future'. They were expensive, they burdened the people, and the *tailles* and *aides* for the war were granted without their convocation" (p. 82 / PDF 98). "The continued existence of war taxes, used for distinctly non-military purposes, is perhaps a symptom of the king's ability to take taxes without the intervention of representative assemblies from the 1440s onwards" (p. 225 / PDF 249). "There was no Parliament in France, and therefore no Commons" (p. 219 / PDF 243).

**The standing army of 1445 (ch10 §II).** "Without a standing army until 1445, Charles VII could not rely upon assured success against the magnates" (p. 19 / PDF 33); the ordonnance "created a standing army from the ranks of the unoccupied men-at-arms, without employment because of the truce" (p. 104 / PDF 122).

**Bureau as counsellor (ch06 §VI).** The February 1445 cipher-key names "five men who were often with the king 'in his retreat'", among them "Master Jean Bureau" (p. 98 / PDF 114); at the accession "Louis dismissed all of them except Charles of Anjou and Jean Bureau" (p. 188 / PDF 210), and Gaspard, "*maître* of the artillery," kept his offices too (p. 189 n. / PDF 211).

**Talbot inside the instrument (ch06 §VI).** On the Rouen entry: "From a window above the route, the cortège was viewed by Talbot and other English hostages. They were, wrote Monstrelet, 'very pensive and down at heart'" (p. 203 / PDF 225).

**Basin as contested witness (ch06 §VI; ch10 §II).** "Basin was writing during the winter of his discontent — as an exile from France, twenty years after the events which he purported to describe" (p. 136 / PDF 156); "like his fellow-bishop Thomas Basin in 1465 … in the pay or patronage of any of the rebels" (p. 83 / PDF 99); a former servant of the double monarchy who "had much to forget" (p. 217 / PDF 241); "Lesser minds — Thomas Basin and Jean Juvenal des Ursins, hide-bound with clerical prejudice" (p. 232 / PDF 256). The draft's staging of Basin's Talbot verdict as an exile's moralising, and its tagging of the "fifteen companies" as his, are supported.

**Gascony as a market in compositions (ch06 §V).** "Bribery was sometimes an alternative, sometimes a supplement, to the use of military force. There can be little doubt of the superiority of Charles VII's army to that of his adversaries at this time. Yet brute force was tempered by subtler weapons" (p. 121 / PDF 139). Suriennes took 10,000 écus to leave Fougères; John Edwards "4,500 *l. tournois*" for La Roche-Guyon; Shorthose's pension is at p. 124 / PDF 142.

## 3. What it CORRECTS

1. **Manuscript (Chapter 7 §III; echoed ch10 §III) says the Praguerie was "settled at Cusset in July 1440" with "pardons all round, a pension for Bourbon, the government of the Dauphiné for Louis … and not one clause of the ordonnance withdrawn. The ordonnance was thereafter enforced."** Vale names no Cusset. The settlement is negotiated at Montferrand in May 1440 (p. 80 / PDF 96); "On 28 July Louis was granted the Dauphiné and a pension of 800 *livres* per month" (p. 81 / PDF 97). Pardons were not universal: "Charles refused to pardon La Trémoille, Chaumont and Prie. They were banished from court" (p. 82 / PDF 98). The pension Vale records restored is Alençon's 12,000 *livres* (p. 158 / PDF 178). On enforcement Vale is the other way: the rebels met at Blois "determined to render the military *ordonnance* a dead letter … The attempt at military reform had failed. It was not until 1445 that some semblance of order was brought to the companies" (pp. 78–9 / PDF 94–5). The roster should add Dunois — "Less explicable is the presence of Dunois among the rebels" (p. 77 / PDF 93) — and Vendôme, Lafayette and the bastards of Bourbon (p. 76 / PDF 92). "Five months" holds (p. 238 / PDF 260).

2. **Manuscript (Chapter 7 §III) says the Praguerie rose "by name, against the permanent tax."** Vale's rebels acted "under the pretext of reducing the *aides*" (the June 1440 *mémoire*, p. 77 / PDF 93) — indirect taxes, not the taille — and Bourbon's grievances were the peace talks and the withdrawal of his companies from Lorraine. Reword to "against the aides and the military ordonnance."

3. **Manuscript (Chapter 10 §III) carries "roughly 1.8 million … in 1481 the taille alone bringing in 4.6 million" from the 1911 Britannica [^10-9].** Vale gives the T1 carrier and different terms: "Commynes estimated that Charles raised about 1,800,000 francs a year. His son was raising 4,700,000 by his death" (p. 231 / PDF 255; Commynes, ed. Calmette, ii, 220). Total levy, not "taille alone"; 4.7m at Louis's death, not 4.6m in 1481. Re-pin.

4. **Manuscript (Chapter 6 §VI) says Cadillac "is the only place in the whole record taken by storm through a breach" (Escouchy ii. 64–65), with a GAP-flag on the 17/19 September discrepancy.** Vale's book takes the other side: "On 17 September the penalties of resistance to his sovereignty were made plain to the garrison of Cadillac, who were withstanding siege, although the walls of the place had been battered by his guns. On his arrival at the place, they surrendered. The captain was executed on his orders" (p. 140 / PDF 160; citing Leseur ii, 19; Escouchy ii, 56; MS fr. 32511). Battered walls, surrender on the king's arrival, no storm; and a different Escouchy pin. The GAP-flag should record that the biography carries this too; resolve at the page images before "only storming" goes to print.

5. **The memo's "27 December 1444 — Gaspard Bureau grand-master of the artillery."** No such date in Vale; Gaspard appears only as holder of the office in 1461 (p. 189 n. / PDF 211). Drop it.

6. **Manuscript (Chapter 10 §III): "not consulted, year after year."** True of Languedoïl and of any Estates General; Vale shows Languedoc's Estates still granting — the *aide* of spring 1453 "granted by the Three Estates of Languedoc at Montpellier," "justified by the king's evident necessity" (p. 127 / PDF 145). And on 21 March 1461 the dying king expected "the lords of the blood and the people of the Three Estates" with him "soon" — "The Three Estates never met before his death" (pp. 181–2 / PDF 203–4). Say Languedoïl, and note the 1461 summons that never sat.

7. **Minor.** Vale's glossary gives "20 companies … about 12,000 mounted troops" (p. 245 / PDF 267), not Basin's fifteen; do not cite Vale for fifteen.

## 4. What it ADDS

**(a) The English attack on the train and its administrator, Caen, June 1450 — ch06 §VI; spine §8(e)4.** Somerset offered five Scots of the bodyguard "4,000 écus and fifty pounds sterling if, with the aid of four Englishmen, they took one of the following: Dunois, André de Villequier, Jacques Coeur, or Jean Bureau, treasurer of France … The remaining thousand troops were to go on foot among the king's artillery, putting torches into the powder kegs and spiking the bombards" (p. 138 / PDF 158; MS Dupuy 38; Charles to James II, January 1457, Stevenson i, 346–51). A documented English intention, three years before Castillon, to strike the guns and seize the accountant of guns. It does not prove Talbot's mind, but it narrows the void the draft admits the conjecture lives in. Place beside the Formigny culverin seizure.

**(b) The post-war permanence of the cost — ch10 §III.** Charles to James II, January 1457: "the king's responsibility, both for his troops as for the costs of paying them, is no less than what it was beforehand … at present the king has to watch all the coastline daily from Spain to Picardy, which amounts to more than 450 leagues of land; in which he has continually to keep men-at-arms in great numbers … in such a way that all the revenue of Normandy … could not suffice, by 100,000 francs, to pay the men-at-arms detailed to guard the same" (p. 125 / PDF 143; Stevenson i, 341–2). "Until the peace" becoming "always," in the crown's own voice.

**(c) The fisc of 1449–53 still ran on expedients — ch10 §III–IV, in support of the co-evolution ruling.** "The war effort could not wait for the inevitable delays which attended the collection of taxation" (p. 127 / PDF 145). Coeur lent "70,680 *l. tournois* to finance the first expedition to Guyenne" (p. 129 / PDF 147) and was arrested within weeks; on 5 July 1453 the money-changers of Tours agreed his salt-cellars "should be broken up" and minted, "seeing that the king is at present at his conquest of Guyenne, and demands every day that all the money that can be found should be sent to him" (p. 130 / PDF 148). "Foreclosure had become the prerogative not of the creditor, but of the debtor" (p. 133 / PDF 153) — a disanalogy ch12 should state rather than exploit: the crown seized the property; the American method leaves title and severs discretion.

**(d) The Alençon interrogations, 1456 — taxation as the wedge; ch10 §III or ch07.** Alençon's envoy told Lord Rivers that Normandy's people "were more heavily burdened with *tailles* than they had ever been and were all weary of being in the king's obedience"; the invading English "were to abolish all taxes and issue pardons, but should reimpose taxation three or four years after the reconquest" (pp. 156–7 / PDF 176–7). Even the restorers assumed the tax would return.

**(e) The court's artillery habits — ch10 §IV.** Chastellain: the king spent an hour "with artisans, armourers, siege-engineers, bombardiers and similar people; he remembered their business and their fixed day; no one dared to put them off" (p. 137 / PDF 157). In 1449–50 the Genoese gunner Louis Giribault built, and modelled for the king at Rouen, "a gun-carriage which was not drawn by horses, thereby economising on the costs of transporting the guns from siege to siege" (p. 141 / PDF 161; Contamine 311–12).

**(f) Smaller.** The Milanese ambassadors at Nancy, 26 May 1445, had "sent a copy" of "this *ordonnance* for the reduction of men-at-arms" to Milan (p. 97 / PDF 113; Kendall and Ilardi i) — a lead on ch10 §I's "lost" text. A commission of 24 July 1453 inquired into "irregularities in the taking of the town of Castillon by his troops" (pp. 139–40 / PDF 159–60). A franc-archer at Thors: "When the king loses us, he'll lose his kingdom" (p. 150 / PDF 170).

## 5. What it CONTRADICTS

Vale's thesis is the strongest single-polity objection the central chapter has met; quote it, do not smooth it. "The means had often been extant in the past, but the power had not" (p. 12 / PDF 24). "Charles VII ruled over an essentially weak regime, in which survival was perhaps the one and only concern" (p. 230 / PDF 254). "But in some respects they were sold a lame horse. The standing army which helped to drive out the English under Charles VII could promote, rather than reduce, disorder in the country. It had to be employed, and it was best employed outside the kingdom of France. The king's subjects, though they paid their taxes, were not protected from violence by the regime. There was often too little, rather than too much, government in fifteenth-century France" (p. 231 / PDF 255). "Even after that date, as Louis XI discovered in 1465, a king could not always rely upon his own troops" (p. 19 / PDF 33). On the reconquest's motive: "There were people at court who would stand to gain from the invasion of Normandy. It is not necessary to adduce, with Beaucourt, the notion that the king 'burned with a desire to chase the English from the land'" (p. 112 / PDF 130). Vale explains the reign by persons, patronage and survival, and denies that its institutions add up to a new state. This cuts at ch10 §I ("the reign in which the assembled machine reached maturity"), §V ("the machine did what the machine was for" in 1465), and, in its strong reading, at the DEFEND-tier mechanism of spine §6 with France as the running case.

**The book's best answer.** Vale's distinction is the manuscript's own: élus, aides and taille were the "means"; what changed between 1439 and 1446 was the "power" to use them without consent, and Vale's evidence — taxes without Estates from the 1440s, pensions from the war *aides* as "one of the more effective means by which the king controlled his nobility" (p. 226 / PDF 250), a bribery that "needed money" (p. 19) — is that power at work. He measures the state against public order and personal constancy; the ratchet ruling measures it against the magnates' capacity to reverse the settlement. On the fisc that answer is good. On the army it is weaker: the draft should not let 1445 read as assured force, and ch10 §V's "failed at the exchequer" must sit beside Vale's 1465 sentence and Commynes' record that Louis had sacked the captains who won Normandy (p. 193 / PDF 215). The mechanism survives if stated as Vale's "power," not as a machine that had replaced persons — a register correction, not a retreat.

## 6. Placement

T2; rival on register, ally on facts. Engage BY NAME in ch10 §III (the Estates, pp. 82, 219, 225; the Languedoc nuance; the 1457 letter) and in ch10's critiques or §VI–VII as the "means not power" objection of §5; by name in ch07 §III for the Praguerie corrections. In ch06: the Caen plot in text beside Formigny (Stevenson i as T1, Vale as carrier); footnotes for Talbot at the Rouen window, Cadillac, the Gascon buy-outs, Basin. Footnotes in ch10 §IV for the bombardiers' hour and Giribault; Coeur one paragraph in ch10 §III; "foreclosure" in ch12 only as a stated disanalogy. Vale is no source for Castillon, Formigny or the sieges.

## 7. Verdict

Vale changes the register of Chapter 10, not the book's direction: he confirms the 2 September ruling that the settlement was made in practice across 1439–46 without an Orléans taille grant, and supplies the crown's own statements of the monopoly of force (Montferrand 1440) and of the permanence of the cost (January 1457), while denying in the strongest terms that any "machine" had matured by 1461. The Praguerie paragraph in Chapter 7 must be rewritten on his account — Montferrand not Cusset, the aides not the taille, banishments not universal pardon, an ordonnance that was a dead letter until 1445 — and the 27 December 1444 Gaspard date finds no carrier here. The single most important thing the author should take from it is the Caen plot of June 1450: the English planned to spike the bombards and seize Jean Bureau three years before Castillon, which gives the Talbot conjecture the one thing the draft says it lacks — a contemporary record that, on the English side, the train and its accountant were understood as the target.
