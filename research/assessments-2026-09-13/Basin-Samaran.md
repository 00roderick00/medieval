# Thomas Basin, *Histoire de Charles VII*, ed. and tr. Charles Samaran, tome I (Les Classiques de l'histoire de France au moyen âge, 15; Paris: Belles Lettres, 1933)

**Coverage warning, first.** The front matter is unambiguous: this is "TOME I — 1407–1444" (title page, PDF p. 7; publisher's list, printed p. [311] / PDF p. 359: "tome Ier (1407–1444). XLVIII-309 pages (1933)"). It contains Samaran's introduction (pp. v–xlviii) and Basin's Books I–III only, ending with the Tours truce of 1444 (printed p. 300 / PDF p. 350). **Every passage the task asked me to locate — Castillon and Talbot's death, the portrait of Jean Bureau, the *quindecim* companies of 1445, the reconquest of 1449–50, Formigny, Rouen 1449, and Gascony 1451–53 — lies in Books IV–V, which are in tome II (Samaran, 1944), not in this file.** I read the whole of this file (c. 103k words; OCR is clean in the running text, garbled only in the critical apparatus, the Latin running heads, and one table on pp. xxxvii–xxxviii). What follows reports what the volume does carry: Samaran's assessment of Basin's reliability, and Basin's own account of the years *before* the artillery state — which turns out to be unexpectedly useful as the "before" photograph for ch06 and ch10.

## 1. The book in two paragraphs

Basin (1412–1491), bishop of Lisieux, wrote his history in exile at Trier in 1471–72, as a Norman who had grown up under English rule, surrendered his own city to Charles VII in 1449, and then broken with Louis XI. Samaran's verdict on the witness is the one the manuscript needs to carry: Basin "a voulu être sinon le Tite Live, du moins le Salluste ou le Suétone de son temps" (p. xvii / PDF p. 19); the chapters on the English occupation and the *menu peuple* "sont parmi les plus vivants et les plus dignes de foi" (p. xviii / PDF p. 20); but the whole is "un mélange assez décevant de souvenirs personnels, de renseignements de première main, d'utilisation plus ou moins libre de chroniques", written far from France, with "les erreurs de fait et les anachronismes qui, de loin en loin, font tache dans son œuvre" (p. xviii / PDF p. 20). Samaran quotes Quicherat on the bias that matters most to this project: no one had "un attachement... si raisonné pour le régime du moyen âge... De là ses sorties contre les armées régulières, les taxes, les gens de loi" (p. xix / PDF p. 21). His lost *Conseils* to Louis XI "reposait sur la réduction de l'armée et sur celle des pensions. Il plaidait pour les contribuables" (p. xii / PDF p. 14). Basin, in short, is the fiscal-military state's most articulate contemporary opponent — which is exactly why his testimony *for* it (in tome II) weighs so heavily.

Basin's own thesis, stated in his first chapter, is the arc the manuscript's Part II describes from the other end: Charles VII inherited a kingdom "magna ex parte attritum, squalens, incultum et accolis nudatum" and left it "pacatum et quietum" (p. 8 / PDF p. 60); the cause of the whole catastrophe was the murder of Orléans in 1407, "caput et origo omnium calamitatum" (p. 84 / PDF p. 129). The book's operative refrain — Samaran calls it "comme un refrain chez Thomas Basin" (p. 195 n. 1 / PDF p. 245) — is that the French could have expelled the English at any time after 1435 "si in sua milicia disciplinam atque ordinem debitum posuissent, quemadmodum postmodum, emensis prope octo annis vel novem, ipsos fecisse constat" (p. 194–96 / PDF p. 244–46). That "eight or nine years" points forward from 1435–36 to 1444–45: the ordonnance companies, seen from the side of the disorder they ended.

## 2. What it CONFIRMS

- **Ch06 §III/§IV (Chapter 6, "the payroll can be read"; the Bureau institution and the artillery-first siege).** The manuscript's claim that by the 1440s the crown's guns were a specialist, staffed instrument is confirmed for 1441, before the reconquest, at Pontoise (III.xv). Basin: "Ad partem illam aggeres extruuntur, applicantur tormenta, petrarie admoventur maxime et validissime, quarum crebro jactu et terrifico quaciuntur muri, turres franguntur et propugnacula deiciuntur" (p. 270 / PDF p. 320); and the decisive sentence: "jactus ille assiduus creberrimusque colubrinarum ac serpentinarum vires animosque Anglicis fregit atque abstulit. Nam quociens sese ad propellendum qui scalas conscendebant obicerent, tam expertes illius artis magistros rex habebat, ut, quemcumque in muro vel turri advertere potuissent, illico sine remedio jactu lapidis necabatur" (p. 272 / PDF p. 322). Note the phrase *vires animosque*: the same walls-and-minds coupling the manuscript takes from Blondel at Harfleur (ch06 §IV), here eight years earlier and in a second, independent Latin witness. Samaran's note on the passage is the editor's own endorsement of the book's mechanism: "On sait que c'est principalement grâce aux progrès de cette arme et au soin que prit Charles VII de munir ses troupes de nombreuses bouches à feu que la Normandie et la Guyenne purent être délivrées de la domination anglaise" (p. 271 n. 1 / PDF p. 321).
- **Ch06 §V (Rogers/Curry: "preparations for bombardment... were enough to prompt surrender"; the délai de secours).** Tartas 1442 (III.xvi) is a pure relief-clause composition, with the guns nowhere: the English "pactum tamen dedicionis compulsi fecerunt, nisi ad certum et statutum diem obsessis succursum preberent", the king appeared "ante statutum diem", the English "minime ausi sunt comparere", and the garrison "dedicionem absolutam... facere coacti sunt" (p. 278 / PDF p. 328). This is the instrument the manuscript names as central, attested by Basin two years before the ordonnance companies existed.
- **Ch10 §II (the ordonnance companies replaced an army "sine certo numero et stipendio").** Basin's description of the pre-1445 host: "in milicia Francie nullus ordo, nulla disposicio, nulla disciplina secundum instituciones et precepta rei militaris servabantur neque sub numero et stipendio regulari tenebantur ; sed per turmas, sub hiis quos sibi proponerent duces, pervagantes omnes pene partes et provincias Gallie" (p. 196 / PDF p. 246); and of the écorcheurs: "Erant quippe sine certo numero et stipendio ubique vagantes per regnum armatorum turme... laniones seu scorticatores, juxta vulgi locucionem, appellabantur" (p. 236 / PDF p. 286). The manuscript's "a force that existed *between* campaigns" is precisely the negative of this.
- **Ch03 (the écorcheur economy).** Basin supplies the maxim: "Erat enim tritum tunc communi sermone velud proverbium apud ipsos « melius terram valere vastatam, quam perditam »" (p. 196 / PDF p. 246), with Samaran's reference to Morawski's proverb collection (p. 196 n. 1).
- **Ch07 (the Praguerie of 1440; the dauphin "sixteen").** Basin: Louis "circiter decimum sextum vel decimum septimum etatis annum ageret" (p. 256 / PDF p. 306); the rising's principal author was Bourbon, "tocius sedicionis principalis auctoris" (p. 258 / PDF p. 308); it ended with a general pardon "tam filio quam duci Borbonii et ceteris conjuracionis sociis" (p. 258). Samaran dates the whole to 1440 (p. 259 n. 1).
- **Ch06 §VI (providential register of the chroniclers).** The Castillon "non fato... sed divina ita disponente providentia" the manuscript quotes from Quicherat has exact precedents here: Salisbury at Orléans in 1428 is killed by a bombard stone "nutu divine providencie" (p. 118–20 / PDF p. 168–70); Arundel at Gerberoy in 1435 is wounded "ictu bombardelle... juxta pedem" after "divina bonitas non permisit" (p. 208–12 / PDF p. 261–63). Gunpowder kills English commanders in Basin three times; each time heaven, not the gun, is the cause. Basin's habit, not a judgement on Talbot.

## 3. What it CORRECTS

- **Manuscript ch06 §VI and spine §8(e)(4) — the citation "Basin, ed. Quicherat, i. 261–68" and "i. 263–64" for Castillon and Bureau.** Not correctable from this file; those passages are in Book V (tome II). The manuscript's own flag ("verify against the page images at assembly") stands. Note for the assembler: Samaran's edition is the one to cite, not Quicherat's, because Samaran prints the Göttingen manuscript "revu et complété par Thomas Basin lui-même, manuscrit que J. Quicherat n'avait pas connu" (p. xxi / PDF p. 23), and shows Quicherat's text to be "une copie postérieure de près d'un siècle, copie incomplète et parfois assez corrompue" (p. xxxvi–xxxvii / PDF p. 39). Where Quicherat and Samaran differ in the Castillon chapter, Samaran's Latin is the author's.
- **Manuscript ch10 §II ("fifteen companies of equal size... is Basin's precision").** Not in this volume; tome II. Nothing to correct, but nothing to confirm either.
- **Manuscript ch07 (the Praguerie "rose against a *succeeding* state — specifically, by name, against the permanent tax and the royal monopoly of force").** Basin, who is the nearest thing to a court insider among the chroniclers, gives the rising no fiscal or military-monopoly motive at all. His causes are Bourbon and "quibusdam pravis hominibus" persuading a young dauphin that his father, "ocio vacans et luxui", let the realm be torn apart (p. 256 / PDF p. 306). The manuscript's "specifically, by name" should be pinned to Contamine (its own [GAP] already says so) or softened: the T1 witness in hand does not name the taille or the ordonnance.
- **Manuscript ch07 (Praguerie broken "in five months")** and **ch10 (Louis born 1423)** are consistent with Samaran's notes (p. 257 n. 3: born 3 July 1423; p. 259 n. 1). No correction.
- **Chronology the manuscript should not inherit from Basin.** Samaran corrects him repeatedly: Rouen's treaty 13 January 1419 (p. 65 n. 1); Harfleur 1415 surrendered 18 September (p. 37 n. 1); Basin's "60,000" dead at Rouen "semble exagéré" (p. 63 n. 2 / PDF p. 113). The manuscript's Rouen 1418–19 paragraph is closer to the modern figures than Basin is; do not "upgrade" it with his numbers.

## 4. What it ADDS

1. **Ch06 §V — the 1435–36 Caux rising as the reverse control to Bordeaux 1452.** This is the volume's most valuable gift. In III.iv–v Basin narrates a whole province rising for the French crown without any royal instrument behind it: the Cauchois "totum populum terre adversus Anglicos... insurgere et arma vertere fecerunt", took Harfleur and Montivilliers "impetu multitudinis", then were cut to pieces before Caudebec by "ducenti vel trecenti equites" (p. 216–18 / PDF p. 266–68), after which "ultra ducenta milia animarum ferro, fame et tabo absumpta" and the land lay waste "ultra decem annos" (p. 222–24 / PDF p. 272–74). Basin's own counterfactual is the manuscript's argument stated by a hostile witness: "si illuc quingente tantummodo lancee ad conducendum illum Francorum regi devotissimum populum misse fuissent sub fidis ducibus rempublicam cordi habentibus... Rothomagum et cetera illius patrie opida... recuperassent" (p. 224 / PDF p. 274); the towns "cotidie prestolantes ut ad se agmen illud popularium adventaret" (p. 224). In 1435 the population invited the crown in and there was no state to accept the invitation; in 1449 the same population opened Rouen and the train was there to hold it; in 1452 Bordeaux invited the English in and Talbot had no train. The three cases together dispose of the morale explanation more cleanly than the two the manuscript now uses. Place it directly before "Loyal populations, hostile politics, same cascade" (ch06 §V).
2. **Ch06 §III / ch10 §II — the "before" photograph of the siege train.** Basin's siege vocabulary before 1441 is *petrariae* (Soissons 1414, p. 26; Lagny 1432, "saxis, petrariis et tormentis", p. 168 / PDF p. 218; Calais 1436, p. 240–42), the English at Louviers 1431 use "arietibus et gruibus talibusque belli machinamentis" (p. 170 / PDF p. 220), and Somerset in 1443 ships "portatiles pontes ex lignis, restibus et animalium tergoribus... cum aliis ad expugnandas urbes atque arces plurimis petrariis" and fails before Pouancé (p. 282 / PDF p. 332). Then, at Pontoise 1441, the text suddenly names "bombardellis, quas colubrinas et serpentinas vocant" and the king's "magistros" (p. 272). The manuscript's 1442 Bureau procurement list (ch06 §III) — 20 *serpentines*, 40 *couleuvrines* — is the accounts-side of what Basin saw on the wall the year before. Quote the two together.
3. **Ch06 §IV — the forward base precedes the train.** III.xi: rather than relieve Harfleur in 1440, the French refortified Louviers, "fundamentum tamen ex hoc magnum jecerunt ipsisque valde utiliter cessit in usus ad recuperacionem tocius Normannie" (p. 252 / PDF p. 302).
4. **Ch05 (the English case) — Basin's contemporary misdiagnosis, for spine §8(e)(3) and (6).** Writing of 1444, Basin explains English decline by luxury: "Corrupti enim hiis viciis que ex rebus secundis provenire solent, avaricia et luxuria, simulque variis jam Galliarum deliciis assueti, illam barbaram feritatem seu animositatem... nimium remiserant" (p. 290 / PDF p. 340) — while insisting England itself was rich and internally at peace: "ipsa Anglia florere cernebatur. Que intestina prorsus nulla bella tunc habebat" (p. 290). A witness who has just described the king's gunners at Pontoise still reasons in moral categories. This is the perplexity-contemporary-misdiagnosis point with a T1 pin, and it is the same man who at Castillon will blame temerity and providence.
5. **Ch07 / ch10 — the tax-grievance politics of 1407.** John the Fearless's propaganda, in Basin's telling, was fiscal: Orléans painted as "novorum tributorum atque vectigalium auctor et defensor maximus", the duke as restorer of "antiquas suas libertates atque immunitates", and "tanta invidia apud plebes, que hujuscemodi gravamina vectigalium atque exactionum altius sentiunt" followed (p. 16–18 / PDF p. 66–68). For the Gascony tax-grievance tradition (spine §8(e)(5)): Basin is the source-type from which it descends — a liberties-and-taxes ideologue who reads every rising through his own grievance. Vale's caution about Gascony can be stated as a caution about Basin.
6. **Ch03 / ch08 — private violence and the state's absence.** The brigands of II.vi: "ultra decem millia" hanged in one year, known "ex publicis raciociniis" (p. 112 / PDF p. 162; Samaran doubts the figure, p. 113 n. 2); and the Norman peasant militia of 1434, organised by "decuriones seu decani, quinquagenarii, centuriones, millenarii", massacred at Vicques, "supra mille et trecentos" (p. 198–200 / PDF p. 248–50).
7. **Ch06 §VI (persons and motives) — Talbot before Castillon.** Basin's Talbot here is "ducum Anglorum omnium strennuissimus atque audacissimus" (p. 262 / PDF p. 312); the Poissy raid of 1441, on his "prudentissime" advice, missed Charles VII by "minus quam hore unius" (p. 266–68 / PDF p. 316–18); and at Pontoise he axed a disarmed prisoner himself, "securi ipsemet percusserat" (p. 274 / PDF p. 324). The source of the temerity verdict shows him fast, exact and cruel — not rash.

## 5. What it CONTRADICTS

The volume cuts against the manuscript at one point, and against the register of one spine ruling.

**(a) Basin's causal model.** Basin's explanation of why France lost and then won is discipline, not money and not guns. The refrain quoted in §1 (p. 194–96) attributes the eventual victory to *ordo et disciplina* introduced "eight or nine years" later; the Pontoise chapter attributes the fall of the town to the king's gunners; nowhere in Books I–III does the fisc appear as a cause. His preferred remedy is stated in the intro as fewer soldiers and fewer pensions (p. xii). The T1 witness the manuscript leans on for the companies and for Castillon believed that the permanent army was oppression and that its success was a matter of *virtus* and order. **The book's best answer:** this is exactly spine §8(e)(6) — the contemporary who could not see the fiscal apparatus behind the guns — and Basin's own 1441 sentence ("tam expertes illius artis magistros rex habebat") admits the professional instrument even while his frame denies it. The answer is good, provided the manuscript stops treating Basin as a neutral reporter of the companies and names him as their enemy.

**(b) Spine §8(e)(4), the Talbot conjecture ("he went for the guns").** This volume contains no Castillon, so it cannot contradict the conjecture directly. But it shows that Basin's "temerity" is not a judgement reserved for Talbot; it is his standing category for every commander who attacks a fortified camp — Clarence at Baugé, "pro sua temeritate" (p. 76 / PDF p. 126); the Scots at Verneuil, "temerarii nimium ac superbi" (p. 92 / PDF p. 142); René of Anjou's Germans at Bulgnéville, who ignored Barbazan's warning that nothing is "periculosius seu perniciosius" than *temeritas* and were "ex sua temeritate devicti" (p. 176–78 / PDF p. 226–28); the Cauchois "inconsulte ac temere" before Caudebec (p. 218). Bulgnéville is the closest structural twin to Castillon in the volume: a superior force charging an entrenched camp laid out "secundum rei militaris artem", broken by 800–1,000 archers, the veteran counsellor dismounting to die (p. 174–78). Basin's Castillon verdict is therefore a *topos* applied for the fourth or fifth time, not an assessment of Talbot's intent — which lowers the weight of the contrary T1 verdict. **The book's best answer** is to say so, and to keep the conjecture tagged as conjecture, because Basin's *providence* is equally a topos (Salisbury, Arundel): neither of his two causes at Castillon carries information about the third.

Neither point reaches a DEFEND-tier claim (spine §6). Point (a) touches the *register* of §8(e)(6) and the ch10 use of Basin; point (b) touches the wager in §8(e)(4), in the manuscript's favour.

- **T2 (Samaran's introduction), footnote** in ch06's critiques.md and sources.md: reliability (pp. xvii–xix), the two redactions (pp. xxvii–xxxi), Quicherat's corrupt text (pp. xxxvi–xxxvii).

## 7. Verdict

This volume does not change the book's direction and cannot settle the Castillon or 1445 citations, because it stops in 1444; it changes the *weight* of Basin as a witness, both ways — it supplies a second independent walls-and-minds artillery sentence (Pontoise 1441) and a third controlled case for the morale argument (Caux 1435–36), while exposing Basin as the fiscal-military state's declared enemy whose "temerity" and "providence" are stock verdicts he applies to every assault on a camp. The single most important thing the author should know is that the Castillon, Bureau and *quindecim* passages the manuscript quotes from Quicherat are in tome II (1944), on a manuscript Quicherat never saw, and must be re-read there before any page is pinned.
