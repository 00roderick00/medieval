# Assessment — Ralph A. Griffiths, *The Reign of King Henry VI: The Exercise of Royal Authority, 1422–1461*

**Text assessed:** the 1981 University of California Press printing (PDF pp. 3–4). The task names the 1998 second edition, understood to be a reissue with the same pagination; pins are to the 1981 printing and should be spot-checked before press. Printed page numbers survive as the last line of each PDF page: "p. 620 / PDF p. 672". OCR is clean apart from "The Pall of Lancastrian France" (PDF p. 502); the plates (PDF pp. 517–548) break the offset; printed pp. 713–14 are absent.

**Read:** Preface; ch. 11 pp. 248–54; ch. 12 pp. 284–88; ch. 13 pp. 320–22 and p. 344; ch. 15 pp. 376–92; ch. 18 pp. 504–50 with notes; ch. 21 (Cade) complete, pp. 610–57; ch. 23 pp. 715–41; Epilogue pp. 885–92. **Not read, as instructed:** Part One (chs 1–10, the minority); chs 16, 19, 20, 22, 24, 25; the remainder of chs 11, 13, 14, 17.

## 1. The book in two paragraphs

Not a thesis-driven monograph but the standard narrative-analytical account of the reign, organised around one question: who exercised royal authority when the king would not or could not. "It ended as a study of the exercise of royal authority during the reign of Henry VI" (Preface, p. vii / PDF p. 7). The tripartite structure is the argument: "Henry VI reigned for at least fourteen years before he began to rule, and then, in 1453, collapsed so completely that the realm's government fell into the hands of others" (p. vii).

On the king Griffiths sits between hagiography and Wolffe's "perverse wilfulness": "Henry VI was, in reality, a well-intentioned man, with aspirations that were laudable enough, in an age when kings could not rule by good intentions alone" (p. 253 / PDF p. 273). On the fall of France his verdict is internal: "It was the inner decay of Lancastrian power in France, rather than simply the overwhelming strength and superior equipment, organization, and leadership of the French, that ultimately destroyed the achievement of Henry V and the duke of Bedford" (p. 522 / PDF p. 574). On Cade his governing caution is evidentiary: "To regard those who received a pardon as active rebels to a man, deducing therefrom conclusions about their occupations and geographical origins, is thus quite unwarranted" (p. 621 / PDF p. 673).

## 2. What it CONFIRMS

**Ch05 §I–II, the collapse and the void.** Manuscript: the king "had to be fed and moved" and the realm for eight months "pretend[ed] there wasn't one". Griffiths: "he had to be fed and supported by two men when he moved from room to room ... Henry recognized nobody, understood nothing, and, when it was all over, remembered nothing" (p. 716 / PDF p. 766); "no public announcement of Henry's incapacity was made" (p. 719 / PDF p. 769). The Stodeley newsletter (p. 716) and Clere's letter (p. 717) are the T1 texts the chapter paraphrases and should quote. On the scheduled vs unscheduled vacancy: "England had coped with minorities in the past and in 1422 by a novel and successful constitutional device; but the mental and physical collapse of an adult monarch suggested no obvious precedent" (p. 720 / PDF p. 770).

**Ch09 §III, the non-substitute.** Griffiths: "The strictly limited powers enjoyed by the royal brothers after 1422 were now conferred on York: no more and no less. The formal act, dated 3 April ... was to remain in operation during the king's pleasure or until Prince Edward reached years of discretion" (p. 726 / PDF p. 776); the October 1455 terms "were identical with those of the first protectorate" (p. 718 / PDF p. 768). York's protestation appears at p. 725 as "restethe thexcercice of his auctoritee" — the manuscript's "resteth th'exercice" should be checked against RP V 242.

**Ch06 §IV–V, threat before breach.** "Between mid-August and mid-October a score of surrenders was negotiated without so much as an assault or a siege or, apparently, a shot fired in anger ... the daunting array of siege artillery and cannon, under the supervision of the Bureau brothers, Jean and Gaspard, respectively treasurer of France and maître d'artillerie, was enough to persuade many English captains that capitulation was advisable. Only two places held out for more than a day: ... Harcourt ... for eight days and ... Touques ... for only three" (p. 516 / PDF p. 568). Rouen's "50,000 écus within one year" and hostages including Shrewsbury (p. 517, n. 189); Harfleur "until New Year's Day 1450" (p. 518); Bayeux "a shattering assault by cannon for fifteen or sixteen days" to 16 May; Caen 24 June "after a battering lasting seventeen days"; Cherbourg 12 August (pp. 520–21). All match the census. And the defender bankrupt first: Kyriell's "about 4,500 men" with £9,000 authorised, "a fraction of the £140,000 which Sir John Fastolf had recently recommended" (p. 519 / PDF p. 571); "Lack of provision in 1449–51 ensured the Lancastrian collapse in France" (p. 382 / PDF p. 402).

**Ch07 §II, the restorationist programme.** "their proclamations of protest castigated in traditional vein the evil advisers of the king rather than the king himself" (p. 623 / PDF p. 675); "a touching, conservative faith in traditional, aristocratic government, and in the cleansing of an inefficient and corrupt régime rather than in its complete substitution by something better" (pp. 634–35). The "harvyste of hedys" (p. 649), Heathfield on 12 July (p. 616) and the Canterbury sessions from 28 January 1451 (p. 648) confirm the chronology.

**Ch07 §III, absorption without concession.** "It goes too far to claim that Suffolk's régime 'was destroyed' in 1449–50" (p. 287 / PDF p. 307); the resumption act of 6 May 1450 gained "within a month 186 clauses of exemption" (p. 389 / PDF p. 409).

## 3. What it CORRECTS

1. **Ch07 §I and n. 7-1 — the pardon-roll "gentry skeleton" attributed to Griffiths pp. 619–20.** The attribution is wrong in direction. Griffiths argues the pardon list cannot be read as a roster of rebels: Sir John Cheyne "was the only knight to receive a pardon on 6–7 July" and is offered as an *unlikely* rebel (p. 621 / PDF p. 673); Thornbury, Appleton, the archbishop's officials, abbots and priors took pardons for protection; "The most that can be said of the pardon-list is that it undoubtedly contains the names of many rebels – but also of many loyalists and others who took no part at all ... It represents, in brief, a cross-section of society in the south-east of England, but little more" (p. 621). The "eighteen squires, two MPs" tally is not in Griffiths. The [GAP] closes negatively: the sentence must be re-sourced to Harvey/Bohna, with Griffiths recorded as the dissent (§5).

2. **Ch07 §I — "the first week of June 1450 ... some five thousand men of Kent."** Griffiths: the rebels "had encamped there by 11 June" (p. 611 / PDF p. 663); he rejects the 4 June dating as "faulty chronology" (n. 8, p. 650). On numbers he gives only the contemporaries' 20,000–60,000, "notional" (p. 619, n. 67); nothing in Griffiths yields 5,000.

3. **Ch07 §II — the *Complaint* as one document.** The manuscript attributes "fals and of nowght browght up", "the see is lost, Fraunce is lost", the naming of "Slegge, Crowmer, Isle and Est" and York "named first" all to Stow's *Complaint*. Griffiths distinguishes three manifestos (pp. 628–38 / PDF pp. 680–90): Stow's Kentish complaint, in which "None of the king's advisers was mentioned by name" (p. 635); the Magdalen/Fastolf articles, which carry "his londez ern lost ... the see is lost, ffraunse his lost" (p. 636); and the "desires of the trewe comyns", which names "Stephen Slegge, William Crowmer, William Isle, and Robert Est" and the four dukes (p. 638). Which text carries each phrase must be verified; "quoted from the open transcription" will not survive review.

4. **Ch06 §IV — Fougères "seized by an English freebooter."** De Surienne was a Garter knight (27 November 1447) and royal pensioner; "There can now be little doubt about the complicity not only of Suffolk, but also of the king's lieutenant-general in France, Somerset"; the lords at Winchester "offered their congratulations to de Surienne on a job well done" (pp. 511–12 / PDF pp. 563–64). State policy, not freebooting — which sharpens ch06: the English state chose its casus belli while unable to fund the war. (Griffiths is inconsistent on the citadel's duration: "more than seven months", p. 511; "after five weeks", p. 512.)

5. **Ch09 §I — "On 25 March 1454, a deputation ... rode to Windsor."** The delegation was nominated on Saturday 23 March "to wait on the king next day"; the report came "on the following Monday" (pp. 724–25 / PDF pp. 774–75; cf. p. 716, "on 24 March"). Visit the 24th; record the 25th. The formal act is 3 April (p. 726).

6. **Ch07 §II — Moleyns "lynched by unpaid soldiers."** Griffiths: "hacked to death on 9 January – and by one of the force's own captains, Cuthbert Colville" (p. 519 / PDF p. 571).

7. **Ch06 §VI — Formigny figures.** "3,700 dead and upwards of nine hundred prisoners" mixes sources: Chartier's 3,764 dead go with captives "between 1,200 and 1,400"; the 900/2,300 pair is "one dejected English estimate" (p. 520 and n. 205, p. 546). Kyriell's army "about 5–6,000 strong".

8. **Minor.** Caen's besiegers "well over 20,000" (p. 521); Bordeaux "succumbed ... on 29 June 1451" (p. 530) against the manuscript's 30 June — reconcile against Vale.

## 4. What it ADDS

**Ch05 §II [GAP on household finance] — closed.** "By the time parliament was dissolved in July 1449, the king's current charges and debts had reportedly risen to the 'grete and grevouse' level of £372,000 (compared with £225,315 in 1433)" (p. 377 / PDF p. 397); household expenses reported at £24,000, "closer to £17,000" (p. 376); "a hand-to-mouth existence in a fundamental state of bankruptcy" (p. 377); customs 1441–52 "only 83 per cent of the figure for 1422–32" (p. 379); "during 1436–45 the taxation level was halved and yet the borrowing level was allowed to rise by a third" (p. 391 / PDF p. 411); the direct-taxation table (p. 384). The 1453 life-grant of indirect taxes "came too late" (p. 382). The hollow crown quantified.

**Ch05 §VI — the over-mighty subject as the crown's banker.** Cardinal Beaufort "supported the Lancastrian dynasty to the tune of a quarter of a million pounds", and "financial aid of this dimension consolidated his political power, as a result of which he was able to dictate the kind of security he wanted for his loans" (p. 392 / PDF p. 412). Capability concentrated inside a functioning order, as §VI argues.

**Ch06 §V — the asymmetry at office level.** "The most unlikely individuals now took charge of the king's works and artillery in Wales: grooms of the cellar and pages of the kitchen were transformed into armourers, master carpenters, and masons" (p. 344 / PDF p. 364) — against the Bureau establishment, the mechanism in a sentence. Gascony: Rivers' expedition "postponed time and again"; "£29,500 was assigned for the expedition – but by then it was too late" (p. 530 / PDF p. 582). 1453: "the English commanders were so short of cash for their men that they had to dig deep into their own private purses ... By contrast, the French army was strictly policed and adequately recompensed, and Charles VII's banker extraordinary, Jacques Coeur, gave a substantial contribution" (p. 532 / PDF p. 584).

**Ch06 §IV — primary colour.** Richemont's bombards on the Cherbourg beach, "completely covered with water when the tide rose ... At this the English were quite dumbfounded; for they had never encountered such mysteries before" (Chartier, quoted p. 521 / PDF p. 573); Charles VII's letter of 31 August 1450 ordering annual commemoration (p. 522); Fronsac "induced to negotiate a surrender on 5 June" and "opened its gates a couple of weeks later" (p. 530, n. 261) — the délai de secours running in the south, reconciling the manuscript's 15 June.

**Ch07 §II — the mutiny that decided the rising.** "Their mutiny was one of the most critical events of the entire rising. The captains and men of both the king and his magnates faltered, and then threatened to go over to the rebels unless certain ultimata were conceded" (p. 624 / PDF p. 676). The state's own retinues adopted the commons' programme — ch05's hollow crown meeting ch07's crowd. And the electoral capture: "all four sheriffs who had responsibility for making the returns for the five parliaments immediately preceding Cade's rebellion came from Saye's charmed, but unpopular, circle" (p. 633 / PDF p. 685). §III: the fiscal grievance was resumption — "that alienation of the king's resources should cease and with it the heavy taxation that was required instead" (p. 635 / PDF p. 687); Fortescue's *Governance* on resumption is quoted at p. 387.

**Ch09 §III — two improvisations the chapter misses.** Five physicians "receive formal authority, under the signatures of twenty-eight lords, for their experiments" (p. 717 / PDF p. 767); and "on 28 February, for the first and only time in medieval England, parliament imposed fines on those peers who failed to attend" (p. 724 / PDF p. 774). The queen's bill — "she desireth to have the hole reule of this land" (p. 722) — is the regency claim ch05 mentions without quoting.

## 5. What it CONTRADICTS

**(a) The composition of Cade's host — at full strength.** "The collective behaviour of the rebels was the behaviour of commoners, ill-organized, poorly armed, and badly disciplined, acting beyond the limits of their normal political ken" (p. 623 / PDF p. 675). "With less confidence, its rank and file may be said to have come from the lower orders; from the 'ribaudmen' and 'rustics' ... 'a fulle rude peple'" (p. 622 / PDF p. 674). "To regard the scores of constables who negotiated pardons on behalf of their respective hundreds as thereby admitting their involvement in the rebellion as recruiting agents (a view commonly held by historians) profoundly misunderstands the role they played on 6–7 July; each constable was certainly acting as an agent for his hundred community, but merely to secure protection for neighbours and fellow subjects" (p. 622). This is aimed squarely at the reading the manuscript takes from Bohna (2003) — the county "assembled in arms under its elected constables" — and Bohna wrote against Griffiths. The "propertied-anxious revolt" type-case rests, as drafted, on the inference the standard monograph calls "quite unwarranted".

*The book's best answer.* Two moves, both good. First, Griffiths concedes the propertied character of the *programme* and the *county*: the complaints "reflect the frustrations and resentments of practically all sections of Kentish society, including the substantial and well-to-do, the townsmen and the traders as well as the peasantry and yeomen" (p. 630 / PDF p. 682); Kent was "a shire supporting prosperous and independent – if small – peasant proprietors" (p. 630); the Magdalen manifesto appealed "to a threatened landowning community, obstructed in its litigiousness" (p. 637). The claim can be re-founded on grievance, structure and leadership rather than the roll's occupational tally. Second, Harvey (1991) and Bohna (2003) post-date Griffiths and re-argue the roll and the muster machinery. The honest formulation: Griffiths the caution, Harvey and Bohna the reply, the book siding with the reply for stated reasons. Defensible — but it costs the flat "refined but not reversed"; Griffiths did try to reverse Kriehn. No spine ruling is reached.

**(b) The king's agency — against ch05's Watts frame.** Ch05 follows Watts "at the interpretive level": an artificial king. Griffiths: "The king's personality was in large part determining the actions of his government in these most active years of his personal rule" (p. 251); "Nor can the king be relieved of at least some of the responsibility for the English attack on the Breton fortress of Fougères" (p. 252 / PDF p. 272). A named T2 disagreement (Griffiths/Wolffe vs Watts) that CLAUDE.md §4 requires the memo to record.

*The book's best answer.* The manuscript needs only that executive capacity was absent under fiscal-military load, which Griffiths grants in stronger fiscal terms than Watts. Griffiths' Henry — "extravagant, credulous, over-merciful", who "never shook off his youthful dependence on others in the routine and detail of affairs" (pp. 253–54) — is an undermighty king on any reading; an active but incompetent executive still fails §VI's test. Good answer; but ch05 §II should name both readings rather than "follow" one.

**(c) The fall of Normandy — Griffiths weights the English side.** "It was the inner decay of Lancastrian power in France, rather than simply the overwhelming strength and superior equipment, organization, and leadership of the French, that ultimately destroyed the achievement" (p. 522). Curry's position in 1981 dress; it reaches the DEFEND-tier mechanism obliquely. *The book's best answer* is ch06 §V's "two blades of the same shears" — and Griffiths' own p. 516 shows he does not deny the guns' reputational work; he never asks why the French could pay for them. The answer holds; cite the disagreement, do not hide it.

**(d) A quieter qualification to ch05 §III.** The clean contrast — Henry "total and continuous", Charles "recurrent and intermittent" — is softened by Griffiths' doubt whether "Henry's recovery at the end of 1454 was as complete as some contemporaries thought" and his refusal to dismiss the "supposed second bout of illness in 1455–56" (pp. 717–18 / PDF pp. 767–68). The instrument survives; hedge the sentence.

Griffiths is silent on Castillon's tactics — no Bureau, no gun park (pp. 532–33) — so he neither supports nor contradicts the Talbot wager (spine §6, §8(e)4).

## 6. Placement

- **Ch05 §II — T2 canonical, ally and rival.** Closes the household-finance [GAP] (pp. 376–92); name Griffiths in text once, where Watts is introduced, as the alternative reading of Henry's agency; footnote the rest. §VI: Beaufort as banker (p. 392), household men as armourers (p. 344) — footnote.
- **Ch06 §IV–V — T2 evidence, ally.** Fougères corrected from pp. 511–13; p. 516 quoted in text beside Curry; Chartier's beach bombards (p. 521) as colour; Gascon finance (pp. 530, 532) in §V's third counter-argument; the p. 522 verdict named in text as the English-side reading. Not for §VI.
- **Ch07 §I–II — T2 canonical, rival on composition, ally on programme.** Engage BY NAME in §I: Griffiths' caution in his words, Harvey/Bohna as the reply, n. 7-1 rewritten. The three-manifesto distinction (pp. 628–38) governs every quotation in §II. The mutiny (p. 624) and the sheriffs' circle (p. 633) enter the text.
- **Ch09 §III — T2 evidence, ally.** Pp. 720, 724–26; correct the 24/25 March dates. Footnote.
- **Not for** ch10–12, the appendices or Part I.

## 7. Verdict

Griffiths changes the weight of chs 5, 6 and 9 — closing the finance gap and confirming the census chronology — and changes the register of ch07, whose type-case must now be argued against the standard monograph rather than carried by it. The single most important thing the author should know is that the footnote attributing the Cade "gentry skeleton" to Griffiths pp. 619–20 cites the book for the opposite of what it says; the chapter's propertied-revolt claim survives, but on the programme, the county's structure and the later scholarship, not on the pardon roll. Nothing here touches the mechanism or the spine's rulings; Griffiths is silent on Castillon and, on why the French could pay for the guns, never asks the book's question.
