## PART A — Elizabeth L. Eisenstein, *The Printing Press as an Agent of Change* (Cambridge, 1979)

## 1. The book in two paragraphs

Eisenstein's is a book about an "unacknowledged revolution": the shift ‹from the copyist's desk to the printer's workshop› in the late fifteenth century, which she argues historians have treated as a footnote to the Renaissance or the Reformation rather than as a change of the conditions of all learning (orig. 3–5, PDF 33–35). Her governing thesis, stated in ch. 2, is that print introduced a cluster of effects — increased output, standardisation, the reorganisation of texts and reference, and above all a "preservative power" or fixity — and that these, not the "spread of ideas" alone, remade religion, scholarship and science. She refuses a single formula: ‹On the one hand it seems to have changed nothing; on the other it seems to have changed everything› (orig. 31, PDF 49) [TRANS. CLAUDE], and her stated aim is to follow Bacon's advice to note ‹the force, effect and consequences› of the three inventions — printing, gunpowder, compass — that ‹changed the whole face and state of things throughout the world› (orig. 20 and 43, PDF 42 and 56) [TRANS. CLAUDE].

For this project the load-bearing pages are not the science chapters but §2.5 on fixity and §§4.2–4.4 on the Reformation. There she argues that ‹it was not Protestantism but printing that made the medieval Latin Bible obsolete›, that ‹the forces released by print pointed always toward more democratic and more national forms of religion›, and that the Church had only two options, ‹to contain them or to let them develop› (orig. 353, PDF 248) [TRANS. CLAUDE]. The state-facing corollary, which the manuscript needs and which the AHR article does not carry, is at orig. 116–118: print ‹fixed› the linguistic map of Europe at the same moment as it fixed the Greek corpus, and ‹the étatist policies long pursued by rulers of every kind were carried further›, because in the age of print ‹the reproduction of documents was undertaken by ambitious laymen subject to the authority of the reigning ruler›, with Habsburg, Valois and Tudor monarchs ‹granting lucrative privileges to local firms to supply the state's clergy with printed matter› (orig. 117, PDF 99) [TRANS. CLAUDE].

## 2. What it CONFIRMS

**(a) Ch. 11 §V — "the state, in a single twelve-month span, *mandating* the vernacular canon and *licensing* the means of its reproduction."** Eisenstein carries the mandate at T2 and adds the English instruments the draft lacks. ‹Every ruler who broke with Rome sooner or later officially authorised a vernacular Bible; it was only a question of time› (orig. 348, PDF 246); the crown ‹authorised not only the vernacular Bible and the Book of Common Prayer but grammars and primers›, citing Lily's grammar of 1542 (‹His Majesty's will is to secure the uniformity of his subjects' minds… by absolutely uniform learning›) and Grafton's authorised Primer of 1545, issued ‹to avoid the diversity of primers… and establish one uniform order of primers throughout all our dominions› (orig. 349, PDF 246) [TRANS. CLAUDE]. She adds the sharp comparative point: ‹the timing of the reversal was crucial, because after print primers, catechisms and textbooks could be made uniform and a national type fixed for the long term› (orig. 349) — Valois France banned the French Bible and Psalter while England, having banned the Lollard Bible, now authorised one (orig. 348–349, PDF 246).

**(b) Ch. 11 §V — Cromwell as the operator.** ‹First among European rulers to make full use of the press's potential "in the form of a campaign" was Thomas Cromwell, in support of Henry VIII's divorce› (orig. 311, PDF 222, citing Elton); ‹Henry VIII and Thomas Cromwell used Parliament and the press systematically to win their subjects' support for the Reformation› (orig. 134–135, PDF 110, quoting the Louis XIV study) [TRANS. CLAUDE]; Grafton's ‹repeated petitions to Thomas Cromwell to order Matthew's Bible for every parish church and abbey› (orig. 355, PDF 250). The manuscript's 1538 injunction and Great Bible sit exactly here.

**(c) Ch. 11 §V — "Rome's mirror-move, the Index, followed in 1559."** Confirmed and dated more finely: ‹the Index of Paul IV of 1559 restated all the bans; Bible reading and Bible printing were brought specifically under the Ten Rules by the Index of Pius IV in 1564, and every later Index repeated the rule› (orig. 347, PDF 245); Tridentine policy was a series of ‹rearguard actions to contain the new forces released by Gutenberg's invention› using ‹new mechanisms such as the Index and the imprimatur to channel literature down narrow prescribed lines› (orig. 354, PDF 249) [TRANS. CLAUDE]. And the line the chapter can use for symmetry: ‹the same forces that produced the Bible belt produced the Index› (orig. 363, PDF 255).

**(d) Ch. 2 §IV and ch. 11 §V — print as an étatist instrument, not only a Protestant one.** ‹Monarchs were as eager as popes to exploit print. Both étatist and papalist tendencies were reinforced› (orig. 313, PDF 223); the Plantin–Philip II breviary in 15,000 copies, by which ‹the Spanish king displayed his control over his clergy and Plantin evaded tribute to the papally privileged printer, Paolo Manuzio in Venice› (orig. 117–118, PDF 100) [TRANS. CLAUDE]. This is the Iberian comparator the section needs.

**(e) Ch. 11 §§I, V and ch. 12 §III — legibility and the "Leviathan state".** Eisenstein herself makes the legibility move: with the "great book" of 1533 Englishmen could see that Magna Carta had been confirmed repeatedly in royal statute, where in 1327 confusion had reigned over which charter was which; ‹the mechanism by which the king's will was made known› was itself changed by fixity, so that ‹the king could no longer assume, when granting an immunity or signing an edict, that he was following ancient custom… monarchs learned from political theory that they were "making" law›; and by 1500 ‹legal presumptions were being devised to determine patents of invention and rights in literary works›, on which ‹a nascent bureaucracy would build a vast and complex legal framework›, with ‹new power handed to the bureaucrats› (orig. 118–120, PDF 100–101) [TRANS. CLAUDE]. She flags — as a gap she does not fill — that the ‹growth of the "Leviathan state" and the swarms of officials and bureaucrats across old-regime Europe› have to be ‹read between the lines› of print history (orig. 134, PDF 110).

## 3. What it CORRECTS

**(i) Manuscript (Ch. 11 §V) says "total output in the first half-century somewhere between fifteen and twenty million copies [GAP: the Febvre & Martin volume…]."** Eisenstein does not carry the Febvre–Martin figure; the only aggregate she quotes is Clapham's ‹by his fiftieth year some eight million books had been printed, more perhaps than all the scribes of Europe had produced since Constantine founded his city in 330› (orig. 45, PDF 57) [TRANS. CLAUDE], and she immediately warns that manuscript totals are ‹bound to be contested›. This is not a contradiction — the 15–20 million range is Febvre and Martin's — but the draft's GAP should note that the canonical Anglophone synthesis prints the lower figure, so the range must cite its source or widen to 8–20 million.

**(ii) Manuscript (Ch. 11 §V) says "The Stationers' charter (1557) then delegated enforcement to a chartered guild."** Eisenstein does not touch this; see Johns §3 below for the correction of tone.

**(iii) Manuscript (Ch. 11 §V) has the Index "follow" the Stationers' charter as a "mirror-move".** Eisenstein's chronology cuts the other way: the Index of 1559 restated ‹all the bans› already imposed by the imperial edict and papal bull of 1520 and by mid-century decrees in the Empire, France and Spain (orig. 347, PDF 245) — Rome's move was the continuation of a forty-year sequence, not a response to Mary's charter. Re-phrase "mirror-move" as "parallel move".

Nothing in the manuscript's dates for the Act of Supremacy, the injunctions of 1538 or the Great Bible is touched by Eisenstein.

## 4. What it ADDS

**(a) The "king's two bodies", printed — for Ch. 9 and Ch. 12 §III.** ‹Print made "the king's two bodies" — the body of royal office and the king's own body — more distinct in his subjects' eyes›; rulers' portraits and effigies were reproduced by the thousand, and the cult of a leader's person ‹across a whole nation› may be a by-product of the new communications system (orig. 134, PDF 110) [TRANS. CLAUDE]. Her illustration is the recognition of Louis XVI at Varennes ‹from the portrait on the assignat› — coin had never made a king recognisable in the flesh; paper money did (orig. 84, PDF 80). This is a primary-grade anecdote for the chapter that carries Kantorowicz.

**(b) Print and war — a named thread Ch. 10 §VIII could pick up.** Eisenstein flags John Hale's work on ‹print's influence on military strategy and the training of officers› as ‹a related and equally important problem› (orig. 135, PDF 110). The manuscript's compute-foundry-satellite stack has an early-modern print element (drill books, ordnance tables, fortification treatises) it does not currently use.

**(c) The unmaking of the manuscript's "universal Church" — Ch. 2.** ‹Print made it possible for the Church for the first time to insist on uniformity of worship› (orig. 313, PDF 223, quoting), while ‹the fissures in the Western Church had never before shattered its unity — not Canossa, not Constance, not three rival popes — but the sixteenth-century divorce of an English king did›, and it did so because ‹propaganda campaigns conducted in manuscript› — Philip the Fair's against Boniface — ‹had been more transitory in their effects›, whereas ‹once put in print, a schism deepened and could not be erased› (orig. 311, PDF 222) [TRANS. CLAUDE]. For a chapter that argues the platforms are "the new Church", this is the mechanism by which the old one lost its monopoly: fixity made schism permanent.

**(d) Nationalism as a print effect — Ch. 11 §V and Ch. 12 §VI.** ‹Nationalism and mass literacy went hand in hand, and not by accident› (orig. 362, PDF 254); ‹vernacular Bibles authorised by Protestant rulers balkanised Christendom and gave a once universal Bible a nationalist colouring› (orig. 701, PDF 466); the Protestant regions' abandonment of Church Latin ‹bound Christian and dynastic traditions more closely together› than in Catholic Europe, a point ‹worth noting when considering how different states resolved church–state conflict› (orig. 127–128, PDF 106) [TRANS. CLAUDE]. This is the "canon inside the bloc" argument stated by the book's own T2 authority, with the disanalogy built in: the canon that consolidated was also the canon that fragmented the larger communion.

**(e) The Anglican fusion versus the French — Ch. 11 comparative register.** ‹Despite its patriotism and hostility to extreme papalism, the French Church never fused with a national culture as the Anglican did›; ‹the intimate bond between Church and polity that characterised England was absent in France› because France ‹kept a language barrier — Latin for the liturgy while the law had gone vernacular› and ‹lacked a standard French Bible› (orig. 350–351, PDF 247) [TRANS. CLAUDE]. That is the mechanism for the manuscript's claim that Henry's supremacy was "executable because print had made a national vernacular religion administrable" — and it names the comparator where it was not.

**(f) The state-versus-Church privilege war — a memorable primary case.** The Plantin/Philip II breviary against Manuzio's papal privilege (orig. 117–118, PDF 100) is the single cleanest instance of a prince using a printing licence to displace Rome's licence; it belongs in Ch. 11 §V beside the Stationers.

## 5. What it CONTRADICTS

**(i) Print was not capital-intensive in the way the manuscript's sentence requires — and Eisenstein says the dispersed polity exploited it best.** Ch. 11 §V calls print "the second capital-intensive medium" whose exploitation required licensing "at national scale". Eisenstein: by 1500 ‹every important urban centre had a printing house; the print shop added a new cultural element to hundreds of towns› (orig. 43, PDF 56); ‹in the early modern period, access to publication frequently meant evading censorship and engaging in illicit activity — manuscripts smuggled abroad or printed at a clandestine local press› (orig. 18, PDF 41) [TRANS. CLAUDE]. And the decisive passage, orig. 645 (PDF 430): ‹the scanty scientific output of Europe's most populous, most powerful and most consolidated state, against the mass of print pouring from the small United Provinces… shows that the goals of a print industry stimulated by the nation-state were not consistent›; ‹the weakness of the "central" court and the strength of the local towns gave Dutch entrepreneurs a de facto, though not a de jure, freedom of the press› [TRANS. CLAUDE]. That is the interstitial space winning the medium. Her counterfactual at orig. 702 (PDF 467) is that the press ‹might have been monopolised by priests and rulers and withheld from urban entrepreneurs› — and was not; ‹the press changed society only in combination with other forces›.

**(ii) The forces print released were "democratic" as much as "national".** ‹Whatever happened at Wittenberg or Zurich… the Church had to confront a book market that was expanding; the forces released pointed toward more democratic and national forms of religion› (orig. 353, PDF 248). ‹The printing press… brought "the great liberty of lying" and the propaganda wars that made "king versus commons, court versus country, church versus state" into parties rather than factions› (orig. 132, PDF 109) [TRANS. CLAUDE]. The Reformation Eisenstein describes is the first ‹movement that used the press as an instrument of open agitation against the existing order› (orig. 304, PDF 218). The medium that let Henry mandate a canon let Luther, Lilburne and the pamphleteers of 1641 unmake crowns.

**The book's best answer.** The manuscript's fifth move is about doctrine and legibility — mandating a canon, placing it in every parish, registering the parishioners — not about owning presses. Eisenstein supports that reading on every page she gives the state: the mandated primer, the parish Bible, the printed statute, the breviary privilege. So the answer is to drop "capital-intensive" as the descriptor of print and re-specify: the capital-intensity lay in the *administration of the canon at national scale* (the injunctions of 1538 that ordered the Bible into every parish also ordered every parish to keep a register — the manuscript already has the instrument), while the press itself was longbow-priced. That reframing is honest and stronger, because it converts the contradiction into a stated disanalogy of the kind CLAUDE.md §6 requires: cannon could not be replicated by the subjects; presses could, and were. The Dutch point is harder. The manuscript's Part III predicts durable disorder in the interstices; Eisenstein's interstice (the Republic) out-produced the consolidators in the knowledge medium. The best available reply is that the Dutch case is a knowledge outcome, not a force outcome, and that the seventeenth-century Republic bought its way into the artillery state's league by other means — but the book should say plainly that consolidation of the canon and command of the medium's productive frontier came apart in the first print century, and score it in Appendix C as a risk to C11-1's fifth column rather than assume it away. No DEFEND-tier claim is touched; the sentence at risk is the book's own (c)-tier analogy, and §8(b)'s hybrid ruling absorbs the correction comfortably.

## 6. Placement

Ch. 11 §V: T2 canonical scholarship, engaged BY NAME — she is the authority for the mandate side (Lily, Grafton, Cromwell, the English–French reversal, the Index dates) and should be cited for those in the text, with the disanalogy of §5(i) stated in the same paragraph. Ch. 2 §IV: footnote for §4(c) (fixity made schism permanent). Ch. 9 and Ch. 12 §III: footnote for the "two bodies" and Varennes. Ch. 11 comparative register / Appendix B: the Anglican–Gallican contrast (§4(e)) and Plantin–Manuzio as an Iberian–Papal entry. Coda §VI and Appendix A: no. All citations must go to the 1979 CUP pagination after re-quotation from the English; the Chinese edition can serve for page-finding only.

## 7. Verdict

Eisenstein changes the chapter's weight and its register, not the book's direction: she is the manuscript's ally on the mandate, the canon and the nationalising effect of a fixed vernacular scripture, and she supplies the English instruments and the French counter-case the section lacks. The single most important thing the author should know is that the phrase "second capital-intensive medium" cannot survive her: the press was cheap and everywhere, the state's harness was the canon and the register, and the most consolidated states throttled their own print while the least consolidated polity in Europe ran the medium — a disanalogy the chapter must state rather than absorb.

## PART B — Adrian Johns, *The Nature of the Book: Print and Knowledge in the Making* (Chicago, 1998)

## 1. The book in two paragraphs

Johns's target is the concept of "print culture" as Eisenstein articulated it. "If called upon, we may assert that printed texts are identical and reliable because that is simply what printing is… Yet this book argues that it is substantially false" (p. 2, PDF 25). Fixity, standardisation and reliability were not properties of the press: "Veracity in particular is, it argues, extrinsic to the press itself, and has had to be grafted onto it… The Nature of the Book claims that the very identity of print itself has had to be made" (p. 2). His alternative is to treat fixity "not as an inherent quality, but as a transitive one… fixity exists only inasmuch as it is recognized and acted upon by people — and not otherwise" (p. 19, PDF 42), and his brutal summary is that "Eisenstein's print culture does not exist" (p. 19).

The constructive half, which is what this project needs, is about who did the grafting. "The 'printing revolution,' if there was one, consisted of changes in the conventions of handling and investing credit in textual materials, as much as in transformations in their manufacture" (pp. 35–36, PDF 58–59); "Fixity was in the eye of the beholder, and its recognition could not be maintained without continuing effort" (p. 36). The beholders were made by an institution: the Stationers' Company and the licensing regime "in tandem" (p. 190, PDF 213), whose "bilateral regime" underwrote "the reliability of every published page, whether licensed or not" (p. 624, PDF 647). "Fixity depended on civility" (p. 624). Against that regime stood a rival, royal model — patents and prerogative — whose defeat in 1688–1695 is the pivot of the book's political chapters.

## 2. What it CONFIRMS

**(a) Ch. 11 §V — the sceptics concede.** Johns does not deny standardisation; he re-attributes it. "I do not question that print enabled the stabilization of texts, to some extent; although fixity was far rarer and harder to discern in early modern Europe than most modern historians assume. I do, however, question the character of the link between the two" (p. 36, PDF 59). The manuscript's characterisation of the quarrel — "over *how* print's effects were made, not whether the state could harness them" — is fair to the book itself, not only to the AHR piece.

**(b) Ch. 11 §V — Tudor licensing and the chartered guild.** "In the sixteenth century, almost from the first introduction of printing, Tudor administrations had instituted measures designed to temper any ill effects the new craft might have, and channel it toward approved ends. The Stationers' Company itself was one product. From then on, state regulation and craft morality developed in concert" (p. 189, PDF 212). Mary "incorporated the Stationers' Company in 1557… It was expected to encompass the regulation of all practitioners of the book trade in the capital… preventing seditious publishing and putting a stop to unauthorized printing" (p. 201, PDF 224). Licensing rested on "laws and decrees dating back to at least 1530", with the Star Chamber decree of 1586 as "its principal foundation" (p. 230, PDF 253); "in the period 1586–1695 some form of licensing was in force for all but nine years, and… whenever it lapsed pamphlets and piracies seemed to flourish" (p. 234, PDF 257).

**(c) Ch. 11 §VII and ch. 12 — the hybrid settlement, "operative but never invested".** The propriety of print "had always rested on a pragmatic quadruple alliance between Stationers, writers, readers, and the state" (p. 248, PDF 271); licensing "combined the political interest of the royal court with the proprietorial interest of the Stationers' court" (p. 234). The Stationers' Hall itself was a castle with its own court, "a fee" whose jurisdiction the city did not enter (pp. 193–196, PDF 216–219) — a franchise jurisdiction of exactly the kind the manuscript's ch. 8 describes, chartered by the crown and policing the crown's medium for its own profit. Spine §8(b)'s "hierarchical plurality… the mature legal form" is here in the English print settlement.

**(d) Ch. 12 §VII — the licensing premium as a durable rent and a crown-dependent caste.** Patentees "were wealthy printers or booksellers — or even gentlemen from outside the trade altogether — to whom the crown granted exclusive rights to key titles, or indeed to whole classes of publication. One patentee held the right to all law books, for example; another held that to all bibles. It was reckoned that they and their books would be securely trustworthy by virtue of their gentility and their dependence on royal favor" (p. 39, PDF 62). "It was hoped that by extending protection in this way, a new grade of tradesman would be created, wealthy and powerful within the book trade but directly dependent on crown favor. Patents thus promised to replace the power of the Stationers' community with that of the monarch" (p. 250, PDF 273). This is the book's American prediction — licence, clearance, procurement-adjacency — in its Stuart form.

**(e) Ch. 12 §IV — the crown's claim over the medium as a regalian right.** "As early as 1636, the crown had complained that the register represented a threat to 'as absolute a right of his Ma[jesty's] p'rogative as that of Coyning'" (p. 308, PDF 331); James II in 1685 "reminded the Stationers that the press was 'a Prerogative inseparable from the Soveraignty of our Imperiall Crowne,' and for once this was true in practice" (p. 319, PDF 342). The eighteenth-century commonplace, in the *Annual Register*: printing "was counted the King's Prerogative as much as Coining, but in Time it became a free Trade" (p. 363, PDF 386). The manuscript's ch. 12 licensing move has a documented ancestor that used the manuscript's own vocabulary — print as coinage.

## 3. What it CORRECTS

**(i) Manuscript (Ch. 11 §V) says the 1557 charter "delegated enforcement to a chartered guild" and stops there.** Johns: "the Stationers' Company was never simply repressive in character… By the late sixteenth century, the Company had become central to the creation of a culture" (p. 201). The delegation was not a one-way instrument of the state; it created a second power. By 1664 the crown's own pamphleteer, Atkyns, was arguing that "The Company's charter made it nothing less than a 'Petit State'… and therefore 'inconsistent with Monarchy'" (p. 308, PDF 331). Correct "delegated enforcement to" to "chartered a guild that took enforcement, and much else, into its own hands".

**(ii) Manuscript (Ch. 11 §V) implies licensing was a state capacity exercised on the trade.** Johns: "The early modern state had neither the ideology, nor the finances, nor the mechanisms, nor the police and personnel to construct a regime of censorship recognizable as such to late twentieth-century eyes. On the contrary, the reality of seventeenth-century law and administration was that it operated through the participation of householders involved in the particular activity under constraint, and printing was no exception" (pp. 189–190, PDF 212–213). And Eisenstein's own picture of printers as "'natural' enemies to outside regulation" is, Johns says, "far from the case. In cities like London and Paris, the vast majority supported licensing and similar regimes… Printers were 'mechanicks,' as much in need of 'licensing' as preachers" (pp. 37–38, PDF 60–61). The instrument was co-owned.

**(iii) Manuscript (Ch. 11 §V) treats the 1538 proclamation as the beginning of a durable licensing state.** Johns's Table 3.2 (pp. 232–233, PDF 255–256) gives the sequence: Star Chamber 1586 and 1637; the collapse of the prerogative regime with Star Chamber in July 1641; parliamentary ordinances 1643, 1647, 1649; the Press Act of 1662, "renewed periodically until 1679", revived 1685, "only to lapse again in 1695. Despite the strenuous appeals of the Company, extended over many years, it was never again restored" (p. 234, PDF 257). "What has retrospectively been labeled the 'Copyright Act' of 1710 was in effect a qualified response to the Stationers' pleas for the protection hitherto provided under the licensing regime" (p. 234). The English licensing state lasted, with interruptions, 1538–1695 and then ended.

**(iv) Manuscript (Ch. 11 §V, following Eisenstein) treats standardisation as achieved.** Johns: "Martin Luther's German translation of Scripture was actually beaten into print by its first piracy, and in succeeding years the proportion of unauthorized to authorized texts was roughly ninety to one… The first book reputed to have been printed without any errors appeared only in 1760" (p. 31, PDF 54). Standardisation of the *canon* and stability of the *text* are different things; the manuscript's sentence "the canon could be standardised, distributed, placed in every parish and policed" should say "mandated", which is what the state could do, rather than "standardised", which it could not.

## 4. What it ADDS

**(a) The Corsellis forgery — Henry VI as the founder of English printing. For Ch. 11 §V or Ch. 5, and the best primary-source colour the two books offer.** To make print a royal property in 1664, Richard Atkyns produced a Lambeth manuscript (which "had never been discovered because it had never existed", p. 363) telling how Archbishop Bourchier "had immediately urged Henry VI to facilitate its introduction into England. The king, a well-known enthusiast for such works of intellectual philanthropy, agreed to help. They resolved to bribe a workman to leave Gutenberg's workshop. Bourchier provided three hundred marks for the enterprise, the king seven hundred" — Robert Turnour and William Caxton smuggled "Frederick Corsellis, 'one of the Under-Workmen'" to Oxford, "where he was kept under guard until he had revealed the secret" (p. 340, PDF 363). Atkyns "steadfastly repeated the view that kings, 'not onely in their publick, but private Capacity, as Proprietors,' governed printing by virtue of having introduced it at their own charge" (p. 343, PDF 366). The undermighty king of the manuscript's Part II was retrofitted, two centuries later, as the artillery state's founder of the second medium — the claim had to be forged because the history did not supply it. That is worth a paragraph in its own right: it shows what a Restoration absolutist thought the medium *ought* to have been.

**(b) The quo warranto — the crown's one real attempt at absorption, 1670–1688. For Ch. 12 §IV and the American licensing prediction.** After the Lords upheld the law patent against the register, "the Privy Council lost patience. It finally began the quo warranto proceedings for which Streater had so long argued. The very survival of the Company was now at stake" (p. 317, PDF 340); in 1684 "the Company at last lost its charter. For a moment, the Stationers' Company itself ceased to exist. It was then immediately reincorporated, with new assistants and new bylaws allowing the crown a more direct say in who got elected to the court. It was the first London company to get such a new charter, spearheading what soon became a sustained and systematic campaign by the crown against all civic corporations" (pp. 318–319, PDF 341–342). "The invasion of William of Orange finally removed the prospect of a comprehensive system of prerogative press regulation, and thus of an alternative to the print culture that developed in succeeding decades" (p. 320, PDF 343). This is the manuscript's mechanism attempted on the medium — the consolidator revoking the intermediate authority's charter — and reversed by a change of dynasty. It belongs in ch. 12 as the worked example of what the state-absorbs-stack route looked like when it was tried and lost.

**(c) The numbers the licensing state could not hold. For Ch. 11 §V's disanalogy.** "Repeated attempts were made to limit the number of printing houses to twenty… There were at least 53 houses in 1661–2… By 1705, ten years after the end of the Press Act, numbers had risen again, and there were about 62, not including the royal printers… Even at its most effective, regulation had never succeeded in reconstructing the world of printing in the image of the law" (pp. 72–73, PDF 95–96). Compare the cannon: no one ran fifty-three unlicensed artillery trains in Blackfriars.

**(d) The industrial threshold — where capital intensity actually arrived. For Ch. 11 §V and Appendix C.** "The development of truly mechanized printing with the steam press is certainly one element. The new technology increased production rates vastly, and the relative concentration of capital needed to invest in such machinery did militate against a diversity of printing operations" (p. 629, PDF 652); "By the end of the nineteenth century, print and fixity were as firmly conjoined by culture as ever could have been achieved by machinery" (p. 632, PDF 655). If the manuscript wants a capital-intensive print medium that a state could harness at national scale, Johns locates it in the nineteenth century, with the Society for the Diffusion of Useful Knowledge and the Penny Magazine (pp. 629–632) — and even there, readers "no less creatively appropriative than those who had stymied comparable projects during earlier eras" (p. 632) defeated the uniformity project. The manuscript's own present-day fifth move (models, identity rails, AI factories) is capital-intensive in the way the hand press was not; the parallel is to steam, not to Gutenberg.

**(e) The undermighty crown against its greatest city — a sentence for Ch. 5's continental register.** Johns quotes the summary that the political history of seventeenth-century England was "one of Europe's weakest crowns defied, broken, and replaced by its greatest city" (p. 61, PDF 84). The manuscript's undermighty-king disease, in its seventeenth-century recurrence, ran through the print trade of that city.

## 5. What it CONTRADICTS

**(i) At full strength: the state did not exploit the medium; a guild, a market and a court civility did, and when the crown tried to take the medium for itself, it lost.** "Readers worried about who decided what got into print, and about who controlled it once it was there" (p. 32, PDF 55) — and the answer was the Stationers, not the king: "In the seventeenth century, questions of literary 'propriety' were customarily decided out of writers' and readers' reach, in the citadel of Stationers' Hall" (p. 624, PDF 647). Atkyns and Streater "advocated the destruction of the Company and with it the extensive practical civility it embodied. They should be replaced by a comprehensive new system of press control based directly on crown prerogative… The source of trade identity would no longer be the collective of Stationers, but the king" (pp. 308–309, PDF 331–332). That programme won in the Lords, won in the Privy Council, won the quo warranto — and "Nobody really won… Streater was broken by the expense… He expired in debtors' prison, penniless and forgotten" (p. 320). By 1774 "The 'pirates' had won, and their actions had ceased to be piracies" (p. 366, PDF 389). The English settlement of print was the defeat of the consolidator's model.

**(ii) The revolution was constructed afterwards, by interested parties — and "print culture" is one of their artefacts.** "The historiography of 'fixity' was eventually to become so dominant… That modern writers have been able to refer to the press's ability to impose a 'logic' of cultural behavior is, in fact, a consequence of its very inability to do so: proponents had to propagandize extensively in such terms to establish fixity as plausible. By ignoring the reasons for their statements, we risk taking for a technological fact what was really a hard-won cultural artifact" (p. 625, PDF 648). "The printing revolution and the scientific revolution have remarkably similar pedigrees. Both are creations characteristic of the modern age, retrospectively identifying moments of transformation… both solidified only in the mid-nineteenth century" (p. 378, PDF 401). This reaches the manuscript's method: a book that argues from a "print revolution" as a datable technological step is arguing from a Victorian construction.

**(iii) The general lesson Johns draws for the present is the opposite of the manuscript's.** "Suppose for a moment that we really lived in the new age of global uniformity that is so frequently heralded… Regional and national cultures would have been rendered uniform by their common subjection to News Corporation, Microsoft, and Disney… The study of history in our own realm tells us that such an outcome is unlikely. Monolithic hegemony of this order is fortunately not yet achieved, and if the approach of this book is valid then its achievement may even be impossible. At least, it will not be attainable by the mechanisms now typically proposed" (pp. 637–638, PDF 660–661). If uniformity cannot be imposed by a medium, it cannot be imposed by a state's mandate over a medium either; the manuscript's "mandate the canon, license the presses" is exactly the mechanism Johns says never worked.

**The book's best answer.** To (i): Johns's story is the manuscript's story with the ending the manuscript predicts for the *American* case, not the English. Spine §8(c) says the American method is "regulation of the switch rather than seizure of the property: title left, discretion severed", by "whichever instrument fits the dependency". The Restoration crown reached for seizure (prerogative, patentee caste, quo warranto) and lost; what survived 1695 was liability law, legal deposit and the common-law property in copy — regulation of the switch. The manuscript can therefore cite Johns as the *precedent for its own ruling*: the English print settlement is the case where absorption failed and regulated hierarchy won, and that is the form §8(b) predicts. This answer is good, but it costs something: ch. 11 §V currently says the state "could license, mandate and administer" print and leaves it there; it must now say that the licensing half of the harness lasted 157 years and then was surrendered by a state that could not hold fifty-three printing houses to twenty, while the mandate half (the parish Bible, the primer, the register) endured. To (ii): the manuscript's spine is not the print revolution; it is the artillery revolution, whose technological step (the Bureau train, the délai de secours) is dated by siege durations, not by nineteenth-century commemoration. The book should say so, and should not call print "the second" of anything; it is the legibility instrument, and Johns is the reason to describe it as one. To (iii): Johns's target is media determinism; the manuscript's claim is fiscal-administrative determinism operating *through* a medium it mandates, and Johns himself concedes that "print and fixity were as firmly conjoined by culture as ever could have been achieved by machinery" by 1900 — culture backed by the compulsory school, the Stationers' successor institutions and statute. The honest position is that Johns weakens the fifth move's *mechanism* (the medium does not carry the canon by itself) without touching the four fiscal-military moves; the fifth move's claim must be downgraded from "the medium made the settlement executable" to "the settlement mandated the medium's product and regulated its switch". No DEFEND-tier claim is engaged; the affected ruling is §8(b), which the correction supports, and the affected sentence is the manuscript's own (c)-tier bridge.

## 6. Placement

Ch. 11 §V: T2, engaged BY NAME, as the second protagonist the chapter already stages — but now with the Company-as-petit-state, the numbers (53 v. 20), the 1695 lapse and the "as absolute a right… as that of Coyning" line in the text, so that the "sceptics concede" sentence is earned rather than asserted. Ch. 12 §IV and §VII: T2 evidence for the licensing-and-revocation route in its Stuart form (patentees, quo warranto 1684, James's 1685 declaration) and for its reversal in 1688–1695 — the worked example beside Standard Oil of an absorption attempt, with the opposite outcome; engaged by name. Ch. 5 or Ch. 11: the Corsellis forgery as a paragraph, T2 with Atkyns's 1664 pamphlet as its T1. Ch. 8 §I: footnote — Stationers' Hall as a franchise jurisdiction. Appendix C, C11-1 fifth column: the 1695 precedent as a stated failure mode for the "communications/legibility mobilisation" score. Coda §I (mechanism under conditions): footnote for the constructed-revolution warning. Appendix A: no.

## 7. Verdict

Johns changes the register of ch. 11 §V and the evidence base of ch. 12's licensing prediction; he does not change the book's direction, because the English print settlement he documents — absorption attempted by prerogative, defeated, replaced by regulated hierarchy — is the hybrid outcome the spine already rules for. The single most important thing the author should know is that the one early-modern state that tried to make the new medium "a Prerogative inseparable from the Soveraignty of our Imperiall Crowne" lost it within three years and never got it back, and that ch. 11 §V must carry that ending or be caught omitting it.

## JOINT VERDICT

Read together, the two books split the manuscript's sentence in half and keep one half. Eisenstein carries the mandate — the Tudor primer, the parish Bible, the authorised vernacular, the Index — and adds the French counter-case and the legibility effects (the printed statute, the recognisable king). Johns carries the harness's failure — a chartered guild that became a petit state, a licensing regime the crown could not enforce and finally surrendered, a royal claim over the medium that had to be forged in 1664 and was extinguished in 1688. Neither supports "print as the second capital-intensive medium"; both support "print as the legibility instrument the settlement mandated and could only partly control". The direction of the book is untouched and the spine's §8(b) hybrid is strengthened; the register of ch. 11 §V must change from assertion to concession, with the disanalogy stated: cannon was the weapon the subjects could not replicate, the press the one they always could. The single most important thing to know is that the debate the chapter says it stages is not, on the evidence of the two books themselves, a debate the state wins by default — it is the earliest documented case of the consolidator failing to absorb a stack, and the chapter is stronger for saying so.
