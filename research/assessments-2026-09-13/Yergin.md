# Assessment — Daniel Yergin, *The Prize: The Epic Quest for Oil, Money, and Power* (Simon & Schuster, 1991)

**Text used:** /home/claude/books/Yergin.txt — a 2011 Kindle reflow of the **1991** edition (copyright page, PDF p. 4), not the 2008 reissue; there is no 2008 epilogue. No running heads and no index survive, so pins are **PDF page numbers only** (1 + form-feeds), with .txt line numbers ("l. N") where useful. One calibration: Chernow (*Titan* n. 76) cites Roosevelt's "give us another dissolution" at Yergin printed p. 113 = PDF p. 162 here; the offset (~+49 in Part I) cannot be assumed constant. Chapter notes sit at chapter ends and were read for every passage relied on. OCR slips are scattered ("ioo-octane", "vaccuum") and affect no figure.

**Read:** Prologue (pp. 6–12); ch. 5 "The Dragon Slain" in full (pp. 137–65); ch. 13 "The Flood" from "Anarchy in the Oil Field" to the end (pp. 382–401); ch. 16 pp. 478, 484–500 with notes (the 1940 licensing, the July 1941 freeze); chs. 29–30 pp. 941–43, 985–86, 992–98 (the 1973 embargo, the companies' allocation, Heath and Drake); ch. 32 pp. 1045–46, 1054–56 (EPCA and the reserve); ch. 26 pp. 842, 854, 864; Epilogue pp. 1231–32. **Not read except by grep:** chs. 1–4, 6–12, 14–15, 17–28, 31, 33–36, the rest of the Epilogue. The whole text was grepped for Hepburn, common carrier, Interstate Commerce, Trading with the Enemy, proration, Connally, licence, freeze, Strategic Petroleum Reserve and the fortune figures.

## 1. The book in two paragraphs

Yergin's subject is "mastery", the word he takes from Churchill's 1911 decision to put the Navy on oil — "Mastery itself was the prize of the venture" (p. 7) — and turns into the thesis: "For oil has meant mastery throughout the twentieth century. And that quest for mastery is what this book is about" (p. 7). Three themes carry it: oil as "the rise and development of capitalism and modern business" (p. 8), with Standard Oil "among the world's very first and largest multinational enterprises"; oil as "a commodity intimately intertwined with national strategies and global politics and power" (p. 9); and "Hydrocarbon Society" (p. 10). Writing in 1991 he sees the next century: "mastery will certainly come as much from a computer chip as from a barrel of oil" (p. 9) — a sentence the book should quote.

For this project the book is a narrative of who held the power to turn the oil on and off, told across the very instruments the manuscript's off-switch table names, by an author who did not know he was answering that question. His verdict on the American 1930s system is the one that matters: it "emerged in an incremental way, piece by painful piece, responding to the unfolding of events" (p. 397), and "both the management of petroleum surplus and the relationship between oil companies and the government had been forever changed" (p. 398, l. 13609). He is a T3 carrier of the highest grade — every figure below traces through his notes to Nevins, Hidy and Hidy, Giddens, Nordhauser, Prindle, Ike or the Roosevelt and Ickes papers — but a synthesis, and to be cited as one.

## 2. What it CONFIRMS

**"Rockefeller kept every share and grew richer" (Chapter 12 §IV, l. 6629).** p. 161: "The stock shares in the successor companies were distributed pro rata to the shareholders of Standard Oil of New Jersey… Within a year of the dissolution of Standard Oil, the value of the shares of the successor companies had mostly doubled; in the case of Indiana, they tripled." pp. 161–62: "Nobody came out of this better or richer than the man who owned a quarter of all the shares, John D. Rockefeller. After the break-up… his personal worth rose to $900 million (equivalent to $9 billion today)." Roosevelt, 1912: "give us another dissolution" (p. 162).

**The decree and the structure (probe §1–2).** "Standard was given six months to dissolve itself" (p. 157). The 1899 holding company "held stock in forty-one other companies" (p. 139); after Ohio 1892 "the shares were transferred to twenty companies. But control remained with the same owners" (p. 139). No operational severance: the successors "generally respected one another's markets and carried on their old commercial relationships… competition among them was slow to develop" (p. 158).

**Chernow's finding that Rockefeller left power before 1911, independently.** "by 1897, he had—not yet sixty years of age—stepped aside, turning administrative leadership over to… John D. Archbold" (p. 140); he "did not actively involve himself in its management. Nor would Archbold have allowed that" (p. 142); Rogers on the retained title: "if any of us had to go to jail, he would have to go with us!" (p. 143).

**Regulation, not seizure, as the republic's instinct (spine §8(c)(ii)).** Theodore Roosevelt: combinations could be turned back "no more easily than the spring floods on the Mississippi. But, said the President, 'we can regulate and control them by levees'—that is, by regulation and public scrutiny" (p. 152). Quote this in the new passage.

**The table's dated rows.** Sterling's "state of insurrection", 17 August 1931 (p. 385). After the Supreme Court's January 1935 ruling "new legislation was speedily drafted and passed. The law became known as the Connally Hot Oil Act" (p. 394). The 1935 Interstate Oil Compact, Texas refusing any body with "the legal power to enforce adherence to the quotas" (p. 395).

**The 1941 licence mechanism the probe flagged as undocumented.** p. 494: "Export licenses would be required for all oil exports. On the evening of July 25, the U.S. government ordered all Japanese financial assets in the United States to be frozen. Licenses—that is, government approval—would be required for each use of the frozen funds, including the purchase of oil." The embargo as licence-denial: Acheson "turned the July 25 order into an embargo… by completely preventing the release of the frozen funds… 'Whether or not we had a policy, we had a state of affairs'" (p. 494; n. 17 to Treasury export reports in the Roosevelt papers, the Pearl Harbor Hearings, Blum, Acheson).

**EPCA 1975.** "The legislation that included the fuel efficiency standards also established a strategic petroleum reserve: the same idea that Eisenhower had proposed after the 1956 Suez crisis, and that the Shah had tried to sell to the United States in 1969" (p. 1056).

## 3. What it CORRECTS

1. **"proration from 1930" (Chapter 12 §IV, l. 6636) is too early.** The Commission's power was "critically circumscribed": Oklahoma's "had been empowered since 1915 to regulate oil production to match the market's demand, a power that was very explicitly denied to the Texas Railroad Commission" (p. 383, l. 13084). Its early orders were "checkmated" by federal courts; "the commission itself was held in contempt of court" (p. 384). "In the course of 1932, the Railroad Commission issued nineteen separate prorationing orders for East Texas, and each was declared invalid by the judiciary" (p. 386). Lawful market-demand proration dates from Sterling's November 1932 special session, which "rammed through a bill allowing market prorationing" (p. 386); enforcement from the executive order of 14 July 1933 and Ickes's quota telegrams of 2 September 1933 (pp. 392–93); durability from Connally, 1935. The probe's 14 August 1930 order is not contradicted, but it had no teeth. Rewrite: *attempted from 1930–31, lawful from November 1932, federally policed from 1933–35.*

2. **The 1941 row rests on two statutes.** Yergin ascribes the export lever to the National Defense Act of 2 July 1940: "Section VI gave the President the power to control exports; that would be the lever with which to regulate oil supplies to Japan" (p. 484, l. 16588); after the July 1940 order "an embargo had not gone into effect, only a licensing system" (p. 486). The freeze (the TWEA § 5(b) instrument) controlled the *funds*; the 1940 act controlled the *goods*. The row "TWEA/EO 8832 1941" should say both. Yergin never names TWEA.

3. **Date of the freeze.** Yergin: "the evening of July 25" (p. 494); the probe carries EO 8832 as 26 July. Announcement versus instrument — say so.

4. **The fortune and its sourcing (spine §8(c); review pack l. 115).** Yergin gives **$900 million only** — no base, no "tripling", no year — and n. 13 (p. 165) sources it to "Moore, Archbold, p. 281; Nevins, Study in Power, vol. 2, pp. 383 (Roosevelt), 404-5." So the $900M has an apparatus: Nevins's 1953 archival biography, ii. 404–05 — a T2 lineage Chernow's unfootnoted p. 622 lacks. But **the $300M → $900M tripling is not in Yergin**; its base exists only in Chernow (p. 621, unfootnoted; p. 390 says $200M). Carry "$900 million (Nevins, via Yergin p. 162); base figure Chernow's estimate." Never "tripled" as fact. "$9 billion today" is 1991 dollars.

5. **Hepburn 1906 has no support in Yergin** — zero hits for Hepburn, common carrier or the ICC. "pipelines made common carriers from 1906" (l. 6635–36) rests on the statute alone, which suffices; do not cite Yergin for it. What he carries is the matrix: the post-1904 investigation was "a searing critique of the trust's control of transportation" (p. 153), and the suit followed in November 1906 (p. 154).

## 4. What it ADDS

**(a) The licence inside production control (ch12 §IV; mirror-table proration row).** p. 392: Ickes sent investigators "even to dig up pipelines to measure the accuracy of sworn records. Subsequently, Federal 'certificates of clearance' were required to move any oil out of East Texas." The row currently borrows only a volume analogue; here is a federal licence-to-move, per shipment, 1933–34.

**(b) Ownership rewritten, title left — words for ruling (ii).** p. 393: the 2 September 1933 telegrams were "a historic act, a fundamental alteration in the way the industry operated… With prorationing, the rule of capture was overturned." p. 397: the system "rewrote the book on production and even, to some degree, on what constituted 'ownership' of oil reserves."

**(c) The switch placed where it was least visible, and voluntary in form (the 1439–46 rhyme).** Production control was chosen over price-fixing and "kept at the state level, where it would be less controversial, closer to the actual world of oil production, and much less visible" (pp. 393–94, l. 13449). After the NIRA fell the federal quota became "a sort of informal, voluntary 'quota'… each state essentially accepted and acted on a federally suggested quota" (pp. 394–95). Practice hardening into permanence without a mandatory instrument — the closest dated American parallel to the annual ordinances of 1440–46.

**(d) The border as a fourth instrument.** The 1932 tariff was "the protective dike behind which the rest of the regulatory system could subsequently be put into place" (pp. 395–96); the 1959 Mandatory Oil Import Program "put the prorationing systems in Texas and the other states back into a position where they could stabilize domestic prices" (p. 864). The table lacks import control, which the January 2026 chip proclamation (ch12 §II) makes topical.

**(e) 1973: the switch still in company hands — and the state's discovery that it must take it (the EPCA row's cause).** Gulf's head of supply: "We prorated worldwide. That meant cutting back our own refineries as well as our customers" (p. 992, l. 34203). Washington "gave little direct guidance" (p. 993). McFadzean of Shell: the companies had been "sucked into a vaccuum that had resulted… 'from the failure of governments to plan on how to handle an oil shortage'" (p. 995). Heath, owning 51 per cent of BP, demanded 100 per cent supply; Drake: "'then I must tell you that I must have it in writing'"; Heath: "'You know perfectly well that I can't put it in writing'" (p. 996). Whitehall's real lever was "that it was the British government that decided who would get exploration licenses in the North Sea" (p. 996) — licence, again. Yergin's close: "After the 1973 oil shock, it was clear that the oil companies could not and would not manage future crises by themselves, and that it was up to governments to take on that role" (p. 1231, l. 42524). The reserve's disanalogy row gains an ally: the SPR's build-up "turned out to be fatally slow" (p. 1056).

**(f) The switch as executive property.** Roosevelt to Ickes, June 1941: Japanese exports were "a matter not of oil conservation, but of foreign policy, a field peculiarly entrusted to the President" (pp. 491–92); controls were to be kept "'day to day,' a flexible tool that could be adjusted to specific circumstances" (p. 494). The revocation-rights row should quote this: discretion held at the summit, exercised by non-decision.

**(g) Scale and the over-mighty subject (Chapter 5 / 3, footnote).** Standard paid "more than $250 million in dividends" 1893–1901, "fully one-quarter of the total to Rockefeller", and was called "really a bank of the most gigantic character—a bank within an industry" (p. 142). The majors in their heyday had "the attributes of sovereign states: their own terms of trade, a multitude of dedicated citizens, treasuries that overshadowed those of most nations, their own foreign policies" (p. 1232). Comparators: Japan's 1934 Petroleum Industry Law, by which the government "sought to assert domination over the oil industry to serve its own needs" (p. 478); OPEC's founders wanting "an international Texas Railroad Commission" (p. 842).

## 5. What it CONTRADICTS

At full strength, three cuts.

**First, 1911 did not sever a sovereign switch; the market had already eroded it.** "The world had been changing too fast for Standard Oil; its system of controls had become too rigid" (p. 158). The new era "was born of several coincidences: the rapid rise of the automobile; the discovery of the new oil provinces in Texas, Oklahoma, California, and Kansas; new competitors; and technological advances in refining" — the break-up merely "Added to all these" (p. 161). On Yergin, 1911 liberated the successors' managers and chemists (Burton's cracking, pp. 159–61); it transferred control to no one.

**Second — the serious one — the public switch was not taken from the barons; it was begged for by a bankrupt industry and built as a state-run price cartel.** Ickes watched businessmen "'crawling to Washington on their hands and knees these days to beg the Government to run their businesses for them'" (p. 391). Federal quotas, which "a few years earlier… would have been enough to ignite a rebellion among oil men everywhere; now it was welcomed and greeted with relief" (p. 393). "Some Texas producers urgently petitioned for Federal supervision" (p. 387). The system's objects were "a dollar a barrel" (p. 385) and "the management of petroleum surplus" (p. 398) — producer price support, not sovereign control of energy; and the states refused to "surrender… sovereignty" to any enforcing compact (p. 395). This is capture with police powers, not consolidation.

**Third, the instrument was exported and turned on its maker.** "Many years later, others operating on an even larger scale would seize on it as a compelling model" (p. 397, l. 13582) — OPEC; in 1973 "it was the industrial nations that found their sovereignty diminished and under assault" (p. 1045). The switch the republic built migrated to foreign producers and to companies that "prorated worldwide".

**The book's best answer.** On the first: §8(c) is stated at the level of *control rights*, and Yergin confirms that after §§ 5–6 no one held unified command; that the market was also eroding dominance is the ordinary condition of consolidation. On the second: the thesis concerns *who holds the switch*, not why it was handed over; the taille was voted by Estates who wanted the routiers gone, and Yergin's own gradualism ("piece by painful piece") and voluntary-in-form permanence are the 1439–46 pattern to the letter. The chapter can absorb it by rewording: *the republic acquired the switch when the barons could no longer hold it, and kept it.* On the third: 1973 is the trigger, not the refutation — Yergin's conclusion that governments then had to take the role (p. 1231) is the compulsion spine §7 asserts. The answer is good on control and sequence; it is honestly weak on *motive and direction* — Yergin's state receives the switch, it does not sever it — so "severs" must soften wherever Standard Oil is the exhibit. This reaches spine §8(c) ("the state… destroyed the vertical integration that made oil sovereign") and the hold-on-balance platform-centralisation prediction; it does not touch the DEFEND-tier mechanism, which Yergin neither tests nor addresses.

## 7. Verdict

Yergin changes neither the book's direction nor its register; he changes the weight and wording of the off-switch table, supplying dated evidence for three rows (1932–35, 1941, 1975), the licence instrument inside two of them, and a $900 million that traces to Nevins — while withholding the tripling, Hepburn and TWEA, which the chapter must carry on other authority. The single most important thing: on Yergin's account the American state did not sever the oil switch — it was handed the switch by a broken industry in 1933 as a price cartel with federal police, kept it in a deliberately invisible and nominally voluntary form, and did not learn until 1973 that the companies still held the international switch. The proof case therefore supports the *lag* and the *hybrid form* exactly, but "severs" must become "acquires and keeps" wherever Standard Oil is the exhibit.
