# Assessment — three works: Belloc, *The Modern Traveller* (1898); *The Paston Letters*, ed. Gairdner (Arber reprint, vol. I, 1872); Nicholson, *Love, War and the Grail* (2001)

*Texts: books2/Belloc.txt (scan with thin OCR layer, 88 PDF pages, ~7k words — read whole); books2/Paston-Arber.txt (729 PDF pages, ~281k words — read the editorial front matter, Gairdner's Introduction at the points that touch 1449–53, and every letter from 1448 to 1461 bearing on Gresham, the 1450 crisis, Cade, the Norfolk disorder of 1452, Fastolf and private force; not read: the Introduction's 1422–47 narrative, the letters of 1422–47 and the miscellaneous abstracts of 1454–61); books2/Nicholson.txt (293 PDF pages, ~143k words — read the Preface, Introduction, Conclusions, and the passages on the orders' history and military role; the literary survey chapters (pp. 35–186) skimmed). ## I. Hilaire Belloc (with B. T. B.), *The Modern Traveller* (London: Edward Arnold, 1898)

## 1. The book in two paragraphs

The title page reads "THE MODERN TRAVELLER / BY / H. B. AND B. T. B. / Authors of 'More Beasts (For Worse Children)' / EDWARD ARNOLD / 37, BEDFORD STREET, LONDON / 1898" (PDF p. 5); the library stamp on the last leaf gives "Belloc, Hilaire and B.T.B." (PDF p. 87). It is a comic verse monologue in fourteen numbered sections: a returned African explorer, Mr Rooter, receives a reporter from *The Daily Menace* and narrates an expedition to Timbuctoo with two companions — Commander Henry Sin, a drunken, nationless mercenary sailor ("He was a citizen of earth, / A subject of the world!", PDF p. 14 / printed p. 10), and Captain William Blood, "A sort of modern Buccaneer, / Commercial and refined" whose "Napoleonic brain" turned "To company promotion" (PDF pp. 22–23 / printed pp. 18–19). The satire's target is the joint-stock imperialism of the 1890s — the chartered-company promoter, the "Libyan Association", the missionary "lent, / Upon a plot of land, / A sum at twenty-five per cent" (PDF p. 48 / printed p. 44) — and the press that sold it.

The couplet the manuscript wants sits in section VI, in the context of a labour mutiny, not a battle. Blood, having advised the Liberian minister's trick of changing porters' "wages to Indenture", faces the consequence: "The Caravan grew disaffected, / And Sin and I consulted; / Blood understood the Native mind. / He said: 'We must be firm but kind.' / A Mutiny resulted. / I never shall forget the way / That Blood upon this awful day / Preserved us all from death. / He stood upon a little mound, / Cast his lethargic eyes around, / And said beneath his breath: / 'Whatever happens we have got / The Maxim Gun, and they have not.'" (PDF p. 45 / printed p. 41; the OCR drops the opening quotation mark before "Whatever"). The next page completes the incident: "He marked them in their rude advance, / He hushed their rebel cheers; / With one extremely vulgar glance / He broke the Mutineers. / … / We shot and hanged a few, and then / The rest became devoted men." (PDF p. 46 / printed p. 42). The gun is never mentioned again.

## 2. What it CONFIRMS

The manuscript quotes nothing from Belloc (ch06 §VI: "the Belloc 1898 text is not in corpus and is not quoted here"), so there is nothing to confirm at the sentence level. What the text confirms is the retrieval itself: the couplet exists verbatim as the manuscript remembers it, in a public-domain text, and can be cited at T1 from this scan: Belloc, *The Modern Traveller* (1898), p. 41.

## 3. What it CORRECTS

1. **Ch06 §VI's framing of the couplet.** The draft groups "the satirist's Maxim-gun couplet and its twentieth-century heirs" among "the prophets of the knock-out blow, for whom the weapon was over- rather than under-estimated" (manuscript ll. 3390–97). The text does not support "prophet" or "knock-out blow". Blood is not predicting anything; he is reassuring himself, "beneath his breath", that a monopoly of fire will hold a mutiny of his own indentured porters — and it does, for a page. There is no enemy army, no war, no doctrine. The couplet is about *asymmetric possession*, and Freedman's assessment (assess/Freedman.md §3.1, §7) is right that "we have got the Maxim gun and they have not" is a joke about monopoly, not about decisiveness.

2. **But the monopoly is private, which is the point the manuscript can use.** The Maxim gun in the poem belongs to a three-man commercial expedition, not to a state. Blood has explicitly refused the Crown's commission: "'Thank you: I have my own position, / I need no parchment or commission, / And everyone who knows my name / Will call me Captain just the same.'" (PDF p. 31 / printed p. 27). The narrator, asked his nationality by a German official, answers "'I haven't got a nation.'" (PDF p. 64 / printed p. 60). So the couplet satirises a *chartered-company* monopoly of the decisive weapon — the condition Chapter 5 calls hollow sovereignty and Chapter 8 documents in the Pastons' guns — not the state's. Freedman's inference that the couplet "is this book's thesis, not its foil" therefore needs one adjustment: the poem's monopoly is the dispersal-period one (private force holding the best weapon), and its arc is the thesis's *negative* case. The opening summary of the expedition is "Of how we struggled to the coast, / And lost our ammunition; / How we retreated, side by side; / And how, like Englishmen, we died." (PDF p. 10 / printed p. 6); by section XI "our Caravan / Has all deserted to a man" (PDF p. 68 / printed p. 64); the king's fetish stops their bullets; Blood is ransomed and "sold … by the slice" (PDF p. 79 / printed p. 75); Sin ends "making soup". A weapon monopoly without porters, ammunition, legitimacy or a fisc behind it does not survive the poem. That is the artillery-state mechanism stated as farce: possession of the decisive weapon is not the process that sustains it.

3. **Attribution.** The illustrator "B. T. B." (Basil Temple Blackwood) is co-credited on the title page; a T1 citation should read "H. B. and B. T. B." or note the joint credit.

## 4. What it ADDS

- **The retrieval closes the ch06 §VI GAP in one line**, at T1, with the framing corrected per §3: the couplet is best used not as a knock-out-blow prophet but as the "Belloc syndrome" the spine already names (spine §8(f), item 3: "Talbot and the Belloc syndrome — Talbot's death illustrative of attacking the weapon, not the process"). The text supports exactly that use: Blood's faith in the gun as the thing that "Preserved us all from death", followed by the expedition's collapse for want of everything the gun does not supply.
- **Colour for ch05 or ch08 (private force under a private flag).** Blood's refusal of the Queen's commission and the nationless narrator are a satirist's 1898 portrait of the private military adventurer the neomedieval literature (McFate) treats as new. One sentence at most; it is a joke, not evidence.
- **The frontier-delimitation stanza** (PDF p. 61 / printed p. 57: the peer "Who stoops to a delimitation / With any European nation / Is doomed to disappear") is an aside on the scramble for Africa; no placement.

## 5. What it CONTRADICTS

Nothing at argument level. The one risk is rhetorical: if ch06 keeps "prophets of the knock-out blow" attached to Belloc, a reader who knows the poem will see that the couplet is neither prophecy nor about battle, and the "one mistake run in opposite directions" line will look forced. **The book's best answer**: drop the "heirs" clause from the couplet, cite Belloc for the syndrome (faith in the instrument) and Freedman/Holman for the knock-out-blow genealogy, as the Freedman assessment already recommends. That answer is good and costs one sentence.

## 6. Placement

- **ch06 §VI** — T1, quoted by name, p. 41; framed as monopoly-without-process, paired with Talbot. Retire the [GAP].
- **ch05 or ch08** — footnote at most (the private captain who "need[s] no parchment or commission").
- Nowhere else. OCR note: the text layer is thin but the couplet and its surrounding stanzas are clean; the running page numbers are garbled in places ("4o" for 40, "6o" for 60) and the title page OCR reads "Foi Worse" for "For Worse". The PDF pages of the poem's body run four ahead of the printed pages.

## II. *The Paston Letters, A.D. 1422–1509*, ed. James Gairdner, vol. I (1422–1461) — Arber's "Annotated Reprints" (London, 1872)

## 1. The book in two paragraphs

The file is not Arber's edition but Gairdner's, published under Arber's imprint. The title page (PDF p. 5, badly OCR'd) reads "[The Paston] Letters, 1422–1509 A.D. A New Edition, containing upwards of four hundred letters … hitherto unpublished. Edited by James Gairdner, of the Public Record Office. Volume I. 1422–1461 A.D. London … Annotated Reprints"; the facing page is Arber's series note: "ANNOTATED REPRINTS PUBLISHED BY, AND UNDER THE GENERAL SUPERVISION OF EDWARD ARBER … I think it a cause for great congratulation that this Series opens with a work of such national importance as THE PASTON LETTERS" (PDF p. 6). It is dedicated to J. S. Brewer (PDF pp. 7–8). This is the first volume of Gairdner's three-volume edition of 1872–75 — the ancestor of his six-volume "library edition" of 1904, which renumbered the letters. The manuscript's citations ("Gairdner, vol. II"; "vol. II, no. 193") do not correspond to this volume's numbering (see §3). The edition is superseded for text and dating by Davis (1971–76), which the manuscript also names but which is not in corpus; Gairdner's is a nineteenth-century transcription with modernised punctuation and editorial headnotes, and his Introduction (c. 150 pages, roman-paginated) is a narrative of Henry VI's reign that is T3 by today's standards.

Its value for this project is that it contains, in a citable text, every Paston document Chapter 8 §§I–II uses for 1449–51, and a great deal more from 1448–61 on private force that the chapter does not yet use. What it does not contain is the siege of Caister (1469), which falls in vol. II; the manuscript's Caister paragraph cannot be checked against this file.

## 2. What it CONFIRMS — the manuscript's Paston quotations, checked

All four quotations in Chapter 8 §§I–II are in this text, and three match it verbatim.

- **Ch08 §I, the Gresham petition** ("sent to the seid mansion a riotous peple, to the nombre of a thowsand persones… with curesse, brigaunders, jakks… gonnes… long cromes to drawe doun howsis… with which thei myned down the walles… and so came in to the seid mansion, the wiff of your besecher at that tyme beyng ther in, and xij. persones with her; the which persones thei dreve oute"). This is No. 77, "A.D. 1450. — JOHN PASTON'S PETITION. [Add. Charter 17,240, B.M.]", addressed "To the Kyng, oure Soverayn Lord, and to the right wyse and discrete Lordis, assemblyd in this present Parlement" (PDF pp. 259–62 / printed pp. 105–8). The full arms list is "curesse, brigaunders, jakks, salettes, gleyfes, bowes, arows, pavyse, gonnes, pannys with fier and teynes brennyng therein, long cromes to drawe doun howsis, ladders, pikoys, with which thei myned down the walles, and long trees with which thei broke up yates and dores" (PDF p. 260 / p. 106); the eviction clause is on PDF p. 261 / p. 107. **Match.** The manuscript's ellipses are honest.
- **Ch08 §I, "not abille to sue the commone lawe in redressyng of this heynos wrong, for the gret myght and alyaunce of the seid Lord"** — same petition, PDF p. 262 / p. 108. **Match**, exact.
- **Ch08 §II, "mayntenaunces, champerties, embraceries"** — No. 107, "A.D. 1450(?) — JOHN PASTON AND LORD MOLEYNS. [Add. Charter 17,239, B.M.]", addressed "Un to the right reverent fadir in God … the Cardinal Archebisshop of York, Prymat and Chaunceller of Inglond" (PDF pp. 297–99 / pp. 143–45): "all trespaces, extorcions, riottes, forcible entrees, mayntenaunces, champerties, embraceries, offenses, and mesprisions" (PDF p. 299 / p. 145). **Match.** Gairdner's footnotes on that page define the three terms ("Unlawful support given to a disputant by one not concerned in the cause"; "Bargains made with litigants for a share in what may be gained by the suit"; "Attempts to corrupt juries") — usable for the endnote. The manuscript's description "the Pastons' own bill to the Chancellor" is correct.
- **Ch08 §II, the Osbern letter** — No. 159, "A.D. 1451, 27 May. — JOHN OSBERN TO JOHN PASTON. [From Fenn, iii. 308.]" (PDF pp. 367–69 / pp. 213–15). The text reads: "he seyde he wold do for yow that he may, excepte for the aquitell of the Lord Molyns men, in so meche as the Kyng hath wrete to hym for to shewe favour to the Lord Moleyns and hese men, and as he seyth the indytement longyth to the Kyng, and not to yow, and the Lord Molyns a gret lord. Also, as he seyth, now late the Lord Molyns hath sent hym a letter, and my Lord of Norffolk anoder, for to shew favour in these indytements, he darnot abide the joporte of that, that he shuld offende the Kinges commaundment." (PDF pp. 368–69 / pp. 214–15). **Near-match with one mismatch**: the manuscript prints "Also he seythe that the Lord Molyns hath sent hym a letter", where this edition has "Also, as he seyth, now late the Lord Molyns hath sent hym a letter". Either the manuscript is quoting Davis's text (whose readings differ from Fenn/Gairdner) or the phrase has been smoothed; it must be conformed to whichever edition the note cites. Everything else in the quotation matches.

Two supporting facts in §II also confirm: Fastolf's will is dated 3 November 1459 (Nos. 332–34, PDF pp. 599 ff.), consistent with "died in November 1459"; Lomner's letter of 5 May 1450 (No. 93, PDF pp. 278–80 / pp. 124–26) dates Suffolk's murder to the Saturday, 2 May, as ch07 §II states.

## 3. What it CORRECTS

1. **Citation form.** Manuscript notes [^8-1]–[^8-4] read "Gairdner, vol. II" and "Gairdner, vol. II, no. 193". In this edition the petition is **vol. I, no. 77, pp. 105–8**; the Chancellor bill **vol. I, no. 107, pp. 143–45**; the Osbern letter **vol. I, no. 159, pp. 213–15**. If the notes are to the 1904 edition they must say so; if to this 1872 Arber printing they are wrong. Given the ledger's intention to cite Davis, the cleanest course is to pin everything to Davis when it arrives and use this file only to verify text.

2. **The date of Margaret's eviction.** Manuscript ch08 §I: "On 28 January 1449, by her husband's account, Margaret Paston was … evict[ed]." Gairdner 1872 dates it a year later. Headnote to No. 62: "Lord Molyns dispossessed John Paston of the lordship of Gresham on the 17th of February 1448. After repeated remonstrances … Paston went and took up his quarters there again on the 6th October 1449, and succeeded in keeping possession till the 28th January 1450, when the place was attacked, in his absence, by Lord Molyns' men, who undermined the walls, and drove out Paston's wife." (PDF p. 230 / p. 76); and to No. 77: "The date of this petition must be during the sitting of Parliament, in the beginning of the year 1450. The first expulsion of John Paston from Gresham is here clearly dated in February 1448. The 'October last' in which he re-entered might … have been in the same year, but the letters referring to this dispute in 1449 compel us to put it a twelvemonth later." (PDF p. 259 / p. 105). Gairdner is not consistent with himself — his headnote to No. 65 (Moleyns to the tenants, 24 March) says the letter belongs "either to 1449 or 1450. The latter date, however, is not very probable" (PDF p. 234 / p. 80). The petition's internal chronology (entry 17 February 26 Hen. VI = 1448; suit "on to the fest of Mihelmes than next folwyng"; re-entry "the vj. day of Octobre last past"; eviction "the xxviij. day of Januarij last past") reads most naturally as October 1448 and January 1449, which is the dating the manuscript uses and, to my knowledge, the one Davis and the modern Paston scholarship adopt — but that cannot be verified from corpus. The manuscript should not cite Gairdner 1872 for a 1449 date without noting that Gairdner himself printed 1450.

3. **Gairdner's OCR trap.** The petition's opening line reads in this scan "your horrible liege man, John Paston" (PDF p. 260); the word is "humble". Do not quote from the OCR without checking.

## 4. What it ADDS

The 1448–52 file is richer than the chapter's two documents, and several items close gaps or supply exactly the private-force evidence Chapter 8 §II gestures at.

- **The hand-gun loopholes at Gresham (ch08 §II).** Margaret to John, undated, placed by Gairdner in 1449: "prey zw to gete som crosse bowis, and wyndacs to bynd them with, and quarrels; for zour hwsis her ben so low that ther may non man schet owt with no long bowe … I sopose ze xuld have seche thyngs of Ser Jon Fastolf … Partryche and his felaschep … have made grete ordynaw[n]ce with inne the hwse … and they have made wykets on every quarter of the hwse to schote owte atte, bothe with bowys and with hand gunnys; and the holys that ben made forr hand gunnys, they ben scarse kne hey fro the plawncher [floor], and of soche holis ben made fyve." (No. 67, PDF pp. 236–37 / pp. 82–83). Gunpowder weapons in a gentry house-war a generation before Caister, and the arms sourced from a private captain's arsenal. This is the best single addition; it belongs beside the DeVries catalogue in ch08 §II and could be cross-referenced from ch10's England.
- **Fastolf's Southwark garrison and Cade (ch07 §II).** John Payn's retrospective letter: Cade's herald proclaimed Payn a spy "fro oone Syr John Fastolf, Knyght, the whech mynnysshed all the garrisons of Normaundy, and Manns, and Mayn, the whech was the cause of the lesyng of all the Kyngs tytyll … And morovyr he seid that the seid Sir John Fastolf had furnysshyd his plase with the olde sawdyors of Normaundy and abyllyments of werr, to destroy the comens of Kent" — and Payn "counseyled hym to put a wey all his abyllyments of werr and the olde sawdiors; and so he dyd, and went hymself to the Tour" (No. 99, PDF pp. 286–87 / pp. 132–33). A T1 witness to the discharged Normandy veterans as a private garrison in Southwark in June 1450, and to the rebels' reading of the war's loss as the captains' fault — both usable in ch07's account of the "long failure of the war state".
- **The magnate claims the shire (ch08 §II, ch05).** Norfolk's proclamation of April 1452: "We lete yow wete that nexst the Kynge our soverayn Lord, be his good grace and lycence, we woll have the princypall rewle and governance throwh all this schir, of whishe we ber our name, whyls that we be lyvynge, as ferre as reson and lawe requyrith, hoso ever will grutche or sey the [contrary]; for we woll that the Lord Scales, Sir Thomas Tudenham, Sir Mylis Stapylton, and John Heydon have in knowleche, thowh our persone be not dayly her, they schal fynde our power her at all tymes" (No. 173, PDF pp. 383–85 / pp. 229–31). A duke announcing, under royal commission, that he will rule the county for life: the "keyring of jurisdictions" in its own voice.
- **A private fortress in Norfolk (ch08 §II).** The 1452 "Information of Outrages": Charles Nowell and his fellowship "kepe a frunture and a forslet at the hows of the seid Robert Ledeham, and issu ought at her pleser, sumtyme vj., sumtyme xij., sumtyme xxx and mo, armed, jakked, and salattyd with bowis, arwys, speris, and bylles, and over ride the contre and oppresse the people"; the assault on John Paston "at the dore of the cathedrall cherche of Norweche … on of them holdyng the seid Paston be bothe armes at hese bakke"; and the consequence for ordinary life, "dyvers persones for feer of mordyr darnot abyde in her howses, ne ride, ne walke abowte ther ocupacions, wyth owte they take gretter people abowte hem then acordith to her degre" (No. 179, PDF pp. 391–94 / pp. 237–40). The last sentence is the arms-race logic of a plural legal order stated by its victims.
- **Fastolf as creditor of the Crown (ch10, the English fisc; ch08 §II).** "Fastolf's Claims against the Crown", 1455: unpaid wages, confiscated baronies in Maine ("goten by the seyd Fastolf, and no charge to the King"), a prisoner taken from him by Bedford; "Summa totalis xxjml. iiijxix. xx. [21,099] marc, vs. 6d." for the French items alone (No. 263, PDF pp. 512–20 / pp. 358–66). The veteran captain's balance sheet with the state — the fiscal side of the war whose loss ch06 narrates — and Gairdner's Introduction adds that Caister itself was built on that money under a licence "to fortify a dwelling there, 'so strong as himself could devise'" (Introduction, PDF pp. 92–93 / pp. lxxxviii–lxxxix; T3, resting on Dawson Turner).
- **York's field ordnance at Dartford, 1452 (ch05 or ch08).** The Cottonian Roll extract Gairdner prints: "ther was my Lord of Yorkes ordynaunce, iijmill. gownner, and hym selff in the middell ward with viijmill." (Appendix 4, PDF p. 152 / p. cxlviii). A chronicle number, to be discounted as such, but a T1 for a magnate bringing a gun-train against the king a year before Castillon.
- **For ch06, two sentences from the Introduction (T3, 1872).** Somerset's 1449 message to Parliament as Gairdner summarises it from the Rolls: "Not a single place was furnished with sufficient artillery, and the fortifications, almost everywhere, had fallen into such decay that even if filled with men and guns they could not possibly be defended … the whole province was in such extreme poverty that it could no longer endure further imposts for the charges of its own defence" (PDF p. 45 / p. xli, citing Rolls of Parl. v. 147 — a T1 route). And his Castillon, from Basin and De Coussy: "a train of artillery, with no less than 700 gunners, under the conduct of two able engineers of Paris, the brothers Bureau"; Talbot's "'I will hear no mass to-day until I have overthrown the company of Frenchmen in the park before me'" (PDF pp. 95–96 / pp. xci–xcii). Not load-bearing — the chapter has Escouchy — but a useful footnote on the older tradition.

## 5. What it CONTRADICTS

Nothing in the letters cuts against the chapter; they are its evidence. Two cautions. First, the chapter's claim that "Recovery of the manor followed no verdict; it followed a political event — the fall and murder of the Duke of Suffolk in 1450" is consistent with the file but is the chapter's inference, not a statement any letter makes; the letters after May 1450 show Paston still suing (Nos. 107–08, 159), and Moleyns himself acquitted (headnote to No. 159: "Lord Molyns had probably been acquitted, but the action against his men was still pending", PDF p. 367 / p. 213). Second, Gairdner's Introduction is a Victorian narrative and must not be cited for structural claims (it calls Cade's men "the rabble" and the year "miserable", PDF pp. 58–60). **The book's best answer** is simply to keep the letters at T1 and the Introduction at T3; that is already the doctrine.

## 6. Placement

- **ch08 §§I–II** — T1 evidence, by name: fix the four citations; add the crossbow/hand-gun letter, the Nowell "forslet", and Norfolk's proclamation. Conform the Osbern wording.
- **ch07 §II** — T1: Payn on Fastolf's veterans and Cade's accusation; Gairdner's Parminter and Smyth, "the second Captain of Kent" (PDF p. 60 / p. lvi), as a T3 pointer to the continuing Kentish unrest the chapter already asserts.
- **ch10 (English fisc)** — Fastolf's claims, as a footnote on the arrears economy.
- **ch05 / ch06** — footnotes only (York's ordnance; Somerset's 1449 plea).
- Davis remains the citation of record when retrieved; this file verifies text and supplies numbers and pages for the interim.

## III. Helen Nicholson, *Love, War and the Grail: Templars, Hospitallers and Teutonic Knights in Medieval Epic and Romance, 1150–1500* (Leiden: Brill, History of Warfare 4, 2001)

## 1. The book in two paragraphs

This is not the book the ledger wanted. The outstanding-reading-list entry (§C, "Templars, Hospitallers and Teutonic Knights, Helen Nicholson") and the ch01 §II GAP ("scholarly statement awaits Nicholson/Forey") refer to a history of the orders. The ledger's title matches Nicholson's *Templars, Hospitallers and Teutonic Knights: Images of the Military Orders, 1128–1291* (Leicester, 1993), which the present book cites as its own predecessor (PDF p. 18 / p. 2 n. 4: "idem, Templars, Hospitallers and Teutonic Knights: Images of the Military Orders, 1128–1291 (London, 1993), pp. 86–98"); if the intake note's "Nicholson 2001, the general survey" is meant, that is her *The Knights Templar: A New History* (Sutton, 2001), a different 2001 book. The file is neither: it is her 2001 Brill monograph on the orders' appearances in fiction. In her own words: "This is a study of the appearances of the Military Orders in the epic and romance literature of the Middle Ages, 1150–1500" (Preface, PDF p. 9 / p. vii); "This study is purely concerned with the appearances of the Military Orders in epic and romantic literature, and not with their appearances in chronicles or other forms of writing which were intended to record actuality" (PDF p. 11 / p. ix). Its findings are literary: the orders "did not play a leading or dominant role in knightly epic and romance literature, but from around 1175 until the latter part of the fifteenth century and later they repeatedly appeared by name in certain conventionalised roles" (PDF p. 44 / p. 28); "their major function was to give credibility to a work by giving it a context in actuality" (PDF p. 251 / p. 235); "The Military Orders did not appear in literature as a model of chivalry" (same page).

The book is serious scholarship (Brill, full apparatus, Cardiff), but its subject does not touch this project. **The wanted book — Nicholson's general history of the orders — is still outstanding**, and Forey, *The Military Orders: From the Twelfth to the Early Fourteenth Centuries* (1992), which Nicholson herself cites as the standard account (PDF p. 20 / p. 4 n. 10), remains the better anchor for the GAP.

## 2. What it CONFIRMS

Only the two-page historical sketch in the Introduction bears on ch01 §II, and it confirms the sentence's components at T2-general level: the three orders were "international"; "Their military operations on the frontiers were supported by a vast network of estates in Europe, from which money, warriors and other supplies were sent to the frontier … the Military Orders also became involved in trade and other economic activities and banking. They also became servants and advisors of popes, kings and nobles throughout Europe" (PDF p. 42 / p. 26); the Teutonic Order "moved its headquarters to Marienburg in Prussia in 1309"; "the destruction of the Teutonic Order's hegemony in Prussia. In 1466, with the Second Treaty of Thorn, the Teutonic Order in Prussia became a vassal of the king of Poland, although its Livonian branch retained considerable independence" (PDF p. 43 / p. 27). On "papally chartered": Innocent II's "great bull of exemption for the Order (1139), 'Omne datum optimum'" (PDF p. 65 / p. 49). On "armed": "It is certainly clear that the Military Orders' military discipline was widely admired" (PDF p. 22 / p. 6, with the chronicle references in n. 15). That is enough for a footnote, not for the [GAP]'s "scholarly statement".

## 3. What it CORRECTS

One imprecision, and it is the manuscript's own dating rule turned on itself. Ch01 §II places its substrate at "Europe around the year 1200" and lists "the military order — Temple, Hospital, the Teutonic Knights with their own territorial state in the making". Nicholson: "The Teutonic Order, which originated during the siege of Acre (1189–91) … also began as a hospice for sick pilgrims and did not become a Military Order until 1198" (PDF p. 42 / p. 26). In 1200 the Teutonic Order was two years militarised and had no Prussian project; the Prussian state "in the making" is a phenomenon of the 1230s onward (the Morris assessment supplies the Golden Bull of Rimini, 1226, and the 1234 papal fief). The same paragraph polices the Hansa for exactly this anachronism. Either re-date the clause ("by the mid-thirteenth century") or drop the Teutonic Knights from the c. 1200 list and keep Temple and Hospital.

## 4. What it ADDS

Very little. One footnote-grade observation for ch01 §II or Appendix A, if wanted: the orders' failure was read by contemporaries as a failure of the *international* form — "when the international Military Orders as a whole failed in their vocation by failing to defend the Holy Land … it was the Templars which were most damaged by this failure. As a result it was the Templars, rather than any other religious order, which in 1307 was attacked by a French government in desperate need of financial resources" (PDF p. 19 / p. 3); and "the Order of the Temple was not dissolved because it was unpopular but to suit the political and financial interests of King Philip" (PDF p. 20 / p. 4). A fiscally pressed territorial monarchy dissolving a supranational armed corporation for its assets is, on the face of it, an early instance of the book's mechanism; but 1307 sits a century before the artillery threshold, and using it would be resemblance, not mechanism — flag [ANALOGY-ONLY] if anyone reaches for it.

## 7. Joint verdict

Belloc changes ch06 §VI's register in one sentence: the couplet is retrieved, is verbatim, and is about a *private* monopoly of the decisive weapon that fails for want of everything behind it — which is the artillery-state mechanism as farce, and should be cited as the "Belloc syndrome" rather than as a knock-out-blow prophecy.
