# Assessment — Charles Tilly, *Coercion, Capital, and European States, AD 990–1990* (Blackwell, 1990)

*Read: the whole main text — Preface, Chapters 1–7 (printed pp. ix–225); References and Index searched, not read. Edition: the corpus file is the 1990 first edition ("AD 990–1990"); the reading list names the 1992 revision, which reworks Chapter 7 — nothing below depends on it, but cite 1990 or obtain 1992. OCR: each printed page is one form-feed page plus a blank, so PDF ≈ 2 × printed + 17–19 (it drifts); both are given. Garbling is worst on pp. 6–16 (a prior reader's marginalia scanned into the text, PDF 33–37) and in every figure and table; one repair is marked. Tilly's own slips, not to import: Henry VII "1484–1509" (p. 156); Edward III dying "1477" (p. 155); Mary Stuart "1586"; Tripoli and Acre falling "to the Ottoman Turks" (p. 145). Role: RIVAL FRAME at the scale of the whole mechanism, T2. Tilly never names Charles VII, the taille, the compagnies d'ordonnance, Castillon, the Bureaux or Commynes.*

## 1. The book in two paragraphs

Tilly's question is double — "What accounts for the great variation over time and space in the kinds of states that have prevailed in Europe since AD 990, and why did European states eventually converge on different variants of the national state?" (p. 5 / PDF 29) — and his answer is a two-variable model: coercion, concentrated in states, and capital, concentrated in cities, combining differently across the continent and driven everywhere by war. The thesis: "extraction and struggle over the means of war created the central organizational structures of states" (p. 57 / PDF 133, the clean repetition of p. 15); "over the long run, far more than other activities, war and preparation for war produced the major components of European states" (p. 28 / PDF 75). Three paths follow from the local mix — "a coercion-intensive, a capital-intensive, and a capitalized coercion path to state formation. They do not represent alternative 'strategies' so much as contrasting conditions of life" (p. 30 / PDF 79): Russia and Brandenburg; Venice, Genoa, the Dutch Republic; France and England.

Convergence is late and decided by scale: the war-making advantage went "to those states that could field standing armies; states having access to a combination of large rural populations, capitalists, and relatively commercialized economies won out" (p. 15 / PDF 49); "From the seventeenth century onward the capitalized coercion form proved more effective in war, and therefore provided a compelling model" (p. 31 / PDF 81). The aphorism "war made the state and the state made war" does not occur in this book; it is from Tilly's 1975 essay in *The Formation of National States in Western Europe* (p. 42). Here it survives only as the title of Chapter 3, "How War Made States, and Vice Versa" (p. 67 / PDF 153), and Tilly disowns the 1975 volume's "new unilinear story… That was wrong. This book attempts to repair the errors of the previous one" (p. 12 / PDF 43).

## 2. What it CONFIRMS

**Ch. 10 §I, §IV, §VII — capital-intensity concentrates coercion in the state; the guns as a department.** The manuscript: artillery "too expensive for the nobility to purchase". Tilly: states concentrate coercion more easily where "production of weapons involves esoteric knowledge, rare materials, or substantial capital" (p. 54 / PDF 127); "every thirteenth-century noble household owned swords, but no twentieth-century household owns an aircraft carrier" (p. 84 / PDF 187). And: "few European states ever manufactured a major share of their own coercive means… Such expensive and dangerous manufactures as gunpowder and cannon constituted the chief exceptions" (p. 84). Chapter 10 §IV's artillery-as-department-of-state is Tilly's sentence.

**Ch. 6 and Ch. 10 §II — cannon, sieges, France and Burgundy.** Firearms "tipped the military advantage toward monarchs who could afford to cast cannon and build the new kinds of fortresses that cannon could not easily shatter. Warfare shifted from battles fought on open plains toward sieges" (p. 76 / PDF 171). Of 1490: "Standing armies had displaced urban militias in France and Burgundy, but few other realms" (p. 46 / PDF 111) — the manuscript's pair, named as the pair.

**Ch. 10 §VIII and Ch. 6 — "buy, license, or align".** "Since war pays off on effectiveness rather than efficiency, they gave smaller neighbors hard choices: mount the same sort of military effort at great cost, accept conquest, or find a safe subordinate niche" (p. 190 / PDF 399).

**Ch. 10 §VI — the Swiss control group.** "the Swiss began substituting the training and export of mercenaries for the conduct of their own wars" (p. 80 / PDF 179); Marignano, 1515, "helped persuade the Swiss to avoid wars of their own, but it did not keep them from shipping mercenaries to other people's battles" (p. 82 / PDF 183).

**Ch. 4 — condottieri, Sforza, Venice.** Hawkwood's pension and fresco (p. 80 / PDF 179); Colleoni's fortune "comparable to the riches of… Cosimo de'Medici" (p. 80, from Lane); "The condottieri were the oilmen of their time" (p. 80); Sforza "became duke of Milan in 1450" (p. 65 / PDF 149); the city-states relegated by "that era of large armies, heavy artillery, and extensive fortifications" (p. 65; OCR "lary ies" repaired). "Venice secreted no bureaucracy" (p. 147 / PDF 313); the funded debt "From the thirteenth century onward" (p. 148).

**Ch. 8 §VII and Ch. 11 §II — Tudor subordination.** "the Tudors suppressed private armies, reduced the princely power of great lords along the Scottish border, contained aristocratic violence, and eliminated the fortress-castles" (p. 69 / PDF 157, from Stone 1965); Henry VII and the later Tudors "reduce the great lords' private armies" (p. 156 / PDF 331).

**Ch. 3 §I — "Tilly's protection-market frame"** is pinnable here: "rulers resembled racketeers" (p. 75 / PDF 169). **Ch. 12 §III** — legibility: in commercialised economies "Participants in markets already do a significant share of the requisite surveillance" (p. 89 / PDF 197).

## 3. What it CORRECTS

1. **Spine §8(a) and the intake instruction treat "war made the state and the state made war" as the Tilly formulation to differentiate from.** *Coercion, Capital* does not contain the sentence (Chapter 3's title, p. 67, is the nearest), and Tilly repudiates the unilinear version (p. 12). Quote it from Tilly 1975, p. 42; differentiate in Ch. 10 from the 1990 model — three paths, brokerage, convergence-by-scale — not from a slogan he had abandoned.

2. **Manuscript (Ch. 10 §III; spine §8(a)) uses "ratchet" as its own term.** Tilly p. 89 / PDF 197: "Some historians speak of a 'ratchet effect' by which an inflated wartime budget fails to return to its prewar level (Peacock and Wiseman 1961…)". An established term with a different referent — budget stickiness, not the irreversibility of a settlement; declare the collision in a note.

3. **Reading list says "AD 990–1992"; the corpus copy is 1990.** Cite accordingly.

Nothing else is contradicted on fact; Tilly carries no fifteenth-century French detail to contradict with.

## 4. What it ADDS

**Ch. 10 §III — the third voice.** On Elias's twin monopoly of force and tax: "The missing member, credit, links the military monopoly to the monopoly of taxation" (p. 85 / PDF 189). The triad — capability, finance, administration — has no credit leg; the fiscal-settlement research order should ask what Charles VII and Louis XI borrowed, from whom, on what security. Primary colour: Balsac, 1502 — "success in war depends on having enough money to provide whatever the enterprise needs" (p. 84 / PDF 187, from Hale).

**Ch. 10 §VII and Coda §I — Tilly's falsification checklist and his rule on missing variables.** Pp. 35–36 (PDF 89–91) list six conditions under which the argument fails — Appendix C's precedent. Then: "It would be illegitimate to complain that the argument neglects variables the critic happens to regard as important: physical environment, ideology, military technology, or something else. The missing-variable criticism only becomes legitimate when the critic shows that neglect of the variable causes a false reading of relationships among variables that do appear in the argument" (p. 36 / PDF 91). The exact test the differentiation must pass; see §5.

**Ch. 10 §VIII — seize, make, or buy.** Rulers "could seize them, make them, or buy them… which restated the question as a choice between seizing and buying the means of production instead of the product" (p. 84 / PDF 187). "This state is buying the foundry" is Tilly's third option applied to the means of production.

**Ch. 12 §II and Appendix C — what happens to polities that buy the decisive layer.** Chapter 7: "the process of negotiation and containment of the military that occurred widely in the West may not be occurring, because states acquire their military means from great powers outside the state, in return for commodities or political subordination" (p. 218 / PDF 455; cf. pp. 200–01). A T2 prediction for the interstices: a polity that licenses or aligns gets not a small Tudor settlement but praetorianism.

**Ch. 1 / Appendix A / Ch. 12 — Tilly as neomedievalist, 1990.** "powerful rivals to states — blocs of states such as NATO, the European Economic Community or the Warsaw Pact, world-wide networks of traders in expensive, illicit commodities… and financial organizations such as giant international oil companies — have emerged to challenge their sovereignty… states as we know them will not last forever" (pp. 3–4 / PDF 25–27); the Envoi: "corporations whose capital is everywhere and nowhere… The state system Europeans fashioned has not always existed. It will not endure forever" (p. 225 / PDF 469). His rivals include "blocs of states" — the units the manuscript casts as successors.

**Ch. 4 §VI and Ch. 12 §IV (the EU).** Capital-intensive states' military advantage "was historically great for naval warfare, less so for artillery and cavalry, and a long-term drawback in mass-army tactics" (p. 62 / PDF 143): Venice and the Union weakest exactly at the artillery layer — the Depreter test in Tilly's words.

**Ch. 11 and Ch. 12 §IV — three footnotes.** Philip II by 1580: "the manufacture of gunpowder was a royal monopoly" (p. 56 / PDF 131), followed within a half-century by a return to contracting — Parrott's oscillation in the ally's text. Henry VIII "took on the obligation to provide stipends forever" to the conforming clergy (p. 103 / PDF 225). And America "sets itself apart from all other Western countries" by never disarming its civilians (p. 69 / PDF 157).

## 5. What it CONTRADICTS

**(i) The motor is war in general; what converged the forms was manpower and capital at scale, not the gun.** "the changing character of war gave the military advantage to states that could draw large, durable military forces from their own populations" (p. 63 / PDF 145); "The city-state and city-empire lost out, however, once mass armies recruited from the state's own population became crucial" (p. 64 / PDF 147). France and Spain in the late fifteenth century financed "wars fought by means of expensive fortifications, artillery and, above all, mercenary soldiers" (p. 183 / PDF 385) — artillery ranked *below* mercenaries. "Only the great sixteenth-century expansion in the scale and expense of international wars… gave national states a definitive advantage" (p. 224 / PDF 467). And technology is a variable he declines by rule (p. 36). This reaches spine §8(a) and the DEFEND-tier mechanism in §6.

**(ii) The periodisation puts the whole "artillery state" inside an age of brokers.** "brokerage: an era (roughly 1400 to 1700 in important parts of Europe) when mercenary forces recruited by contractors predominated in military activity, and rulers relied heavily on formally independent capitalists for loans… and for installation and collection of taxes" (p. 29 / PDF 77). "From the fifteenth to seventeenth centuries — the critical period for European state formation — armies deployed through much of Europe consisted largely of mercenaries" (p. 81 / PDF 181). "The shift from brokerage to nationalization begins with the Thirty Years' War" (p. 122 / PDF 263); France left mercenaries "during the seventeenth" century (p. 106 / PDF 231). France's fiscal consolidation is Henry IV's — "from negotiation to enforced payment of taxes" (p. 87 / PDF 193) — and its penetration Richelieu's intendants, improvised "in desperation" (p. 26 / PDF 71). "Before 1400… no state had a national budget"; regularisation comes "During the sixteenth century" (p. 74 / PDF 167). On this chronology Chapter 10's audited accounts of 1445–94 and Chapter 11's "domestication… 1470 and 1530" are a century and a half early.

**(iii) The intermediaries were not suppressed until 1789.** "no European state (except, perhaps, Sweden) made a serious attempt to institute direct rule from top to bottom until the era of the French Revolution" (p. 25 / PDF 69). "Before the seventeenth century, every large European state ruled its subjects through powerful intermediaries who enjoyed significant autonomy" (p. 104 / PDF 227). "After 1750" states moved to direct rule, "sweeping away autonomous intermediaries in the process" (pp. 103–04); "Thus national standing armies, national states, and direct rule caused each other" (p. 104). CLAUDE.md §2 ends the chain in "suppression of intermediate authorities"; Ch. 10 §I says "suppressing every authority between the crown and the taxable subject". Tilly says that is the Revolution's work.

**(iv) Inadvertence.** National states' components "usually formed as more or less inadvertent by-products" of armed force (p. 26 / PDF 71); Ch. 10 §III's crown "definitive in intent" by 1445–46 cuts against this.

**(v) Multiple paths and the capital-intensive survivor — the objection that reaches the wager.** The capital-intensive path "warred without building vast permanent state structures" (p. 30); "the Dutch Republic, with very small land forces, became Europe's leading state during the seventeenth century" (p. 76 / PDF 171); Venice and the Dutch "competed effectively with major land powers for another century" (p. 183). Convergence took until the nineteenth century (pp. 46–47). Chapter 12 runs all four candidates down one path on a fifteen-year horizon and scores the EU as a binary. Tilly's model holds a third outcome for a capital-rich, coercion-poor polity: effectiveness in the kind of war its capital suits, for a century, without the apparatus. This touches spine §8(b) and the EU conditional in §5.

**(vi) The present read as dispersal** (pp. 3–4, 225, quoted above). The founder-scale ally on the mechanism is, on the prediction, on the other side.

**The book's best answer.** On (i) and (ii): the differentiation is real and stronger than spine §8(a) states, because Tilly's account has a hole exactly where the manuscript's evidence is thickest. He concedes cannon-casting monarchs from c. 1350 (p. 76) and standing armies in France and Burgundy by 1490 (p. 46), yet his France begins consolidating c. 1600; Charles VII does not appear. His own rule (p. 36) admits the technology variable if its neglect produces a false reading — and it does: war-and-extraction alone cannot say why Charles V's paid force and the 1360s levies were reversed by 1380 while the settlement of 1439–46 held for three centuries, and his brokerage era has no room for a crown already paying its army quarterly through its own treasurers by 1445. The manuscript supplies the irreversibility his model lacks. That answer is good — but it must concede his larger point: the *convergence* of European forms was decided by sixteenth- to eighteenth-century scale; the mechanism explains the first consolidators, not why everyone became like them, and Part III should say it is a claim about consolidators. On (iii): a wording change the book cannot avoid. Chapters 10–11 document subordination of the intermediaries' autonomous fiscal-military capacity — bonds, absorbed orders, licensed retaining, ranked courts — not their elimination; Ch. 12 §II already has the Tudor state building "councils *for*" the marches, and Tilly grants the Tudors exactly that (p. 69). "Suppressing" should become "subordinating" in Ch. 10 §I and CLAUDE.md §2 — a doctrine sentence, so Roderick's. On (iv): co-evolution concedes most of it; the 1446 "until the conclusion of peace" formula is Tilly's inadvertence and Contamine's intent at once. On (v): weaker, and not yet made. The manuscript must say whether the compute-led stack has the property Tilly assigns to mass armies (which ended the Dutch niche) or to navies (which sustained it): if decisive force can today be bought and fielded by a capital-rich polity without a single fisc, Tilly predicts a durable EU on the Dutch pattern, and the Depreter test is not a binary. On (vi): Bull's test applies to Tilly — a list of dispersal without a mechanism for its end — and Chapter 1 gains a second founder to inherit and arm.

## 7. Verdict

Tilly does not change the book's direction — his capital-intensity, his state-made cannon, his "safe subordinate niche" and his Tudor subordination all run with the manuscript — but he changes its register in two places: Chapter 10 §VII must differentiate from the 1990 model rather than the 1975 slogan, and Chapter 12 §IV must answer his capital-intensive path, which predicts a durable Dutch-pattern EU rather than the Depreter binary. The single most important thing the author should know is that *Coercion, Capital* contains no French fifteenth century at all — no Charles VII, no taille, no compagnies, no Castillon; Tilly's France consolidates under Henry IV and Richelieu, inside an age he calls brokerage — so the "differentiation from Tilly" is not a refinement of his account but a claim about a period he skips, and by his own rule on missing variables (p. 36) it stands only if the book shows what it can: that war-and-extraction alone cannot explain why the 1360s settlement was reversed and the 1440s one was not. One wording must change to survive him: the crown of the 1440s–1530s subordinated its intermediaries; on Tilly's evidence nobody suppressed them before 1789.
