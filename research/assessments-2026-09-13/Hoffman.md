# Assessment — Philip T. Hoffman, *Why Did Europe Conquer the World?* (Princeton, 2015)

*Read: the whole main text (Chapters 1–7, printed pp. 1–214) and Appendix C; Appendices A, B, D, E skimmed for algebra only; bibliography and index searched, not read. The text is clean apart from soft-hyphen residue, scrambled figure labels and the edition's own typos ("in in"). Pins: printed page first, PDF page in brackets; PDF = printed + 9 throughout the main text. Role: RIVAL MECHANISM, T2. Hoffman never cites Contamine, Curry, Vale or DeVries 1998; he does cite Rogers 1993, Hall 1997, Guenée 1971 and Henneman 1976.*

## 1. The book in two paragraphs

Hoffman asks why western Europeans, poor and backward in 900, controlled 35 per cent of the globe by 1800 and 84 per cent by 1914, and answers that they alone sustained four centuries of improvement in "the gunpowder technology" — defined deliberately broadly to include organisation, supply and finance: "Leaving part of the technology out in order to focus on the weapons alone would be a bit like trying to analyze the impact of computers by considering only the hardware and ignoring software and the Internet" (p. 7 [PDF 16]). The engine is a tournament: "Between the late Middle Ages (1300–1500) and the nineteenth century, Europe witnessed a tournament with just as much intensity and commitment… To snatch the prize, they raised taxes and lavished resources on armies and navies that used the gunpowder technology and advanced it by learning from their mistakes" (p. 15 [PDF 24]). Four conditions are necessary and jointly sufficient: frequent war, heavy spending (a valuable prize and low, similar "political costs" of mobilising resources), heavy use of the new technology rather than an old one, and few obstacles to adopting rivals' innovations (p. 48 [PDF 57]). Western Europe met all four continuously from 1400; China, Japan, India, Russia and the Ottomans each failed at least one.

The ultimate cause is political history: "Political history is then the ultimate cause here, but that means that the outcome was not at all preordained" (p. 16 [PDF 25]). A millennium without strong states after Rome bred "parochial altruism" and enduring fragmentation; the papacy blocked any hegemon; and some rulers — "the late medieval kings of France" first among them — "were learning how to lower their own political costs of mobilizing resources" by winning permanent taxation (p. 121 [PDF 130]). Chapter 6 extends the model to the nineteenth century's armed peace, where research replaces learning-by-doing and innovation accelerates without war; Chapter 7 concludes that after 1945 western Europe "did just what the tournament model would predict and sat out the arms race" (p. 205 [PDF 214]).

## 2. What it CONFIRMS

**Ch. 6 §III and Ch. 10 §IV — the protagonist is an institution, not a gadget.** The manuscript: "The protagonist of this chapter is not a gadget. It is a payroll." Hoffman, from Hall 1997: "the armies of French King Charles VII (1422–1461) developed a highly effective artillery service during the Hundred Years War that helped drive the English out of the strongholds they occupied in France. The advances, though, did not stop at war's end. During the war, they came primarily in logistics and the organization of sieges. But afterward, or at the very end, the French also adopted better gunpowder and began using cast-iron cannon balls and the gun carriages" (p. 39 [PDF 48]; n. 44, p. 40 [PDF 49], citing Hall 1997: 115–22 and DeVries & Smith 2012: 154–55). This independently carries the manuscript's sequencing — organisation first, iron shot and carriages after — and its refusal to make the Bureaux inventors.

**Ch. 6 §V — the political cascade as evidence for, not against, the weapon.** The manuscript answers Curry's "Normandy fell politically" with the transmission-belt argument. Hoffman makes the identical move for Cortés's allies: "Their decision to ally with him was in fact clear evidence of the power of his technology, not a sign that it was irrelevant" (p. 11 [PDF 20]).

**Ch. 10 §VII and Ch. 12 §IV — the entry price and the polities that "buy, license, or align".** The manuscript: "the only entities that could pay it were the ones we would shortly begin calling states". Hoffman formalises this as the fixed cost b: great powers "may spend the revenue on expanding their fiscal bureaucracy or on building a bigger army or navy. That will raise the fixed cost for any newcomer who wants to enter the ranks of the great powers" (p. 141 [PDF 150]); "Intimidated by them, weaker rulers within Europe will bow out of military competition" (p. 141 [PDF 150]).

**Ch. 10 §III — the pre-existence of fiscal capacity that the guns made decisive.** The manuscript's ratchet requires that capacity existed "before the guns and had not, before the guns, been decisive". Hoffman: late medieval rulers "had established (often with the help of representative institutions) their realms' first permanent taxation—export duties, salt and hearth taxes, and impositions on income or assets" (p. 54 [PDF 63], citing Guenée 1971), and Europe "did get strong states, at just the moment when the gunpowder technology had enormous potential for improvement" (p. 131 [PDF 140]).

**Ch. 6 §VII — capital-intensity.** "The gunpowder technology (which substituted physical and human capital for military manpower)" (p. 155 [PDF 164]); "weapons and the gunpowder technology in general were capital-intensive" (p. 98 [PDF 107]).

**Ch. 12 §II and Coda §I — Cerny's absent variable, formalised.** Hoffman's Tokugawa case is the control: once the shogun "had become hegemon within Japan itself… The tournament model would predict that the shogun would then stop devoting resources to war and cease advancing the gunpowder technology too. And taxes would therefore fall" (p. 82 [PDF 91]) — which happened (p. 84 [PDF 93]).

**Ch. 12 §V and Coda §V — the mutually observed pause.** Under armed peace rulers "will arm themselves and watch one another warily" (p. 189 [PDF 198]) — "Europe's nineteenth-century equivalent to the Cold War" (p. 196 [PDF 205]).

## 3. What it CORRECTS

1. **Manuscript (Chapter 10 §III) says** "The constitutional theory of French taxation before 1439 was consent: the Estates granted, for a purpose and a term, and the grant lapsed"; **Hoffman p. 135 [PDF 144] says** the French kings "gained the right to impose permanent taxes during the Hundred Years War… after a disastrous French defeat in 1356, when King John II of France was taken prisoner… his son, who became King Charles V in 1364, managed to get the levies increased and made permanent in the 1360s. He did so by tailoring the taxes to suit the powerful nobility and, even more important, by showing that he could use the money effectively to provide the public good of security" (citing Henneman 1976, the manuscript's own [GAP: Henneman]); Montpellier's per-household take "may have jumped 21-fold between 1320–1333 and 1368–1370" (p. 136 [PDF 145]). Hoffman omits the 1380 deathbed abolition of the *fouages*, so his "permanent" is overstated; but the manuscript's "before 1439… consent" is imprecise by his account and must be rewritten as the *second* making of permanence, the first having been reversed. Chapter 10 §II already concedes Charles V's disbanded army; it must concede his tax too.

2. **Manuscript (Chapter 6 §VII, Chapter 10 §VII, Coda §I) says** "the price of decisive siege capability moved sharply within a datable window" and "Prices, however, are facts"; **Hoffman Tables 2.5–2.6 (pp. 61–62 [PDF 70–71]) say** English artillery prices relative to factor costs *fell* at 1.4 per cent a year over 1382–1439 (2.4 per cent against spades), and Frankfurt handguns showed productivity growth of "3.0 percent a year between 1399 and 1431, an impressive figure by modern standards and astounding for the end of the Middle Ages" (p. 63 [PDF 72]). The unit price of the gun fell in exactly the manuscript's window; what rose was the cost of the *establishment*. Wherever the draft says "price of decisive force" unqualified it should say establishment or entry cost — the 1442 document already measures the right thing.

Nothing else in the chapters read is contradicted on fact.

## 4. What it ADDS

**Ch. 10 §III — a continental parallel for the army-as-ratchet.** Brandenburg: the Great Elector got "a temporary tax increase" from the Estates, "joined a war between Sweden and Poland (1655–1660) and invoked the fighting to raise taxes even higher, a decision he imposed unilaterally. After the war, his enlarged army quashed resistance to making the tax increases permanent" (p. 136 [PDF 145]). This is the 1439–46 sequence — emergency grant, practice, irreversibility secured by the force the grant bought — run again two centuries later, and it closes the manuscript's want of a comparator for the ratchet itself.

**Ch. 10 §IV and Ch. 6 §IV (Fougères) — institutional learning as tacit and losable.** Koxinga's heirs, four years after taking Fort Zeelandia with European siege works, "proved incapable of capturing a new fort the Dutch had erected… They had apparently forgotten how to build European siege works" (p. 78 [PDF 87]). This is the argument for why a train is a career service (the twenty-year cannoneers of 1469–91), and why a ducal train at Fougères was not the royal one.

**Ch. 10 §VIII — three footnotes.** The complementary-skills cluster: "It would be a bit like trying to re-create, say, Silicon Valley somewhere else" (p. 163 [PDF 172]). The research/procurement split, on torpedoes: "Research and procurement were thus becoming distinct parts of defense contracts… as in modern defense contracting" (p. 201 [PDF 210]) — the precedent for the section's conceded disanalogy. The cheap layer under the monopoly: "in early seventeenth-century Paris or London, two or three weeks work would buy even a poor, unskilled day laborer a matchlock musket" (p. 157 [PDF 166]). (Hoffman does not supply the Hussite prehistory; that GAP stays open.)

**Ch. 11 — the janissary trap.** "What Machiavelli considered a source of strength… ultimately proved a devastating weakness, for the Ottoman sultans therefore never got the permanent tax levies that the negotiation ultimately gave their western counterparts" (p. 148 [PDF 157]). The Ottoman "most radical case" needs this caution: a force monopoly that bypasses bargaining forecloses the fisc.

**Ch. 12 §II — the geometry of blocs.** Winners did not absorb big losers because "ingesting an entire big country risked provoking unmanageable resistance" (p. 119 [PDF 128]) — "inside blocs, not across them," from the economist's side.

**Coda §V — the armed peace as precedent, with its finding.** Great-power military spending "rose on average at a 1.7 percent per year rate between 1816 and 1913" (p. 191 [PDF 200]), and "The armed peace in the nineteenth century could then do more to improve gunpowder technology than the incessant war of the early modern period" (p. 195 [PDF 204]). The Coda's confessed limit has a documented precedent in which the tournament ran on regardless.

**Ch. 2 (outside brief) —** the papacy as centrifugal force: "the papacy strove to keep the Holy Roman emperor—or any other ruler—from permanently reassembling Charlemagne's empire" (p. 132 [PDF 141]).

## 5. What it CONTRADICTS

**(i) The engine.** Hoffman's causation runs the other way from the manuscript's. Technology is the *output* of spending: "each unit of resources spent on war gives a ruler a chance at finding a military innovation" (p. 43 [PDF 52]); "Innovation is then an inadvertent by-product of fighting wars" (p. 44 [PDF 53]). The fisc is the output of war, not of the gun: "Spending on war gives a ruler a chance at lowering his variable cost of mobilizing resources, either politically or via financial innovation" (p. 138 [PDF 147]). And the gun is decisive in his story only because it was *new*: rulers "must use the gunpowder technology heavily, and not older military technologies" because "A new technology… has enormous potential for improvement via learning by doing" (pp. 46, 48 [PDF 55, 57]). There is no threshold, no minimum price of sovereignty, no moment at which anything became irreversible; the French permanent tax is a 1360s political bargain (p. 135 [PDF 144]) made six decades before a siege train mattered, and the artillery service of Charles VII is one instance of learning among many. His version of the manuscript's whole Part II is one sentence: Europe "did get strong states, at just the moment when the gunpowder technology had enormous potential for improvement" (p. 131 [PDF 140]) — *at just the moment*: coincidence, not coupling. This reaches spine §8(a) (the ratchet) and the DEFEND-tier mechanism in §6, and it is exactly the Tilly-general "war made the state" that §8(a) says the book is differentiated from.

**(ii) Consolidation kills the tournament.** "if one of these monarchs had somehow annihilated the others and become a European hegemon, then learning by doing would have screeched to a stop, for no one would have dared challenge the hegemon" (p. 66 [PDF 75]). Fragmentation is the *cause* of Europe's lead. A book whose title event is a Tudor consolidation, and which predicts consolidation inside blocs, is arguing for the thing Hoffman's model says ends innovation — unless the inter-bloc contest is preserved.

**(iii) The private-budget inversion.** "if spending by these private entrepreneurs had been the only driver of learning by doing, then western Europe would never have developed a lead… for the entrepreneurs' budgets were minuscule compared to the rulers'" — the Dutch state's military spending in 1609 was "over 16 times what the East India Company was spending" and "nearly 50 times" in the 1630s (p. 160 and n. 14 [PDF 169]). Chapter 10 §VIII concedes the inverse today: private American AI investment "$109.1 billion in 2024" dwarfs the public lines. On Hoffman's model the learning is where the spending is — in the platforms — and the state is the minor partner.

**(iv) Europe sits out.** After 1945 "most of the western European powers did just what the tournament model would predict and sat out the arms race… the United States would provide for their security, allowing them to free ride" (p. 205 [PDF 214]). The model's prediction for a polity with high political costs and a patron is non-entry, not a "live conditional".

**The book's best answer.** On (i): the spine already concedes co-evolution and forbids "the gun conjured the fisc"; what the manuscript claims and Hoffman lacks is a mechanism of *irreversibility*. Hoffman assumes it — "political learning or financial innovation is usually not forgotten: once the political costs fall, nothing short of financial crisis, revolution, or other major exogenous political cataclysm will raise them again" (p. 140 [PDF 149]) — and his own French case refutes the assumption, since the 1360s levies *were* reversed in 1380 and had to be remade in 1439–46. The manuscript supplies what he assumes; and his fixed cost b, raised for everyone else by the great powers' spending, is the ratchet at inter-state scale. This answer is good, and naming him strengthens it. On (ii): the wager is for three or four blocs, not one — the tournament survives at bloc scale, and Hoffman's armed-peace chapter shows innovation surviving a century of non-war; but the manuscript must then say that a single winner would falsify the mechanism, and Appendix C should score it. On (iii): the answer is weaker. The manuscript's claim is about *control* of the decisive layer, Hoffman's about spending as the source of learning; both can be true, but the draft has not said why control migrates to the smaller spender, and in the fifteenth century it did not have to, because the crown was the larger spender. This is where Hoffman bites hardest and the draft should say so. On (iv): the spine's own answer — consolidation is compelled only where a polity wants to be decisive; the EU's free-riding is the model's prediction and the Depreter test scores whether it changes — is adequate, provided Chapter 12 states the free-rider equilibrium as Hoffman's prediction and the book's falsifier.

## 6. Placement

- **Ch. 10 §VII** — engage BY NAME in the text as the rival mechanism, T2. The section already stages Curry's reversal; Hoffman is that reversal formalised, and the answer (irreversibility; b as the ratchet; the 1380 reversal his model cannot explain) belongs in the text. The central causal chapter cannot leave its best-credentialled rival in a footnote.
- **Ch. 10 §III** — Henneman via Hoffman on the 1360s, in text as the settlement's first, reversed, making; Brandenburg as the ratchet's comparator, a sentence.
- **Ch. 6 §III, §V, §VII** — footnotes (Hall via Hoffman; the Cortés-allies passage beside the Curry answer); the "hardware… software and the Internet" line may be quoted in §III. Correct the "price" vocabulary in §VII.
- **Ch. 10 §VIII** — footnotes as above; engage by name on the private-budget inversion (contradiction iii).
- **Ch. 12 §IV, §V; Coda §V** — engage by name on the EU as the tournament's free-rider prediction; the armed peace as precedent (ally).
- **Coda §I** — one sentence naming Hoffman's four conditions beside the book's three, as the rival "mechanism under conditions" from which the book differs by the ratchet.
- **Ch. 11** — footnote: the janissary trap; the domestication of military entrepreneurs (Wallenstein, p. 162 [PDF 171]). **Ch. 2** — footnote: the papacy.
- **Appendix C** — consider a scored falsifier: a single bloc achieving hegemony would, on Hoffman's model, end the innovation the stack depends on.

## 7. Verdict

Hoffman does not change the book's direction — his evidence runs with the manuscript's on capital-intensity, the entry price, the institutional character of the train and the bloc geometry — but he changes its register: the rival engine is now a Princeton economic historian with price series, and Chapter 10 must meet him by name, not absorb him as another conceding sceptic. The single most important thing the author should know is that Hoffman dates France's first permanent taxation to Charles V in the 1360s (Henneman) and runs his whole account without a threshold or a ratchet — so the manuscript's differentiation must rest, explicitly, on the irreversibility Hoffman merely assumes (p. 140) and his own French case disproves, and it must stop saying "price" where it means establishment cost, because Hoffman's tables show the unit price of artillery falling through the very decades the book calls the repricing.
