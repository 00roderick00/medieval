# Assessment — David Parrott, *The Business of War: Military Enterprise and Military Revolution in Early Modern Europe* (Cambridge, 2012)

## 1. The book in two paragraphs

Parrott's subject is "military enterprise – warfare organized and waged by private contractors – in early modern Europe (c.1500–1700)" (p. 1 / PDF 21); his target is the war-and-state-formation tradition from Hintze and Weber through Roberts to Tilly. The sentence the council reported is exact, at p. 2 (PDF 22): "Underpinning the argument of this book is a simple proposition: the maintenance of wholly state-recruited and state-administered military force is an anomalous development over the broader course of European history. An explicit drive to establish fully state-controlled armies and navies, and the maintenance of a closely controlled monopoly of force, is a particular preoccupation of European states from roughly 1760 to 1960." The counter-norm follows: "The characteristic pattern of European warfare from the world of the Greek city-states to the ancien régime of the eighteenth century, and once again during the past half-century down to the present day, is military organization on the basis of contracts with private suppliers … This rarely means total military devolution, more often … varying forms of public–private partnership" (pp. 2–3). And the theoretical claim: "there is no necessary incompatibility between the growth of the power of the state and the development of a substantial sphere of private military activity; indeed, the latter made possible a robustness and organizational 'reach' that would otherwise have been unattainable to government authorities" (p. 8; restated p. 320).

The mechanism he substitutes for technology is duration. "Much more than technological or tactical developments, it is arguable that the lengthening of periods of continuous warfare – a process which started in the 1550s … and culminated in the Thirty Years War – was the key early modern 'revolution in military affairs' … The 'revolution in military affairs' was essentially about the rise of attritional war" (pp. 76–77). Long war made the contractor's credit necessary and financeable; the Thirty Years War produced "a spectrum" of partnerships (pp. 102–3), of which Bavarian "restricted enterprise", not Wallenstein, was optimal (p. 115); after 1650 rulers asserted command as a "mark of sovereignty" while venality and supply contracting carried the finance (ch. 6); and the eclipse after 1760 was contingent — killing-power, the nation-in-arms, "putting the 'fiscality' back into the military-fiscal state" (pp. 317–27). His last sentence: contemporary governments have made "a widespread and comprehensive return to military outsourcing and the military contractor-state" (p. 327).

## 2. What it CONFIRMS

**Ch4 §III–IV, the Sforza checklist and Prigozhin.** Manuscript: "the Sforza move from below is closed … because the modern strong state holds the market for force too completely — it is the paymaster, the arsenal, the recruiter and the court." Parrott, on the general contractors of the Thirty Years War: "No more than in Renaissance Italy was it possible to survive for long as an independent, self-sustaining and predatory military force without the sponsorship of one of the belligerent powers" (p. 105); the independent contract army "lacked even medium-term durability" (p. 135); "There are remarkably few Wallensteins in the story of military contracting" (p. 308). Wallenstein's own Sforza move — "his Imperial investiture as duke of Mecklenburg in 1628" — "was halted by the disintegration of his political position in 1629" (p. 120; motive at p. 250). The checklist survives its largest early-modern test.

**Ch3 §IV, the paymaster disanalogy.** Manuscript: "the modern contractor cannot sustain itself against its paymaster for a fiscal quarter." Parrott on the Dutch States General, who paid colonels as employees: "they saw clear political advantages in keeping them dependent on the provinces as paymasters" (pp. 112–13); and on the sixteenth century: "without the advances provided by the rulers' revenues or his access to credit, very few of the mercenary commanders could have afforded to recruit and equip their own units" (p. 78). See §5(v) for the twist.

**Ch10 §VI, the Swiss as control group.** Manuscript: pike squares "forced no institution-building"; "the first formal Franco-Swiss troop convention following in 1521." Parrott: after Burgundy the Swiss neglected artillery and "by the early sixteenth century they had fallen substantially behind in terms of both technology and tactical thinking about the uses of firepower" (p. 47); "it was not until 1521 that the French king managed to negotiate an agreement for the levy of Swiss troops" (p. 51). Marignano, 1515 — French "superiority in numbers and firepower", 8,000–9,000 Swiss dead (pp. 27–28) — is the Swiss meeting the artillery state.

**Ch10 §II, the militia road not taken.** Manuscript: the francs-archers "tried … and discarded in favour of the payroll." Parrott: "the project of establishing militias was an almost unmitigated failure wherever it was attempted" (p. 99), the French legions of the 1530s included.

**Ch10 §III, permanence made in practice.** Manuscript: "'until the peace' had quietly become 'always'." Parrott, on the 1650s: rulers "used the experience of war taxes imposed during the Thirty Years War as a bargaining counter to obtain permanent tax grants from their subjects that would allow them to establish small permanent armies" (p. 21); Brandenburg's Estates granted "530,000 talers … for six years from 1653", and "in a great break with previous practice, instead of using these revenues to hire troops under contract, they were deployed to boost the number of permanent troops" (p. 284); "there was no going back to the minimalist tax regimes of the pre-1618 era" (p. 283). The ratchet of 1439–46, re-run two centuries later, reported by the historian least inclined to see it.

**Ch10 §II.** "The permanent forces of the king of France from the mid-fifteenth century numbered around 6,000 men" (p. 30; n. 8: Potter's 1,500 lances) — consistent with the manuscript's establishment.

## 3. What it CORRECTS

**Ch11 §V says: "domestication of the market for force — by licence, absorption, or monopoly purchase … Between roughly 1470 and 1530, England, France, Spain and the Ottoman state each executed variants."** Parrott's Part I says the opposite for the market in men: "The decades from 1500 to 1550 saw the largest percentage increase in the military establishments of European states before the second half of the seventeenth century" (p. 73), and the increase was contracted — 23,000 Landsknechte in François I's army at Marignano; 19,000 German infantry and 8,200 Reiter in Henri II's 40,500 at Pierrepont, 1558 (p. 59); the tercios viejos "usually between 10 and 15 per cent" of the Army of Flanders in the 1570s–80s (p. 89). The one licensing prosecution — Vogelsberger, executed 1548 — "had no real impact on subsequent willingness to continue contracting abroad" (p. 58). The defensible form of move three is licence and monopsony over a *growing* contracted market, with absorption confined to a permanent core; Ottoman direct provisioning of "around 18,500 troops in 1527 and rising to 75,000 in 1609" (p. 199) is the genuine outlier.

**Ch10 §II carries (flagged tertiary) "some 24,000 men by 1483."** Parrott: "no European ruler … could hope to maintain a permanent, full-sized campaign army of even 10,000–20,000 men from ordinary income" (p. 30). The flag is reinforced; the figure should not survive without Contamine at source.

**Ch4 §II, "Venice, the most institutionally careful buyer"** — Parrott hedges: "Florence … was in fact not far behind" (p. 45).

Nothing else in chapters 3, 4, 10, 11 or 12 is contradicted on a fact or date.

## 4. What it ADDS

**The paymaster's confession, for ch3 §III or ch11 move three.** Philip III to his Council of State, 1607: "no one would be more eager than I to dispense with [military] contracts if that were possible" (p. 96). The Council's vocabulary — *administración* versus *asiento*, "the shame and dangers of relying on asiento" argued from "the crown's sovereignty and its reputation" (pp. 197, 265) — is the early-modern form of the manuscript's provision/sovereignty distinction.

**Paymaster equals policy, for ch4 §IV.** "Karl von Harrach, could coolly inform the Emperor that His Majesty would only be able to dictate the policy of Wallenstein's army if he himself paid for its running costs" (1626; p. 125). Beside Prigozhin's ammunition videos, the best single early-modern line for the chapter.

**The state running it worse, for ch12 §IV's "honest half".** Philip II's 1570s direct administration of "around 100 of the 146 galleys" "proved a disaster, costing almost twice as much per galley as the Genoese contracts", reversed by the mid-1580s (p. 82).

**The technology that forced ownership, for ch10 §VII and ch12 §IV.** Parrott concedes one case where the manuscript's mechanism ran exactly as described: "The decisive factor here was not some ideologically motivated decision to take direct control of navies, but a technological change in warfare at sea, in its decisiveness akin to the role of siege artillery and the trace italienne" (p. 290). Rated warships could not double as merchantmen; "the most obvious private–public funding mechanism for operating navies would no longer work. These ships would only come into existence if the ruler was prepared to spend heavily from tax revenues to build, arm and crew them" (p. 291; counts in Table 6.1, p. 293). The sceptic conceding — it belongs in the book's sceptics-concede signature.

**Devolution as hollow crown, for ch5; reassertion, for ch11.** "By the late 1580s almost all the territory north of a line from Nantes to Lyon was paying taxes to the Catholic League rather than the crown … In some cases they defied one of the crown's most fundamental marks of sovereignty by minting their own coins" (p. 88). Then Bossuet, echoing Hobbes: "in the truly godly and well-governed state, the king alone would be armed" (p. 272); and Article 65 of Münster (1648) defining princely sovereignty "by their rights to raise armies, make alliances and decide on war and peace" (p. 285). For ch3 §III: Brown & Root/Halliburton (pp. 323–24) and Clerke's Act 1782 (p. 325).

**Cheap cannon, for ch10 §VIII's diffusion paragraph.** "The main military-technological innovation of the later sixteenth and seventeenth century, the cast-iron cannon"; "in 1630 alone the Trips imported 1,182 cannon from Sweden" (pp. 201–2). The projectile's price fell within a century of Castillon — an instance of the manuscript's projectile-to-apparatus migration.

## 5. What it CONTRADICTS

At full strength, in his words.

**(i) The norm is inverted.** "Ever-increasing state control of military force, building towards a 'monopoly of violence', is treated as the essential long-term historical process; the use of private military initiatives … is treated as a historical dead-end" (p. 3). State-administered force is the 1760–1960 anomaly (p. 2), and the contractor forms were "simply better, more flexible and more capable of maximizing the military capacity of the state than any attempt to build up comprehensive direct control of military force would have been" (p. 317). Part III predicts absorption of private force (ch11 move three; ch12 §III "operative but never invested"; §IV "the state will absorb the stack"); Parrott's last page predicts "a widespread and comprehensive return to military outsourcing and the military contractor-state, embraced with all the political, financial and organizational pragmatism of the states that moved in this direction from the sixteenth to the late eighteenth century" (p. 327). This reaches spine §7 and §8(c): on Parrott the contractual-oligarchic route is not a phase but the equilibrium.

**(ii) The rival mechanism.** Against artillery as ratchet (spine §8(a)): "Much more than technological or tactical developments … the lengthening of periods of continuous warfare … was the key early modern 'revolution in military affairs'" (p. 76). Against the chain's third link, "administrative capacity suppressing every authority between the crown and the taxable subject" (ch10 §I): arguments linking army growth and state power "in some reciprocal explanation of mutually reinforcing growth become lost in a hazy circularity of implicit and untested assumptions" (p. 294); "resources to boost the war effort were bought through selling out on conventional notions of state-formation through the centralization and concentration of authority … In most respects an autonomous state administration continued relatively weak and underdeveloped, except in its ability to mobilize military resources" (p. 315). The "fiscal-military state" is, for him, "a synthesis of ideas about the role – and crucially the limitations – of state power" (p. 314); ch10 §III's retrojection should know its owner reads it as a theory of weakness.

**(iii) Artillery was not state-sized for long.** Cast-iron guns were a merchant commodity by 1630; "the English crown felt no impulsion to assume direct control of its production" (p. 201); the bishop of Münster could raise "20,000–25,000 men on his own account, and possessing an impressive artillery train" (p. 281); Liechtenstein reformed the Austrian artillery in the 1740s "in significant part on the back of private funds" (p. 295). And state control bought no decisiveness: after 1650 "A vast and inflationary expansion in armed forces did nothing to make war more decisive or to achieve strategic goals" (p. 195). The DEFEND-tier claim (spine §6) meets a century in which the state paid more, controlled more, and decided less.

**(iv) The vocabulary.** "The origins of this definition of state-formation as achieving a narrowly defined monopoly of force, rather than, say, the successful co-option of both internal and outside resources and skills … is deeply entrenched in a series of implicit assumptions" (p. 8). Ch10 §III's "1439 the monopoly of force" and ch3 §II's "monopoly asserted at the level of paperwork" are the phrasing he indicts.

**(v) The disanalogy is mis-drawn.** CLAUDE.md §6 and ch3 §IV rest the contrast on "PMSC state-dependence vs. condottieri fiscal independence." Parrott shows the condottiere was not independent either: the *prestanza* "was the vital element in the financing of the military system" (p. 43); the fourteenth-century companies had "no easy access to credit" and "could simply melt away in a few weeks" (p. 41); Wallenstein "never became independent of financial support from his warlord, the Emperor" (p. 229). What differed was the *paymaster's* capacity — and Parrott's paymasters bought it by selling office and proprietorship.

**The book's best answer.** Four parts; three are Parrott's own concessions.

First, scope. His "devolution" is devolution of *provision* — recruitment, supply, credit — with the ruler retaining "a shareholder interest in armed forces acting in its name, and in many cases this interest amounted to a majority holding" (p. 264), after 1650 "everywhere the majority shareholder" (p. 195), and even today "the 'core activities' of the armed forces have so far remained directly under state control" (p. 4). The manuscript's mechanism is about the decisive layer and the off-switch (spine §8(c): "the mechanism runs on control"), not who bakes the bread. Ch12 §IV's "hybrid — licence, carriage, firebreak, the platforms' equity intact" is Parrott's partnership seen from the sovereignty side; the disagreement is which shareholder holds the switch, and his own Harrach line (p. 125) says the one who pays.

Second, his own case of the mechanism: where the decisive weapon ceased to be dual-use — the rated warship — the state had to own it, "akin to the role of siege artillery" (pp. 290–91). The full-stack cannon of ch12 is that case.

Third, his explanation of the eclipse is the manuscript's chain: administrators "would favour direct control … because it allowed for more predictable year-on-year financial planning and budgeting, and it integrated the military more effectively into an all-important fiscal system" (p. 326); and the contractor-state returned "only following the disappearance of mass conscripted armies, the relative marginalization of the military … and the huge decline in the percentage of state budgets devoted to military expenditure" (p. 327). Ch12 §II documents all three reversing; on Parrott's stated conditions the contractor-state's licence is being withdrawn.

Fourth, the ratchet re-run: Brandenburg 1653 (p. 284) is 1439–46 in miniature.

Is the answer good? On (i), (iv) and (v): yes — and (v) should be taken as a correction: restate the disanalogy as a difference in paymasters, which strengthens ch3. On (ii): partly. Parrott's duration mechanism runs in 1560–1660, the ratchet in 1440–1500; the manuscript need not win the later century, but must concede that its chain is a one-way conditional, not a law — (iii) is a century of state control without decisiveness, and ch10 §VI's Swiss already imply as much; ch10 §VII's Curry citation ("could not be provided by any other organisation than the state") needs its date-bound qualifier made explicit. On the timetable: weak. The venality-and-contract phase that spine §8(c) casts as "the 1890s phase … not its terminus" lasted two centuries in France and worked; Appendix C scores fifteen years. The honest statement is the critiques entry's own: if no switch is severed while incorporation deepens, "Parrott's equilibrium is the better description." The contradiction reaches spine §6 (DEFEND), §7, §8(a) and §8(c).

## 7. Verdict

Parrott changes the book's register, not its direction: the prediction survives, but only if every "absorption" and "monopoly" in Part III is rewritten as control of the decisive layer and the switch, with provision conceded to contract — which is what ch12 §IV already half-says and ch11 move three does not. The weight he adds is real and two-sided: he supplies the best early-modern lines for the Sforza checklist and the paymaster point, and he supplies, in his own concessions (the rated warship, the fiscal motive for direct control, the conditions for the contractor-state's return), the manuscript's answer to himself. The single thing the author should know is that Parrott's strongest evidence is not that states never absorbed force but that the hybrid, venal, contracted phase lasted two hundred years and worked — so the wager's exposure is its fifteen-year clock, not its mechanism.
