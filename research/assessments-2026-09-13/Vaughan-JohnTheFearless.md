# Assessment — Richard Vaughan, *John the Fearless: The Growth of Burgundian Power* (1966; repr. 2002)

Read in full (PDF pp. 21–319 / printed pp. xiii–289); bibliography and index skimmed only. Pins are "PDF p. N / p. N" (printed running head second). OCR is good for prose; it fails on the maps and on two rotated tables — PDF p. 131 / p. 105 (surviving account series) and PDF p. 134 / p. 108 (revenues 1417–19) — whose conclusions the surrounding prose supplies. Numerals are sometimes corrupted ("I4II", "g" for 9); every figure below is checked against context.

## 1. The book in two paragraphs

Vaughan's subject is the Burgundian *state*, not the man: "my book has no hero. Its subject is not the life of a man, but the history of the Burgundian state from 1404 to 1419, when John the Fearless was its ruler" (PDF p. 21 / p. xiii). Against the French tradition (du Fresne de Beaucourt, Calmette) that reads John as a treasonous French prince, he argues that John ruled an autonomous composite polity with a deliberate dynastic programme in France and the Empire alike: "In everything but the title of king, John the Fearless was a European monarch" (PDF p. 258 / p. 228). The archives are both evidence and thesis: "I find, reflected in these documents, policy, order, deliberation, even purpose … Burgundy did not just come together by chance under Philip the Bold; nor was it mere accident which caused its power to increase under John the Fearless" (PDF pp. 317–18 / pp. 287–8). "Whatever he may have been to France, he was a loyal Burgundian" (PDF p. 319 / p. 289).

The load-bearing chapters for this project are ch. 5, "The Means to Power" (finance, civil service, army; PDF pp. 129–78 / pp. 103–52) and ch. 10, "The Bridge of Montereau" (PDF pp. 293–316 / pp. 263–86), where the 1419 murder is reconstructed from the depositions and ruled premeditated. Vaughan's state is explicitly not a modern one — "it would be ludicrous to envisage them as founders of anything like a modern state" (PDF p. 317 / p. 287): revenue "between 400,000 and 450,000 livres of Tours" a year (PDF p. 132 / p. 106), a war-time army raised per campaign from the nobility of Artois and the two Burgundies, a peace-time army "limited to the ducal escort or bodyguard, the garrisons of ducal castles" (PDF p. 177 / p. 151). T2; still the standard English account, its archival base not displaced by Schnerb (2005) or Famiglietti (1986).

## 2. What it CONFIRMS

**Ch05 §III — the assassination sequence.** Manuscript: "on 23 November 1407 John resolved the argument by having Louis assassinated in a Paris street … John the Fearless, cut down at the bridge of Montereau on 10 September 1419". Vaughan: "the murder committed on 23 November 1407 was certainly premeditated and carefully planned in advance" (PDF p. 66 / p. 44); the meeting "on Sunday, 10 September, on the bridge of Montereau-Faut-Yonne" (PDF p. 304 / p. 274). The [GAP] naming this book at ch05 §III closes for dates and sequence.

**Ch05 §III — the crown as a vacancy.** Manuscript: "the substitute-executive question ate the polity". Vaughan on 1408: "The government of France, divided between the royal dukes, a vacillating and pleasure-loving queen, an insane king, the youthful dauphin and various councillors, was irresolute, decrepit, half-hearted" (PDF p. 96 / p. 74).

**Ch05 §VI — the over-mighty subject came first.** Manuscript: Burgundy was a creation "of royal grant in the 1360s, endowed before anyone knew the king's mind would fail." Vaughan gives the endowment its fiscal form: "nearly half Philip's revenues, to the tune of some 235,000 francs per annum, came from the French royal treasury" (PDF p. 63 / p. 41).

**Ch09 §III — France legislated the substitute; intermittency defeated it.** The manuscript cites 1393 and 1403. Vaughan carries the rest of the series: 27 January 1406, John "substituted for his dead father in the council of regency … set up, some years before, to rule France during Charles VI's 'absences'" (PDF p. 59 / p. 37); 26–27 December 1407 and 18 January 1408, John "implicitly but totally excluded" (PDF p. 90 / p. 68); 27 and 31 December 1409, John given "the guardianship and government of my lord of Guienne" and the dauphin "empowered … to rule during the king's 'absences'" (PDF p. 105 / p. 81). "Rewritten twice a year" is now pinnable.

**Ch06 §IV — Rouen.** Manuscript: July 1418 to January 1419, "five months and three weeks". Vaughan: "from 30 July 1418 until their surrender from starvation on 19 January 1419" (PDF pp. 299–300 / pp. 269–70); Bajart, 19 October 1418: "At Rouen they are eating horseflesh" (PDF p. 300 / p. 270).

**Ch10 §VI / ch06 §VII — Burgundy as rival consolidator.** Manuscript: "not a subject but a rival consolidator". Vaughan: Burgundy "was one of the major powers of the west European continent" (PDF p. 258 / p. 228) and John "made and signed treaties with other powers, and even with France herself, as if he were completely independent" (PDF p. 280 / p. 250).

## 3. What it CORRECTS

No date, name or figure in the manuscript is contradicted. Two precisions. Ch05 §III's "at a parley with the Dauphin's party" understates Vaughan's finding and half-inherits the Dauphinist "fracas" version he rejects (PDF pp. 307–8 / pp. 277–8): the dauphin "was one of the murderers" (PDF p. 313 / p. 283); John "was murdered in Charles's presence according to a prearranged plan" (PDF p. 316 / p. 286). Ch05 §VI's "creations of royal grant in the 1360s" is exact for the duchy (1363) but the Flemish–Artesian–Comtois half came by crown-brokered marriage (1369) and succession (1384); Vaughan states the base as "the duchy of Burgundy and the county of Flanders" (PDF p. 281 / p. 251). Say "royal grant and royal marriage-brokering".

## 4. What it ADDS

**(a) Ch05 §VI — the apanage as fiscal pipe, and the war as a fight for it.** The book's strongest gift. Crown gifts and pensions: Philip the Bold 198,941 and 188,600 francs in 1402–4; John 37,000 and 2,000 in 1405–7 (PDF p. 64 / p. 42). Royal debts owing to John by April 1407 totalled 347,591 francs, with "no sign whatsoever, in the ducal accounts" of payment (PDF pp. 64–5 / pp. 42–3), while Louis of Orléans drew "nearly 159,000 l. … from royal aides ceded to him" of a 454,158 l.t. income (PDF p. 53 / p. 31). Vaughan: "among the many motives which impelled John the Fearless to assassinate his rival Louis of Orleans, was the need for funds from France, combined with the bitter knowledge that Louis was obtaining them" (PDF p. 65 / p. 43). After the murder: "In 1406 he had the shadow of power only; in 1409 he enjoyed its substance and, if this was an expensive game, the king was paying for it, not he" (PDF p. 111 / p. 87); "about 70,000 l.t. per annum from the king of France" across 1406–12 (PDF p. 133 / p. 107). The civil war was a contest among crown-endowed subjects for the undermighty king's revenue stream — the direction-of-causation argument with its motive supplied.

**(b) Ch09 §III — hollow sovereignty in its literal images.** The king "set out on the road to Arras with the white bend of the Armagnacs over his arms, just as happily as he had left for Bourges two years before, decked out with the Burgundian cross of St. Andrew" (PDF p. 227 / p. 197). The office split: 16 February 1418 "the Parlement and chambre des comptes at Paris are formally abolished and new ones set up at Troyes" (PDF p. 251 / p. 221); the dauphin "had installed his own Parlement at Poitiers and his own chambre des comptes at Bourges" (PDF p. 293 / p. 263). His letter of 29 June 1418: the rebels "seized our great seal and the king's, which they can abuse at their will, we forbid you to obey any letters or writs sent to you whatsoever, except our own" (PDF p. 297 / p. 267).

**(c) Ch07 §III — the Cabochien ordonnance as the Paris instance.** Ch07 has no French urban case; the outline names this one. Vaughan: the revolt of 28 April 1413 "was in part an example of the class warfare which was endemic in the urban populations of the later middle ages … But in part it represented reform rather than revolution, for it was encouraged, supported and exploited by a group of serious men in government and Burgundian circles"; the ordonnance of 26–27 May, 258 articles, "was not a revolutionary manifesto but a detailed programme of administrative reforms … but … was never implemented" (PDF p. 125 / p. 99). Absorption: "solemnly torn up by the clerk of the court at the Paris Parlement" (PDF p. 223 / p. 193); 500 named partisans excluded from amnesty, cut to 200, then forty-five (PDF pp. 233–4 / pp. 203–4). Scale: "two serious riots … five or six executions … and less than fifty persons thrown into gaol" (PDF p. 126 / p. 100). The disanalogy to state: this rising was sponsored by an over-mighty subject running an anti-tax platform — John's "specious promise to abolish or much diminish the aides" (PDF p. 54 / p. 32); Hesdin, 25 April 1417: "in future [the people] pay no taxes, forced loans, subsidies, impositions, salt-duty or any other exactions" (PDF p. 246 / p. 216). Sigismund's hostile gloss: John relied "on riots, and with the help of the ignoble and ignominious mob, especially of impious butchers" (PDF p. 283 / p. 253). The magnate's anti-tax populism is the Praguerie's mirror image.

**(d) Ch08 §II — private war as procedure, French variant.** Louis de Chalon's defiance, 10 August 1411: "I … am no longer his man, nor his subject, nor well-disposed towards him: but his enemy … may God by his grace give you success in arms in some other war than against me" (PDF p. 206 / p. 178). The Burgundy–Bourbon treaty of June 1414 "of 'abstinence from war'": "each bound himself to give the other at least three weeks' notice in the event of a declaration of war" (PDF p. 209 / p. 181) — two subjects of one king contracting the notice period of their feud. Petit's syllogism — "It is permissible and meritorious to kill a tyrant … Therefore the duke of Burgundy did well to kill him" — and royal pardon the next day (PDF pp. 92, 94 / pp. 70, 72).

**(e) Ch10 §VI — antecedents of the recette de l'artillerie.** The draft dates it to 1458. Vaughan: in 1414–15 the Dijon comptes "were obliged by him to open an artillery book"; "a single maître d'artillerie, Germain de Givry"; "on 13 May 1415, John the Fearless ordered all the artillery in the two Burgundies which was not actually in use in his castles, to be brought together in a special arsenal in Dijon"; "by 1419, twenty-three ducal castles in the duchy were equipped with cannon" (PDF pp. 176–7 / pp. 150–1). Griette, which at Dun-sur-Auron in 1412 "shot stones of enormous weight" and needed "nearly twenty men", was "cast at St. Omer … in the presence of Duke John himself, in May or June 1411" (PDF pp. 119–20 / pp. 95–6).

**(f) Ch06 — the délai de secours as political.** "The siege and conquest of Rouen … had no effect whatsoever on the general political situation" (PDF p. 294 / p. 264); the royal muster "to Beauvais for 15 October" 1418 was defeated by the Dauphinists' "direct prohibition" (PDF p. 299 / p. 269). Campaign speed, political in cause — spine §8(e)1.

**(g) Ch03 §II — court clientage, partly closing [GAP: Vaughan, *Philip the Good*].** Planes as retainers' badges: "In 1406 he gave away 315 gold planes … In 1411 he presented 200 gold planes to the gentlemen of his household and 300 silver ones to the servants" (PDF p. 264 / p. 234); Lorraine's fief-rent, "an annual 'pension' … of 2,000 francs" for sworn service in arms (PDF p. 287 / p. 257); court cost "at least 150,000 l.t. per annum, or more than one quarter of their annual revenues" (PDF p. 146 / p. 120). John's figures, one generation before the gap's.

## 5. What it CONTRADICTS

**(i) A great power without the triad.** Vaughan's Burgundy is "a major European power" (PDF p. 292 / p. 262) with none of the artillery state's legs. Army: "the war-time army, instead of being a quasi-permanent institution … was normally something mobilized and created for one expedition or campaign" (PDF p. 164 / p. 138). Finance: no permanent tax; forced loans from the duke's own receivers (PDF p. 139 / p. 113); pensions halved or stopped nine times (PDF p. 141 / p. 115); war paying for itself — Othée "must have paid for itself, for John's share of the indemnity … was 123,750 l.t." (PDF p. 143 / p. 117). Guns: "It is difficult to assess the military importance of artillery in the Hundred Years War" (PDF p. 176 / p. 150), and in the 1410s everyone had them — "the men of Liège used cannon at Othée"; Ham fell "partly because of the Flemish artillery" (same page); at Rougemont in 1411 the train was "a rather motley collection of artillery, which included the 'great bombard of Burgundy' as well as privately owned pieces lent by the lord of Villars, the lord of Arlay, and other nobles" (PDF p. 207 / p. 179). Vellexon took four months, "a combination of mining and bombardment", delayed by "shortage of cash" (PDF p. 202 / p. 176). This reaches the DEFEND-tier claim (spine §6): cannon as what "no magnate could replicate".

**(ii) Power as custody of persons, not fiscs.** "The secret of success was, of course, control of the person of the king" (PDF p. 51 / p. 29); "His policy in France was based on the possession, or at least the control, of the court and government" (PDF p. 96 / p. 74). Fiscal-administrative capacity is, for Vaughan, an instrument of dynastic custody, not the decider. And the Empire, which "provided neither an army nor revenues, nor any significant means of power" (PDF p. 280 / p. 250), lost nothing by it: dispersal stable for a century.

**(iii) The disclaimer.** "The word state has been used in this book for want of a better one" (PDF p. 317 / p. 287). Vaughan will not be enlisted for a chain ending in the modern state.

**The book's best answer.** To (i): the manuscript's threshold is the *train*, not the gun (ch06 §VII), and Vaughan's own evidence dates the threshold by what was scarce. In 1409 the Dijon comptes reported that "gunpowder is not to be had and, in any case, there is no money to buy it" (PDF p. 199 / p. 173); in 1412 "casks of gunpowder were scrounged from the towns of the county" (PDF p. 202 / p. 176); the 1417 battle plan forbade fighting near Paris, "whence they can easily and plentifully supply themselves with cannon, caltrops, ribaudekins" (PDF p. 175 / p. 149). Guns were everywhere; *supply at campaign tempo* — powder, carriage, cash — sat with whoever held a treasury and a city. That is the mechanism restated, and Rougemont's motley train is its "before" picture, the 1415 arsenal its first "after". The answer is good, at a price: ch06 §VII's claim that artillery "put the price of decisive force above what any subject could pay" must be dated to the 1440s and not read backward, and the 1410s conceded as a decade when decisive force was cheap, distributed and indecisive — which is what an undermighty-king civil war looks like. To (ii): custody politics is the symptom ch05 §VI and ch09 describe; the honest concession is Vaughan's, that nobody in 1404–19 was consolidating and Burgundy grew "not by chance" without any of the settlement's instruments. The pendulum did not swing because guns arrived; it swung when the settlement of 1439–46 and the guns of 1449–53 together made a crown that could outspend the custody game. Vaughan is the control period — same technology, same actors, no ratchet — which supports §8(a)'s co-evolutionary form and cuts against any technological-trigger reading. To (iii): grant it; the chain runs to the Tudor state, not the modern one. No spine ruling is reached by (ii) or (iii).

## 6. Placement

- **Ch05 §III, §VI** — T2 ally, BY NAME: the assassination dates (gap closed); the fiscal-pipe argument (§4a); Vaughan's Montereau verdict stated as his, against the French tradition he names.
- **Ch09 §III** — T2 evidence, footnoted: the 1406–09 ordonnances; the badges, Parlements and seals (§4b) quoted in text.
- **Ch07 §III** — T2 evidence for a new Paris paragraph between the Praguerie and the Ciompi (§4c); Vaughan named for scale and the "reform rather than revolution" reading; Coville (*Les Cabochiens*, on the RETRIEVAL LIST) remains primary for the ordonnance's text.
- **Ch08 §II** — footnote: Chalon's defiance, the 1414 abstinence treaty, Petit's syllogism.
- **Ch10 §VI** — named once as ally (1415 register and arsenal), once as rival (Rougemont, Liège cannon), answered as in §5. Depreter is currently the paragraph's only archive; Vaughan supplies its earlier half.
- **Ch03 §II** — footnote, marked as John's figures standing in until *Philip the Good* arrives.
- **Ch06 §IV** — footnote: Rouen dates; the unmustered relief of October 1418.
- Not for ch11, ch12 or Part I.

## 7. Verdict

The book changes the manuscript's weight, not its direction: it closes two stated gaps at T2, supplies the Paris case ch07 lacks, and gives ch05 the fiscal motive for a civil war it currently narrates only dynastically. Its effect on register is a discipline — Vaughan's Burgundy of 1404–19 is a great power built without permanent tax, standing army or decisive guns, and the mechanism chapters must absorb that decade as the control period rather than argue past it. The single thing the author should know: on Vaughan's evidence the cannon of the 1410s were owned by towns, nobles and rebels alike and decided almost nothing, so the DEFEND-tier claim must be stated as a claim about supply at campaign tempo — powder, carriage, recette — dated to the 1440s, with the motley train at Rougemont in 1411 as the picture of what came before.
