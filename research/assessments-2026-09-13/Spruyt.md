# Assessment — Hendrik Spruyt, *The Sovereign State and Its Competitors: An Analysis of Systems Change* (Princeton, 1994; pbk 1996)

*Read in full: introduction, chs 1–9 (printed pp. 3–194) and the notes to chs 2, 5, 6, 8. The OCR scan holds a two-page spread per PDF page; printed page = PDF − 15 (left) / − 14 (right); pins give both. OCR is garbled at printed pp. 31–32 (Tilly/Strayer), 143–44 (Venice/Florence oligarchy) and 171; nothing is quoted from those stretches.*

## 1. The book in two paragraphs

Spruyt's question is not why states beat feudalism but why the sovereign territorial state beat its *synchronic* rivals — the city-league (the Hansa) and the city-state (Venice, Florence) — which were also post-feudal, also trade-financed, also armed. His answer has two stages. Generation: the eleventh–thirteenth-century trade expansion created towns, and the *coalition* each region's towns could strike decided the institutional form — king-plus-towns in France, towns-versus-lords in Germany, urbanised nobility in Italy. Selection: sovereign territoriality then won because a final locus of authority standardised coinage, measures and justice, suppressed free-riding, could *credibly commit* in treaties, and because sovereigns recognised only like units ("mutual empowerment"), so that non-territorial actors were denied standing — the Hansa "was not allowed to be an equal participant at the Peace of Westphalia. Individual free cities, however, were permitted" (PDF p. 31 / printed p. 16). His thesis sentence: "History has covered its tracks well. We often take the present system of sovereign states for granted and believe that its development was inevitable. But it was not" (PDF 17 / p. 3).

He is explicit that war is not the motor. "Contrary to the common explanation that states emerged as the result of changes in warfare, I argue that the sovereign state emerged as the result of a social coalition based on the affinity of interests and perspectives between incipient monarchy and burghers. Changes in the nature of warfare might explain the growth of government, particularly following the fifteenth century, but they do not explain why some governments were structured on the principles of sovereignty and territoriality, whereas other governments were not" (PDF 91 / p. 77). And on selection: "Warfare did not obliterate the alternatives to the state. There were no decisive battles to end the Hanseatic League or the Italian city-states" (PDF 193 / p. 178); "the ability to wage war is an intervening variable, itself determined by institutional makeup" (PDF 45 / p. 30). It is the strongest institutionalist rival to the Tilly line the manuscript already knows how to answer — and it is a rival that shares half the manuscript's premises.

## 2. What it CONFIRMS

**Ch 1 §II — the plural order.** Manuscript: "authority was real, dense, law-governed — and nowhere exclusive." Spruyt, via Ruggie: "Jurisdiction was neither discrete — jurisdictions overlapped — nor exclusive — different authorities might claim final jurisdiction on the same matter" (PDF 27 / p. 13). The John of Toul oath balancing four lords (PDF 53 / p. 39) is a usable exhibit.

**Ch 1 §VI — the state as episode.** "There is, however, nothing inevitable about the emergence of the sovereign, territorial state" (PDF 33 / p. 18). And the return-swing licence, from 1994: "with another dramatic transformation, the contemporary 'winner'… might prove to be susceptible to change itself" (PDF 193 / p. 179).

**Ch 1 §II dating of the Hansa.** The manuscript's "around 1200 it is nascent" is Spruyt's chronology: first preserved treaty with a prince 1189 (PDF 135 / p. 121); "already existed de facto in 1292" (PDF 133 / p. 119); "the first general council of the Hansa towns met in 1356. That year is usually taken to be the founding" (PDF 139 / p. 124). This carries, at T2, the [GAP: Dollinger] on the Hansetag's date in ch 4 §VI; Lübeck became *formal* leader only in 1418 (PDF 141 / p. 126).

**Ch 4 §VI — the Hansa as non-territorial, quasi-imperial, and ended without a battle.** Manuscript: "it was never defeated; it 'silently disintegrated'." Spruyt: "the demise of the Hansa was long and protracted. There was no decisive battle that sealed its fate. Decline was relative, not absolute" (PDF 181 / p. 166) — Hanseatic tonnage was fifty per cent *higher* in 1600 than 1500. And the universalism reading the manuscript reaches for: "In a sense its logic resembled that of an empire… It lacked a clear hierarchical authority and formal territorial borders" (PDF 143 / p. 129); "Preferably, the league would extend political control over its entire sphere of economic interaction" (same page). Stralsund 1370: "it controlled the royal succession and took control over the castles that controlled the Sound" (PDF 141 / p. 127).

**Ch 4 §VI — Venice as proto-state.** Spruyt: "Unlike Venice or Florence, whose revenues and military power equaled that of any sovereign, territorial state" (PDF 101 / p. 87); 1423 budget 750,000 ducats against France's one million (PDF 159–61 / pp. 145–46).

**Ch 10 §VII / Coda §I — fiscal capacity as the selector.** Spruyt concedes the fiscal link outright: "there is no doubt that the military revolution led to an exponential increase in state demands on society following the Middle Ages" (PDF 45 / p. 30); and his own evidence for selection is a revenue arc — Charles VII 1.8–2.25 million livres, Francis I 8 million, the 1580s about 30 million, while Venice "had only doubled by 1570" (n. 176, PDF 275 / pp. 260–61). That is ch 10 §III's tripling, carried at T2 from Collins and Lane. He grants that "Sovereign states were better, in the long run, in raising more revenue and larger numbers of troops" (PDF 193 / p. 178).

**Ch 4 §III — functional versus formal sovereignty.** "Despots called themselves dukes or princes, but not kings, although they basically exercised regalian rights" (PDF 163 / p. 148). The Sforza thread has a general case behind it.

## 3. What it CORRECTS

1. **Ch 4 §VI: "The Hansa had no centre, no treasury, no standing force and no legal personality."** Spruyt disputes this at length: "It will also not do to argue that the Hansa had no well-established sources of revenue and hence cannot be construed as a true political organization" (PDF 141 / p. 127). It levied general fees (the fleet of 1407), Kontor fees, the *Pfundzoll*, yearly dues from 1557, and "developed a constitution with a precise enumeration of duties" (p. 127); the three regional *Drittel* "each… had its own treasury and elected aldermen" (PDF 139 / p. 125). The correct statement is narrower: no *direct tax*, no founding constitution until 1557, no officials of its own ("town councils, such as Lübeck's, operated on its behalf", p. 125), and — the point that matters for the platform parallel — a legal personality *refused by choice*, see §4.

2. **Ch 8 §VII and ch 11 §I: "the courts followed the companies and the taille", and the 1470–1530 window for the canonical move.** By Spruyt's account France's judicial and canonical assertions are thirteenth-century: Louis IX "outlawed trial by combat in 1258" (PDF 177 / p. 163); the 1265 decree that royal coin "should be good throughout the realm" (p. 162); the dictum c. 1250 that the king "recognizes no superior in temporal affairs" (PDF 117 / p. 103); Saisset tried by royal officials in 1300, *Unam Sanctam*, Anagni, Avignon (PDF 111–13 / pp. 97–98). Ch 8 dates the French sequence from the parlements of 1443–77; Spruyt corrects *first assertion*, not enforcement, but "The monopoly of force is claimed in 1439" reads as first-claim and should not (n. 13 to ch 1: Louis IX "tried to limit the bearing of arms by the nobility", PDF 213 / p. 198).

3. **Ch 10 §III / spine §8(a): the taille.** Manuscript: "The constitutional theory of French taxation before 1439 was consent." Spruyt, quoting Parker: "When the royal taille became increasingly regularized from the 1380s onward, the clergy and nobility were exempted… 'The direct tax system of seventeenth century France was largely in place by 1379'" (PDF 109 / p. 95). This does not overturn the 1439–46 hinge — architecture and exemption, not permanence — but "before 1439… consent" needs the qualifier the manuscript half-supplies (the élus "inherited from the 1350s").

4. **Internal to Spruyt, flagged so the manuscript does not inherit it:** the last Hanseatic diet is 1667 at p. 154 and 1669 at p. 167; the taille is "85 percent of the entire revenue" at p. 89 and "about two-thirds" at p. 163; Venice's sixty-per-cent revenue lead is "the middle of the fifteenth century" at p. 157 and "the end of the 1400s" at n. 176. Cite none of these from Spruyt alone.

## 4. What it ADDS

**Ch 4 §VI / ch 11 §VII — the Hansa's refusal of legal personality, and the state's demand for a list.** The best thing in the book for the platform argument. The Hansa "refrained from drafting such a constitution because they believed that a formal list of membership… would prompt states to cut deals with individual towns and encourage freeriding" (PDF 177 / p. 163). The Merchant Adventurers complained in 1551 that "none of their charters named particular individuals or towns"; the Privy Council's "Moderatio in commercio" of 1560 demanded "an explicit list of the members" (PDF 181 / p. 167); the league refused. At Westphalia the princes' objection was "One does not really know what the Hansa in essence is" (PDF 185 / p. 170). The DMA gatekeeper designation is that demand met by the state's own act of naming. Goes to ch 4 §VI as the Hansa's closing beat and to ch 11 move two.

**Ch 11 §VII / ch 12 §IV — two selection mechanisms the manuscript lacks a name for.** *Mutual empowerment*: "sovereign states selected out and delegitimized actors who did not fit a system of territorially demarcated and internally hierarchical authorities" (PDF 43 / p. 28) — how Lübeck, Bremen and Hamburg got seats at Osnabrück and the league did not (pp. 170–71). *Exit*: "The tasks of organizing long-distance commerce were increasingly taken over by states and their consular services, thereby making the Kontors and offices abroad less necessary" (PDF 187 / p. 172) — function substituted, enclave emptied: the Indian sovereign-stack form of absorption, with precedent.

**Ch 12 §IV — the EU typed as city-state, not Burgundy.** Spruyt's Table 8.1 (PDF 169 / p. 154) crosses borders with internal sovereignty: bordered + consolidated = France; bordered + fragmented = the Italian city-state; unbordered = Hansa. In 1994 he placed the EC in the second cell — "composed of an internally fragmented sovereignty but which is essentially territorial" (PDF 205 / p. 191) — and drew the consequence the Depreter test is looking for: "Or the EC will be forced to take on a more consolidated logic, because it currently lacks a clear focal point through which to conduct international affairs" (p. 191). The dominante-and-subject-towns pathology — subject cities "eager to defect when such an opportunity arose" (PDF 189 / p. 175) — is a sharper diagnostic for the Union than Burgundy's lost centre, from a writer with no war mechanism.

**Ch 1 §III — a second formulation of Bull's test.** Spruyt, via Held, separates "the de iure and de facto sides of sovereignty" (PDF 205 / p. 189) and concludes as Bull did: secessionists "wish to become states" (PDF 207 / p. 192); "De iure the state system is alive and well" (p. 193). His 1994 three-scenario checklist (self-help, the EC, alternative identities) is a second dated baseline for the DELTA.

**Ch 10 §VII — the Rice quotation, usable against its user.** Spruyt quotes Rice — "This pressure, becoming acute about 1450, came from the larger territorial princes" (PDF 35 / p. 21) — and reads it as states-made-guns. The date is the manuscript's.

**Ch 1 §II — counts for the Empire.** Twelfth-century coinage rights held by "106 ecclesiastical and 81 secular lords" (PDF 135 / p. 121); "roughly 2,500 local and regional authorities" by the sixteenth (PDF 177 / p. 162).

**Ch 8 — the Empire control case, refined.** Consolidation in Germany happened at the *Land* level: the lordships "established hierarchical control within their borders. Within these principalities, weights and coinage were increasingly standardized, and princely adjudication became the norm" (PDF 185 / p. 171). Not "assertion without substrate" tout court; substrate built one tier down.

**A line worth keeping (ch 2):** "the fullest articulation of feudal organization and the church… occurred shortly before their demise. Such articulation seems to occur exactly when institutional challenges arise" (PDF 207 / p. 193).

## 5. What it CONTRADICTS

At full strength, in his words. **(i) Sequence.** "the historical evidence suggests that the success of the Capetian kingdom preceded the expensive developments of large infantry armies, artillery, and redesigned fortifications. By 1300 the royal domain encompassed the area west of the Rhône… The most significant developments in military technology and strategy came after this centralization and expansion" (PDF 99 / p. 85). "Stone tellingly suggests that central administration preceded and made possible modern warfare rather than the other way round… Warfare, in other words, threatened to fragment rather than centralize the kingdom" (p. 85). "the political fragmentation of the French kingdom had basically come to an end by the late thirteenth century, in no small part because of the alliance of king and towns" (PDF 121 / p. 107). **(ii) Determinism.** "Many parts of Europe did not centralize. Witness Germany and Italy… these small units survived well beyond the military revolution of the fifteenth century" (PDF 97 / p. 83); China "had access to gunpowder technology before Europe but never developed its military uses in full" (PDF 35 / p. 21). **(iii) The price-above-subjects claim.** "the ability of states to develop new military technologies, hire mercenaries, and use artillery were also part of the repertoire of the states' rivals" (PDF 171 / p. 156) — the Hansa's *Adler*, 3,000 tons and sixty-eight guns (n. 12). **(iv) Causal direction.** "sovereign, territorial states made adoption of new technology imperative" (PDF 35 / p. 21). This reaches DEFEND-tier (spine §6: cannon → fiscal-administrative discipline → consolidation) and ruling §8(a).

**The book's best answer.** Three parts. First, Spruyt has *bracketed* the manuscript's question. His dependent variable is the *logic* of sovereignty — "not the particular level of monarchical administration or royal revenue, nor the physical size of the state" (PDF 17 / p. 3) — and he concedes that warfare "might explain the growth of government, particularly following the fifteenth century" (p. 77). The manuscript's mechanism is about the growth, permanence and reach of government — the suppression of intermediate authorities — not the invention of the concept; §8(a) already places the fiscal settlement before the decisive guns and names artillery the ratchet, not the motor, which is compatible with a thirteenth-century Capetian idea of sovereignty that the Hundred Years War then unmade (Spruyt's own admission: the domain "was most threatened in the Hundred Years War", p. 85). Second, on selection Spruyt's chain converges: "The ability to wage war operated as an intermediate cause of selection but was ultimately propelled by the consequences of particular institutional logics" (PDF 193 / p. 178), and his evidence is the fiscal divergence of n. 176. The dispute is over what made French capacity *irreversible* after 1450 while Venetian capacity plateaued — and Spruyt, who never mentions the ordonnance companies, the taille of the 1440s or the Bureau train, has no account of that turn. Third, on (iii): the manuscript's claim is that the train's price sat above what any *subject* could pay, not above what a fiscally sovereign republic could — which is why ch 4 already reads Venice as proto-state. Honestly assessed: the answer to (ii) and (iii) is good and mostly drafted (Coda §I's failure modes are Spruyt's counter-cases by another name). The answer to (i) is adequate only if ch 8 §VII and ch 11 §I stop implying that royal justice and the canonical assertion in France were fifteenth- and sixteenth-century *inventions*; as drafted they do. The answer to (iv) is a genuine standoff — the gun locked the fisc, or the sovereign wanted the gun — and §8(a)'s co-evolution is the right register, but it must be stated against Spruyt by name, because a reviewer will reach for him first.

## 6. Placement

- **Ch 10 §VII:** BY NAME, T2 rival, beside Curry — the institutionalist form of her causal reversal and the sharpest anti-Tilly statement in the IR literature; quote pp. 77, 85, 178; answer with §8(a) and n. 176.
- **Ch 4 §VI:** T2 evidence, by name, for the Hansa's structure, revenue, refused membership list and Westphalia; corrects the "no treasury, no legal personality" sentence; footnote for Venice's revenue.
- **Ch 1 §II, §III, §VI:** T2 ally, footnotes — the plural order, the de iure/de facto test beside Bull, "nothing inevitable".
- **Ch 8 §III and §VII:** T2 evidence; the *Land*-level mimicry passage refines the Empire control case; adjust "claimed in 1439".
- **Ch 11 §VII:** by name for mutual empowerment and exit as the legal form of "leashed rather than destroyed".
- **Ch 12 §IV:** by name, one paragraph — the EU as the bordered-but-fragmented cell and the 1994 forecast.
- **Coda §I and §VI:** footnote — the synchronic-variation method warrants the comparative rule; his 1994 verdict ("more firmly entrenched… save perhaps in the case of the EC", p. 192) joins the Bull lineage as caution inherited.

## 7. Verdict

Spruyt does not change the book's direction but forces a change of register in two places: ch 10 §VII must name and answer him, because he is the anti-Tilly the manuscript's own anti-Tilly differentiation (§8(a)) will be measured against, and ch 8/ch 11 must stop implying that France's judicial and canonical assertions began in the fifteenth century. His weight is otherwise additive — the Hansa's refused membership list and the 1994 typing of the EU as a city-state are the two most usable finds. The single most important thing the author should know: Spruyt concedes everything the mechanism needs after 1450 (war drove "an exponential increase in state demands", fiscal capacity selected the winners) and denies only that the gun *started* it — so the book's quarrel with him is exactly the ratchet-not-motor point already ruled, and should be fought there and nowhere else.
