# Assessment — Lawrence Freedman, *The Future of War: A History* (PublicAffairs, 2017)

*Text: /home/claude/books/Freedman.txt (ebook-derived, ~160k words). Read in full, introduction to chapter 25; notes and index consulted for pins. No running-head page numbers survive, so PDF pages come from the form-feed count and printed pages from the book's index where an entry exists. Text clean; the few misspellings are the edition's own.*

## 1. The book in two paragraphs

Freedman's subject is not war but the literature that has imagined it — from Chesney's *Battle of Dorking* (1871) through Bloch, Wells, Douhet, Kahn, Hackett, Clancy, the RMA, cyberwar, drones, and the futurists of Sino-American war — read for what it reveals about the assumptions of its own moment. Two recurring themes organise the whole: "a growing appreciation of the difficulties of containing war so that its destructiveness could be bounded in time and space, and second, linked to this, a search for a form of decisive force that might inflict a knockout blow on an enemy and so end a war quickly and successfully" (PDF p. 17 / printed p. xviii). The genre's structural error follows: "Explanations for why the first moves in a coming war might be more successful than those attempted in previous wars tended to point to new technologies or tactics. It was easier to anticipate the hardware than the politics" (PDF p. 17). The book's verdict on the whole tradition, delivered through H. R. McMaster's "vampire fallacy", is that "technology encouraged a fantasy of a war that was fast, easy, and decisive: yet they still found themselves facing 'slow, bitter and indecisive war'"; and, more pointedly for this project, "the technology was rarely monopolised or else, even if one side enjoyed superiority, adversaries found ways to limit their effects" (PDF p. 316 / printed pp. 276–79 per index).

Parts II–III turn from great-power scripts to the civil wars, interventions and "grey zone" conflicts that actually filled the decades after 1990, and end in a deliberate refusal: "It would be against the spirit of this book to predict the incidence and form of future wars" (PDF p. 323). His reasons are the manuscript's discipline: "prediction is often purposive, closely bound up with advocacy, and so is about the present as much as the future" (PDF p. 323); "Another and quite different tendency is to assert that we are on the verge of a great, transformational discontinuity. The possibility that much will carry on as before is far less interesting" (PDF p. 324); such works "should all, however, be treated sceptically" (PDF p. 324). His one general proposition: "The reason that the future is difficult to predict is that it depends on choices that have yet to be made" (PDF p. 18).

## 2. What it CONFIRMS

**"Decisive" as composition, not storm (ch06 §V; spine §4).** The manuscript's "Artillery collapsed the defender's time horizon and forced composition; it did not demolish its way in" is Freedman's own definition of the word: "The word 'decisive' had an air of finality… but in other respects—unlike words such as 'victory' and 'defeat'—it was quite neutral. The decision could take the form of a negotiated settlement that left neither side satisfied. The essential feature was that they both accepted the result and that it reflected a situation largely achieved by military means" (PDF p. 31 / printed p. 9); and sieges are "potentially as important as actual battle in shaping wars" (PDF p. 31). A T3 authority for the chapter's use of the term.

**Force that takes and holds (ch12 §V, nuclear paragraph and drones).** The manuscript's "the mechanism concerns force that takes and holds — the siege train, the stack" is Freedman's recurring criterion. On air power: "Because they could not hold territory, aircraft could not on their own 'win' wars" (PDF p. 43). On swarming drones: "What it could not do was provide an answer to the problem of holding territory and especially cities in the face of a hostile population. It was territory that still mattered most" (PDF p. 283 / printed pp. 247–48). On hybrid war, the distinction between "reasonably disciplined and substantial forces able to take and hold contested territory, and supporting capabilities that could help to disorient and demoralise an opponent" (PDF p. 259). And flatly: "Numbers were still needed to take and control territory" (PDF p. 284). The spine's "longbows… are not decisive" has its contemporary-strategy statement here.

**The private frontier (ch10 §VIII "frontier-lab anomaly"; ch03).** "the lead with the new technologies was taken by the private sector… which not only meant that the advances were out of state control but also that the state took second place in competition for the skilled engineers and software developers" (PDF p. 281). The undermighty-king condition of the AI layer, stated in 2017.

**The RMA warning label (ch10 §VIII).** The manuscript adopts from a 2026 newspaper the caution that attributing to weapons the advantages of systems has misled military minds before. Freedman carries the documented case: "The technological optimism underlying the RMA was overdone" (PDF p. 219); "The lesson was that in this sort of war numbers mattered, despite all the advanced equipment now available to American forces" (PDF p. 224 / printed p. 184).

**Nuclear weapons as leveller, not consolidator (ch12 §V).** "Countries lacking comparable conventional strength to the US will also continue to see nuclear weapons as a vital leveller", with Putin's 2014 reminder that "Russia is one of the leading nuclear powers" (PDF p. 318 / printed p. 281).

**Kaplan's record (Intro §IV; Appendix A).** Clinton's reluctance in Bosnia "was said to be the result of reading Kaplan's book"; in 1999 he "apologised for blaming conflict on 'some Balkan disease'… 'I regret it now more than I can say'" (PDF p. 184 / printed p. 155). The Intro's "genre with a rap sheet" can cite the sheet.

## 3. What it CORRECTS

1. **The Belloc anchor is misassigned (ch06 §VI, the GAP tag).** Freedman does not mention Belloc, *The Modern Traveller*, or the couplet; "Maxim" occurs once, in the opposite sense to the manuscript's: "The deadly Maxim gun was first deployed by British forces over 1893–1894 in the First Matabele War in Rhodesia. Yet remarkably few of these or any comparable guns were purchased before 1914" (PDF p. 36 / printed p. 12). The tag must be re-pointed: Freedman anchors the *knock-out-blow* genealogy (Chesney, PDF p. 26; Le Queux's "quick knockout blow, taking London", PDF p. 35; Douhet and Holman's 1930s "orthodoxy", PDF p. 79 / printed pp. 56–57; Yamamoto, PDF p. 89; Pearl Harbor "was not a knockout blow", PDF p. 269; Ghost Fleet, PDF p. 286 / printed pp. 250–52; McMaster, PDF p. 316); the Belloc text is a T1 retrieval with no T3 carrier here. The phrase "the satirist's Maxim-gun couplet and its twentieth-century heirs" also yokes two errors: Belloc satirises a weapon *monopoly*, Douhet's heirs promise a weapon that *decides*. Freedman's genealogy is of the second; the first is, awkwardly, closer to this book's own thesis (§5).

2. **"Bush 2003" is the wrong pin for the RMA claim.** Freedman locates the transformational promise in Rumsfeld on Afghanistan: he "explained that Afghanistan was going to be a new type of war, 'like none other our nation has faced'. He saw opportunities to demonstrate that future war could be won with only a modest force so long as it was backed by the most advanced 'transformational' capabilities" (PDF p. 223 / printed p. 184; the Rumsfeld source is his *New York Times* piece "A New Kind of War", n. 25). For 2003 the datable item is different: "Three American presidents announced the end of combat in Iraq—George H. W. Bush at the start of March 1991, his son George W. Bush on 1 May 2003 and then Barack Obama on 31 August 2010. Each time it turned out that the announcement was premature" (PDF p. 144 / printed p. 121). If ch06 §VI wants "the Afghanistan predictions", the primary is Rumsfeld 2001, not Bush 2003.

3. **"Knock-out blow" is doing two jobs in the spine (§2, "a strategic knockout blow against the French cannon").** In Freedman the term means a war-opening blow that "floor[s] the opponent" (PDF p. 17) — Sedan, Barbarossa, Pearl Harbor. Talbot's lunge at the guns is a tactical seizure inside a lost campaign, as ch06 §VI's own conjecture ("a last rational lunge at the instrument") says. Keep "knock-out blow" for the prophets; give Talbot "seizure", or the "one mistake run in opposite directions" line invites the objection that they are not the same category of act.

4. **Dating.** "Against more capable opponents, drones, with their slow speed, low altitude, and vulnerability to air defences and electronic countermeasures, would be more restricted in their use" (PDF p. 278) is a 2017 judgement partly overtaken by the Ukraine record; cite it as opinion of its date.

## 4. What it ADDS

**(a) The genealogy itself, sourced (ch06 §VI — closes the GAP in substance).** Holman via Freedman: "the 'theory of the knock-out blow solidified into a near-consensus among military intellectuals during the 1920s and by the 1930s had become an orthodoxy, accepted and promoted by pacifists and militarists alike'" (PDF p. 79 / printed pp. 56–57; Holman, *The Next War in the Air*, 2014, pp. 23–4 — a T2 retrieval). Freedman on the genre: "The prominence of knockout blows in writing about future war was the result not only of their potential strategic impact or their drama but also because they helped make a point" (PDF p. 316 / printed p. 276); and the post-1945 failures, Korea 1950, the Falklands 1982, Kuwait 1990, "all of which failed to achieve their objective" (PDF p. 316).

**(b) The Tokyo exchange, September 1941 (ch06 §VI).** The Emperor: the Army had told him "'that we could achieve peace immediately after dealing them one blow with three divisions'… 'If you call the Chinese hinterland vast, would you not describe the Pacific as even more immense?'"; Nagano: "if after the surgery, the patient dies, one must say that was meant to be" (PDF pp. 88–89 / printed p. 65). The Belloc syndrome at the top of a state; cite via Freedman (T3), his n. 24 to ch. 6 the route to the T1.

**(c) Wells as ancestor of the "apparatus" (ch10 §VIII or ch12 §IV).** In 1902 Wells foresaw the commander "represented 'far in the rear' by a 'central organizer' who would 'sit at the telephonic centre of his vast front'", war "won with the seizure of the 'vital apparatus of the urban regions'" (PDF p. 41 / printed p. 18); and his "law": "A people must develop and consolidate its educated efficient classes or be beaten in war" (PDF p. 44 / printed p. 21) — administrative capacity as military power, which Freedman immediately shows sliding into eugenics. Usable with that caution; retrieve *Anticipations* (T1) before print.

**(d) The Dorking genre as a named objection (Intro §IV; Coda §V).** Freedman, quoting *The Economist*, defines the genre's device: presenting "'new technologies as decisive, both a thrilling idea and a necessary device if… dominant nations were to be portrayed, initially at least, as victims,' and as a means of imparting a stark message 'of the wrongheadedness of politicians or senior officers, of national decline, of geopolitical change'" (PDF p. 287 / printed p. 252). The Introduction meets one genre (declinism); this is the second and more dangerous, because the "wrong-fight" observation about America (spine §5) reads as Chesney's complaint.

**(e) The platform-cost paragraph (ch12 §V).** Augustine's law, "In the year 2054, the entire defense budget will purchase just one tactical aircraft" (PDF p. 275 / printed p. 240), and the response — "'Uninhabited systems' could 'help bring mass back to the fight'" (PDF p. 276; Scharre 2014) — give the longbow/cannon debate its acquisition-history form; the Death Star/droid passage (PDF pp. 274–75 / printed pp. 239–40) is the trade version.

**(f) Interstitial disorder, named (ch12 §II; ch05; Appendix A).** Norton's "feral cities", whose government "'has lost the ability to maintain the rule of law within the city's boundaries yet remains a functioning actor in the greater international system'" (PDF p. 291 / printed p. 256); Jackson's "quasi-states", "'juridical statehood'" without "'empirical statehood'" (PDF p. 179 / printed p. 151); van Creveld's "'political entities similar to those existing before 1648'" (PDF p. 171 / printed p. 143) — a neomedieval datum Appendix A does not index.

**(g) Contemporaries' misreading (ch06 §V, upgrade 3).** "how individuals became prisoners of their experiences and missed what was blindingly obvious to later generations" (PDF p. 16); "Von Moltke's stunning victory reinforced a classical model of warfare despite hinting at its limitations" (PDF p. 24 / printed p. 3). Tilly appears in the Luttwak give-war-a-chance debate (PDF p. 249 / printed p. 217) — a footnote for ch10's Tilly differentiation.

**(h) Coda discipline (Coda §I, §V).** Arendt, via Freedman: "'Predictions of the future are never anything but projections of present automatic processes and procedures… every action, for better or worse, and every accident necessarily destroys the whole pattern in whose frame the prediction moves'" (PDF p. 148 / printed p. 123). The Coda's "mechanism under conditions" should quote the objection it answers.

## 5. What it CONTRADICTS

**(i) "The technology was rarely monopolised" — against the DEFEND-tier mechanism (spine §6; §4).** "One regular assumption was that the odds of success might be shifted decisively as a result of some new technology. Gunpowder to muskets, steam turbines to aircraft, missiles to digital networks, all changed the character of warfare… But the technology was rarely monopolised or else, even if one side enjoyed superiority, adversaries found ways to limit their effects. Even for modern Western forces, technology encouraged a fantasy of a war that was fast, easy, and decisive" (PDF p. 316 / printed pp. 276–77). McMaster's fallacy names the manuscript's vocabulary: "'New concepts with catchy titles' promised 'fast, cheap and efficient victories in future war'… The fallacy lay in neglecting 'war's political and human dimensions' and equating 'targeting to tactics, operations and strategy'" (PDF p. 316 / printed p. 279). The spine's "The cannon of today is the full-stack, AI-automated state… That is what will be decisive on the battlefield" is, in this frame, the vampire risen again — and the Belloc couplet, celebrating a weapon monopoly, is the manuscript's own thesis in a satirist's hat.

**(ii) The decisive stack was sold as lowering the fiscal threshold, not raising it (ch10 §VIII).** The RMA's promise: "The infrastructure of war, which required the mobilisation of whole societies, could be reduced. The accuracy of weapons meant that fewer would be required, putting less strain on industry and the transport infrastructure" (PDF p. 219 / printed p. 187). And the sensing layer has diffused: "Smartphones carried capabilities such as satellite imaging, navigations, data stores, and instant, encrypted communications of a quality once available to only the most advanced military organisations" (PDF p. 282 / printed p. 248). Both cut against "the capital intensity has migrated from the projectile to the sensing-and-deciding apparatus".

**(iii) The genre charge, and the prediction charge (Intro; Coda; spine §7).** "There is no longer a dominant model for future war, but instead a blurred concept and a range of speculative possibilities" (PDF p. 20); "As much guidance on the future is provided by the unending wars of sub-Saharan Africa as by the promise of artificial intelligence" (PDF p. 324). A book that predicts a "definitive Tudor turn" driven by the AI stack is, on Freedman's taxonomy, a "transformational discontinuity" work of the Dorking kind — technology decisive, a dominant nation cast as laggard, "national decline, geopolitical change" as message — and his whole history is a record of that form being wrong.

**The book's best answer.** On (i): the manuscript's "decisive" is Freedman's own neutral sense — a decision "achieved largely by military means" that "could take the form of a negotiated settlement" — and its exhibit is a train that forced compositions, not a blow that floored anyone. The prediction is of *consolidation* inside blocs, not victory between them; ch10 already concedes that apparatus "without a coherent political object produced stalemate" (Iran). And "rarely" leaves the fifteenth century as the documented exception, attested from the sceptics (Curry's state-only threshold). On (ii): the Ukraine attrition belt and the 155mm ramp show the RMA's low-mobilisation promise failing on the record; the smartphone objection is answered by the March 2025 week — consumer sensing existed and the decisive layer still had an owner. On (iii): the only reply Freedman would accept is method — a published, dated falsifier list — which the book has.

Honestly weighed: the answer to (i) is good *provided* the text never lets "decisive" slide from composition-forcing to battle-winning — and spine §4's "decisive on the battlefield" does slide. The answer to (ii) is good and evidenced. The answer to (iii) is only as good as the indicators; the resemblance to the genre is real, and "prediction is often purposive, closely bound up with advocacy" lands on a book whose author has advised the constituencies ch12 §VII addresses. This reaches spine §6 (DEFEND: the mechanism's present operation through the AI-military stack) and §8(d); it does not touch (a)–(c).

## 7. Verdict

Freedman changes the book's register, not its direction: he is the most authoritative sceptic of exactly the claim the spine defends hardest — that a new technology will be decisive and can be held by those who pay for it — and the manuscript must meet the "vampire fallacy" by name, with "decisive" pinned to his own neutral, settlement-forcing sense and never to battle. Weight is added in his favour on territory, numbers, the private frontier and the nuclear leveller. The single most important thing: Freedman does not carry Belloc, so the ch06 GAP is re-pointed — Holman and the knock-out-blow genealogy are what he supplies — and the couplet itself, once retrieved, is a two-edged epigraph, because "we have got the Maxim gun and they have not" is a joke about monopoly, which is this book's thesis, not its foil.
