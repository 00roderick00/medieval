## 1. The book in two paragraphs

An archaeology of a fantasy, and the fantasy is anti-political: "HIS BOOK IS AN INQUIRY INTO WHAT HUMANS MEAN and intend in abandoning constitutional democracy, the liberal nation-state, and even humanity itself for rule by automation, government by machine, and replacement by artificial intelligence" (printed p. xiii, head illegible / PDF p. 4). The rise is traced through the history of science — census, statistics, Technocracy Inc., Simulmatics, direct mail, Twitter, ai.gov — the fall through science fiction. The connecting claim is epistemic: where the age of numbers produced the liberal nation-state, "the age of data involves a different set of arrangements and consequences, antithetical and finally inhospitable to both liberalism and democracy" (printed p. 94, head illegible / PDF p. 107). She refuses determinism flatly: "liberal democracy made possible the rise of the Artificial State. It didn't make it inevitable" (printed p. 69 / PDF p. 80).

Her object is the reduction of politics to prediction and persuasion: "The Artificial State is an unfinished digital communications infrastructure with which governments and especially private corporations organize and automate political behavior and public discourse"; it "is government without consent, even government without humans" (printed p. 5 / PDF p. 15). Its agents are private — "The corporations that owned those machines began to subsume the state" (printed p. 4, head illegible / PDF p. 14) — and by the 2020s "vastly consequential decisions about the future of Earth and everyone and everything on it were being made by a handful of men" (printed p. 205, chapter opener, head absent / PDF p. 219). Part Two adds an ecological cause of death: "an artificial world cannot survive without the natural world" (printed p. 130 / PDF p. 142).

### What she means by "the artificial state" — and whether it names our object

The definition is given twice, the first by negation: "The Artificial State is less a state than a dream of being without one, the dream of the severing of humans not only from the natural world but also from one another, every umbilical cord cut" (**printed p. xv / PDF p. 7**; the page is also captured illegibly at PDF 5 and 8). The second: "By an 'artificial state' I mean something capacious . . . something different than Hobbes's Leviathan, not something made by man but something nonhuman, something beyond human, something inhuman: the rule of humans by machines manufactured by corporations" (printed pp. 4–5 / PDF pp. 14–15).

**Her artificial state is an anti-state dream and does not name this book's object.** Two tests settle it. It contains no decisive force: greps of all 306 pages return **zero** occurrences of *foundry*, *TSMC*, *CHIPS*, *chip*, *export control*, *Anduril*, *Starlink*, *Starshield*, *Pentagon*, *procurement* and *licensing*; *antitrust* occurs once, as something the industry defeated; the military limb is one sentence — "OpenAI, Google, and Meta made their technologies available to the military. Nvidia began using the language of national sovereignty, invoking 'sovereign AI'" (printed p. 112, head OCR'd "12" / PDF p. 126). And her direction is the inverse of ours: "Democracy and the liberal nation-state had to be abolished to free corporations from the restraining force of any government" (printed p. 205). **The consequence is a title collision, not an agreement: two books, one phrase, two objects.** Any sentence treating her Artificial State as a synonym for the artillery state, or as independent support for it, is a provenance error. The real overlap is narrower and should be stated as such — both hold that the constitutional appearance of consent can outlast its substance.

## 4. What it ADDS

### The second-reader's question, answered from her text

The question (second-reader review, line 112): "whether an artificial state might emerge through the expansion of public authority over private technology, as well as through private technology's displacement of public authority. A stronger state does not by itself answer the question of consent." **Her answer is unambiguous: no. She gives no account of public authority expanding over private technology in a democracy.** Her causal claim is the inverse — abdication: "it played out the way it did because of the failure of liberal democracy to limit corporate power over politics and government" (printed p. 69 / PDF p. 80). Every instrument she names is one of withdrawal: Gingrich closing the Office of Technology Assessment, "formed in 1972", whose "mandate included assessing the implications of any new technology supported by the federal government" (printed p. 70, head illegible / PDF p. 81); the 1996 Telecommunications Act, delivering "an almost entirely unregulated internet" (printed p. 71 / PDF p. 82); the AI Action Plan "relieving all AI infrastructure projects of the burdens of environmental review" (printed p. 222 / PDF p. 238). Her AI.GOV chapter — the likeliest place for the expansion — has the state adopting the industry's prose, not its discretion: ai.gov "employed the language of the publicity departments of OpenAI, Anthropic, Meta, Google, and Microsoft", and the verdict is a reporter's, endorsed: "The nation is in Silicon Valley's hands" (printed p. 222). The countercurrents she frames as capture: "Musk's businesses, sizably underwritten by the U.S. federal government" (printed p. 98 / PDF p. 112); Palantir's CEO, "We are dedicating our company to the service of the West and the United States of America" (printed p. 112). On consent she is clear only about the authoritarian cases (§2(b)–(c)). *Public authority expanding over private technology inside a democracy is not a state of affairs her book describes at all* — which is why the question stays open, and stays ours.

### Technologies that assist government v. systems that replace government by consent

She draws the line three times. "The perfectly sensible automation of certain government functions, like counting the population or distributing benefits, happened swiftly . . . Automating the workings of politics and the functions of democracy took longer" (printed p. 6, head illegible / PDF p. 16). Then, load-bearing: Cold War computing served "classified military and intelligence work but also . . . government administration of everything from welfare provision to national security. **Only later would these tools be applied, by private companies, as substitutes for the democratic functions of representation, deliberation, and participation**" (printed p. 38 / PDF p. 49). And, as a conceded gain, nations that built digital infrastructure "were able to offer services to the public more efficiently, and more cheaply" (printed p. 73 / PDF p. 84). The line is **administration against representation**: machines may administer; they may not represent.

What it does to ch12 §§III–IV. It **supports** §III's legibility paragraph and its disanalogy — the draft's "uniform legibility is a governing *capacity*" is her administration side exactly — and supplies the two-sided formula the draft now hedges with `[ANALOGY-ONLY]`: "The ability to count gave the state power; the ability to be counted gave the people power" (printed p. 94 / PDF p. 107). It **sharpens** §IV's absorption verdict with the criterion that matters for claim 3(ii): not whether the state acquires the stack but whether what it acquires is administrative capacity or the representative function itself — the identity–payments–legibility stack straddles that line, which is what makes §VII's *prefet* inference more than a flourish. It **embarrasses** §IV at the trust-busting sentence (§3(iii)).

### What she says citizens can actually do — against §8(g)(2)'s falsifiers and DC-1..6

The Epilogue is the whole answer, and it is four pages. Its premise: "because it is a construct . . . it can be dismantled. Other very stubborn systems for organizing human societies without consent have been dismantled before: the divine right of kings, feudalism, human bondage, imperialism, fascism" (printed p. 225, head absent / PDF p. 241). Its mechanisms: "everything destructive that they have done can be undone by voters, elections, legislation, and judicial enforcement" (printed p. 226 / PDF pp. 242, 244); "That rock is the right of the people to elect a government by consent and not by automation"; "Technologies can be held to publicly defined standards, answerable to voters and not to corporations, subject to the ordinary assessment undertaken of any emerging technology", followed by seven assessment questions; and "The democratic governance of technology, instead of technological governance of humanity: that can still happen" (printed p. 227 / PDF pp. 243, 245).

Three findings. **First**, her mechanisms map onto three falsifiers — elections (DC-1), legislation (DC-2), judicial enforcement (DC-3) — and onto **none** of the fourth: a public institution capable of replacing an essential supplier is absent. Greps return no *nationalize*, no *state capacity*, no *procurement*, no *licensing*, no *public ownership* of anything but nineteenth-century land. **DC-4 gets a zero from her, and the zero should be recorded.** **Second**, she supplies no instance of any of the three working on a stack commitment. The only live resistance documented is local and environmental — "'Grow tomatoes, not data centers' became a slogan as a backlash against data centers gained momentum in cities and towns and states all over the world" — beside individual withdrawal: "Don't tether yourself to a computer. Unplug" (printed pp. 227–228 / PDF pp. 243, 245–246). **Her resistance is cultural, rhetorical and local rather than institutional: the falsifiers appear as rights, not as demonstrated operations** — which is the answer Appendix C needed. Her one institutional proposal is restorative and dated by its own loss: the seven questions are a technology-assessment rubric, and the body that did that work was abolished in 1995 (printed p. 70). **Third**, her dated measurable claims are opinion series, not institutional ones: "A 2025 survey found that a mere 9 percent of Americans and only 11 percent of 'AI experts' expected the technology to have a 'positive' effect on elections over the next twenty years. Another survey that year found that 61 percent of Americans wanted more control over AI" (printed p. 227); and, across seventy countries by 2025, "nearly two in five" agreed that "AI could make better decisions on my behalf that my government representatives" (printed p. 8, head illegible / PDF p. 18). Baselines for a consent series, not a control series. And she publishes **no falsifier of her own**: "little seems to me more inevitable than its eventual fall" (printed p. xiii) is undated and untestable.

### Two further additions

**(1) A twentieth-century lineage for the barons' anti-constitutional programme (ch12 §VII; Appendix A).** Technocracy Inc., 1932–33: "Popular voting can be largely dispensed with" because "it is stupid"; the movement "does not subscribe to the basic tenet of the democratic ideal"; "ninety percent of the courts could be abolished"; and its Canadian national chairman, "Technocrat number 10450-1", was Joshua Haldeman — Elon Musk's grandfather (printed pp. 32–33 / PDF pp. 43–44, the second head OCR'd "41" and inferred; the descent at printed p. 36 / PDF p. 47). **(2) Blackstone's artificial persons, closing part of ch09 §II's Kantorowicz `[GAP]` at one remove.** "Persons also are divided by the law into either natural persons or artificial . . . artificial are such as are created and devised by human laws for the purposes of society and government, which are called corporations or bodies politic" (printed p. 201 / PDF p. 215). ## 5. What it CONTRADICTS

**(A) The object, and so the mechanism's present operation.** On her account the thing being built is not a state and the direction is not consolidation: "less a state than a dream of being without one" (printed p. xv); the corporations "began to subsume the state" (printed p. 4); decisions made by "a handful of men" (printed p. 205); "The nation is in Silicon Valley's hands" (printed p. 222). A working historian reading the 2025–26 record finds the barons ascendant and the state hollowed, and finds no licence, no procurement lever, no export-control regime, no foundry, no constellation and no munitions base worth a paragraph. **This reaches the DEFEND-tier claim at spine §6 (the mechanism operating now through the AI-military stack) and the ruling at §8(b)/(c) that for the United States "the state will absorb the stack."**

**(B) The fiscal-administrative limb, historically.** Her record holds three dated cases in which a state wanted the legibility-and-planning stack, had the fiscal capacity, and did not get it: the American National Data Center, "abandoned due to privacy concerns" and dubbed "the Snooping Machine"; the Soviet OGAS, which failed because "the process of developing the network was left to unregulated, independent contractors" (printed p. 59 / PDF p. 70); Chile's Cybersyn, "abandoned after Pinochet took power in a coup in 1973" (printed p. 62, head OCR'd "42" / PDF p. 73). Private firms built what the states could not. **This reaches §8(g)(4) and ch12 §IV.**

**(C) The wager's shape.** She predicts the fall, not the settlement, on ecological grounds: "the fall of the Artificial State is foreordained. The Artificial State can neither defy the laws of nature nor escape natural limits" (printed p. 130). That is a third trajectory Appendix C does not score — neither consolidation nor durable dispersal but collapse. Beside it, one datum against the stack's indispensability: "A 2026 survey of nearly 6000 companies in the U.S., the UK, Germany, and Australia found that 80 percent of them reported gaining nothing from using AI" (printed p. 223 / PDF p. 239; NEEDS VERIFICATION — no survey named in text).

**The book's best answer.** *To (A):* the two books name different objects, and her own text supplies the hinge she does not turn — technologies "available to the military", a firm "dedicating our company to the service of the West", businesses "sizably underwritten by the U.S. federal government", Nvidia's "sovereign AI". A supplier who dedicates himself to a sovereign, on the sovereign's money, is being absorbed; and §7's tense already holds the consolidation to be compelled, not accomplished. **Grade: adequate, no more** — it argues from her silences, and the honest statement is that she has not tested the absorption claim: she is not evidence against it, and the manuscript must not enlist her for it. *To (B):* abdication and absorption are consecutive, not alternative — §8(c)(i)'s own ruling about the lag. **Grade: good on the sequence, weak on the three failures.** The concession the renovation should make, in one clause: the mechanism predicts acquisition only where the capability is decisive *and* the state's dependence inescapable; in 1960s data-processing neither held. *To (C):* the ecological ceiling enters as a confessed limit, as §8(f) grants the nuclear one, not as a rival on the indicator sheet. **Grade: good, and the weakness here is hers.**

## 7. Verdict

The book changes the manuscript's weight and the honesty of one ruling, not its direction: it is the best-argued contemporary statement of the reading the book must beat — barons ascendant, state hollowed, "the nation . . . in Silicon Valley's hands" — by a historian who read the same 2025–26 documents and found no licensing, no procurement lever and no decisive stack worth a chapter. The single most important thing the author should know is that the phrase is shared and the object is not: hers is "less a state than a dream of being without one", so the two titles collide, the manuscript must say once and plainly that it means the opposite thing by the word, and it must not enlist her as support. Second: the ledger check is complete and the coinage survives absolutely, and the second-reader's question gets a clean negative — no account of public authority expanding over private technology in a democracy, remedies named as rights rather than shown as operations, and no public substitute for any supplier anywhere in the book, which is the DC-4 zero Appendix C needed.
