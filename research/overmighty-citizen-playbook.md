Severing the Power Switch: An American Legislative
Strategy for the Tech Barons
1. Executive summary
The American response to private power has usually been neither confiscation nor laissezfaire. When a private actor controls a bottleneck through which public life must pass, Congress,
courts, and administrative agencies have repeatedly altered the architecture of control:
separate business lines, divest a vertical link, prohibit discriminatory access, simplify a
holding company, open an interface, or place rates and conduct under public supervision. The
object is not to make the proprietor poor. It is to make the proprietor unable to exercise
sovereign-like control through a privately owned switch.
Rockefeller remains the archetype. The Supreme Court applied the Sherman Act to dissolve
Standard Oil into 34 companies, yet Rockefeller retained his shares and became richer as the
successor securities appreciated; the state attacked integrated control, not the fortune (Library of
Congress; Standard Oil Co. v. United States). The same design recurs in railroad rate regulation,
separation of commercial from investment banking, dismantling utility pyramids, the
Paramount theater divestitures, AT&T’s breakup, IBM’s licensing and unbundling obligations,
and Microsoft’s interoperability decree.
Today’s equivalent power is layered: cloud, frontier-model compute, AI accelerators, operating
systems and app stores, search and advertising exchanges, social graphs, identity, payments,
and satellite connectivity. In Q3 2025 AWS, Azure, and Google Cloud accounted for 63% of global
cloud-infrastructure spending (Omdia). The strategic response should therefore be cumulative,
not a single “breakup”: structural separation of commerce from infrastructure; commoncarrier duties for critical cloud and satellite services; portability and mandatory APIs; PUHCAstyle cross-line limits; acquisition presumptions; frontier-model licensing and incident
disclosure; federal multi-cloud procurement; public research compute; egress and app-store
rate rules; and a Digital Platforms Commission with structural-remedy authority. Wealth
remains private. The power to exclude, discriminate, surveil, bundle, or disable becomes
legally bounded.

2. The “over-mighty subject” tradition
“Over-mighty subject” is older than Coke: Sir John Fortescue warned in The Governance of
England (c. 1471–75) of the danger to a king from “ovur mighty subgiettes,” meaning magnates
whose retainers and resources rivaled royal capacity (Ohio State historical study). Coke belongs in
the lineage not as phrase-maker but as a jurist of bounded prerogative: his seventeenth-century
arguments denied that the monarch could personally exercise judicial power (constitutionalhistory study of Coke). Bagehot later described the constitution as an allocation between the
“dignified” and “efficient” parts and emphasized parliamentary government’s capacity to
concentrate legitimate, accountable action (Bagehot, The English Constitution).

In the United States, James Willard Hurst’s institutional history shifted attention from formal
rights to how law constituted economic power—corporations, markets, administrative capacity
—and how public authority could channel private energy without abolishing it (Michigan Law
Review assessment of Hurst). That frame is more useful than a morality play about billionaires: the
relevant variable is command over institutional chokepoints.
The American translation is republican and statutory. Congress defines prohibited
combinations or incompatible lines; agencies supervise networks; courts order divestiture or
police decrees. Wealth taxation, eminent-domain acquisition, and public ownership exist, but
the characteristic response to concentrated industrial power has been structural or behavioral
regulation. This is a preference, not an absolute constitutional rule—and sometimes remedies
were too weak—but it explains why the operative question is “what power must be severed?”
rather than “how much money may this person keep?”

3. Historical case studies — the American legislative pattern
3.1 Standard Oil and Rockefeller
Standard Oil converted scale into control over refining, transport rebates, pipelines, marketing,
and rivals. Congress’s instrument was the Sherman Antitrust Act of 1890, 15 U.S.C. §§1–2; the
Supreme Court held in 1911 that the combination unreasonably restrained and monopolized
interstate petroleum commerce and ordered dissolution (Standard Oil Co. v. United States). The
decree distributed the constituent corporations among shareholders rather than transferring
them to the Treasury. Standard Oil became 34 independent companies (Library of Congress).
The mechanism was structural: replace a unified command center with separately governed
enterprises, removing the ability to coordinate exclusion across a vertically integrated system.
The remedy did not confiscate Rockefeller’s securities. He retained proportional interests in the
successor companies; their appreciation helped make him the first widely reported billionaire
by 1916 (Library of Congress). The paradox proves the thesis: personal wealth can rise while
private governing capacity falls. The “power switch” was Rockefeller’s integrated control of an
industry, not the market value of his property.

3.2 The railroads
Nineteenth-century railroad owners could decide which towns, shippers, and commodities
received favorable access. Congress responded with the Interstate Commerce Act of 1887,
creating the Interstate Commerce Commission and prohibiting unreasonable and
discriminatory rates; the Hepburn Act of 1906 strengthened the ICC’s power to set maximum
rates and inspect accounts (U.S. Senate history; National Archives).
This was not a breakup. It converted private tariff discretion into an administratively
reviewable public function. Rail shares and fortunes remained private; the carrier lost the
unilateral power to favor an affiliate or punish a dependent shipper. The ICC therefore supplies
the cleanest analogue for cloud egress, app-store commissions, and nondiscrimination: leave
title intact, regulate the essential-network switch.

3.3 The Money Trust and J.P. Morgan
The 1912–13 Pujo Committee documented a dense network of banker influence through
underwriting, credit relationships, and interlocking directorships. Congress answered with an
institutional package: the Federal Reserve Act of 1913 established a public-law central-banking
system; the Clayton Act of 1914 constrained interlocking directorates and specified
anticompetitive practices; and the FTC Act of 1914 created an expert commission to police
unfair methods of competition (Federal Reserve history; DOJ history of the Clayton Act).
The mechanism was distributed. Liquidity and monetary coordination ceased to depend on an
informal banker’s syndicate; Section 8 attacked shared governance across competitors; an
administrative body gained continuing jurisdiction. Morgan partners kept their capital and
banking businesses. What they lost was the quasi-public capacity to coordinate corporate
America through overlapping boards and privately rationed credit. The modern lesson is that
one need not prove a single illegal merger to break a network of control: governance links and
public-function substitution can be regulated directly.

3.4 Glass–Steagall and later bank firebreaks
The Banking Act of 1933 separated Federal Reserve member commercial banking from much
securities underwriting and dealing, creating a line-of-business firebreak between deposits and
investment banking (Federal Reserve History; Congressional Research Service). Bank owners were not
dispossessed; institutions chose sides, reorganized, or divested incompatible operations. The
switch removed was the universal bank’s ability to combine government-supported deposits,
lending relationships, securities promotion, and speculative risk under one roof.
The pattern continued, with changed boundaries, in the Bank Holding Company Act of 1956’s
controls on banking and nonbanking affiliations, Riegle–Neal’s 1994 nationally uniform
interstate branching framework (Riegle–Neal, Pub. L. 103-328), analyst “Chinese wall” rules,
Sarbanes–Oxley’s 2002 audit and governance mandates, and Dodd–Frank’s Volcker Rule
restricting proprietary trading by banking entities (Federal Reserve Volcker Rule materials; SEC
Dodd–Frank implementation). These measures vary from structural to behavioral, but all constrain
combinations of functions while leaving equity privately owned.

3.5 Utility holding companies and Samuel Insull
Insull’s utility empire exemplified pyramided control: a small equity stake at the apex could
command layers of operating companies, with opaque intra-group transactions and
geographically scattered assets. Title I of the Public Utility Act of 1935—PUHCA—gave the SEC
registration and reorganization authority. Its “death sentence” policy required holding systems
to simplify and confine themselves to integrated, economically coherent utility systems (SEC
text and historical notes).
The SEC could compel divestitures and collapse holding-company tiers. It did not socialize
electricity or wipe out all investors; operating utilities remained largely investor-owned under
public-utility regulation. The severed switch was leveraged pyramidal command, not wealth
itself. PUHCA is consequently the strongest template for a conglomerate that spans cloud,

models, applications, devices, advertising, identity, and payments: prohibit control
architectures whose strategic logic is cross-layer preference rather than operational necessity.

3.6 Meatpackers
By 1920 the “Big Five”—Swift, Armour, Morris, Cudahy, and Wilson—used slaughtering scale,
stockyard interests, refrigeration, distribution, and retail adjacencies to extend power beyond
meatpacking. A federal consent decree forced withdrawal from specified adjacent businesses;
the Packers and Stockyards Act of 1921 then imposed fair-practice, nondiscrimination, and
oversight duties on packers, stockyards, and dealers (DOJ’s Swift final judgment; USDA statutory
text).
The combined remedy mixed line-of-business exclusion with sector regulation. The firms and
owners kept meatpacking assets and capital; they lost control of gateways that could foreclose
producers and merchants. It is a direct analogue to barring a platform operator from using
infrastructure control to enter and privilege every dependent market.

3.7 The Radio Trust / RCA
RCA joined radio patents, equipment manufacturing, and communications through
relationships with GE, Westinghouse, and AT&T. The 1932 antitrust consent decree required GE
and Westinghouse to divest their RCA holdings and constrained patent and commercial
arrangements (DOJ final judgment). The remedy separated ownership and weakened a patent
pool’s capacity to coordinate an emerging medium. Shareholders received value rather than
expropriation; the targeted switch was cross-ownership plus exclusive intellectual-property
control.

3.8 Paramount and the Hollywood studios
The major studios integrated production, distribution, and first-run theaters, using block
booking and circuit power to control access to audiences. In 1948 the Supreme Court sustained
core antitrust findings in United States v. Paramount Pictures (334 U.S. 131). Subsequent decrees
required theater divestiture by vertically integrated defendants and constrained licensing
practices (DOJ Paramount Decrees review).
The studios retained films, brands, production capital, and distribution businesses; theater
assets were sold, not seized. The power switch was the ability of a content producer-distributor
to own the scarce exhibition gateway and discriminate against independent content. This is the
canonical analogue for separating app-store operation from first-party apps, or retail
marketplaces from their infrastructure and house brands.

3.9 Alcoa and DuPont–GM
Judge Hand’s 1945 Alcoa opinion treated durable control of primary aluminum capacity as
monopolization even without a theatrical predatory act, making market structure central to
Section 2 analysis (United States v. Alcoa). Later proceedings considered structural relief but
adopted more limited remedies as entry changed conditions (1957 district-court opinion).

In the DuPont–GM matter, the Supreme Court found DuPont’s large GM shareholding unlawful
under Clayton Act §7 because the stock relationship affected GM’s purchases; divestiture was
ultimately required (United States v. E.I. du Pont de Nemours). DuPont’s productive assets and
owners’ residual wealth remained private. The switch was a strategic equity link that converted
ownership into preferred access to a captive buyer—an analogue for cloud/model crossinvestments and platform stakes in dependent firms.

3.10 AT&T
The 1956 Bell System consent decree confined AT&T largely to regulated communications and
government work and required broad patent licensing, limiting its ability to carry Bell Labs’
inventions into general-purpose computing. In 1982 the Modified Final Judgment, approved
and supervised by Judge Harold Greene, required AT&T to divest local exchange carriers; the
1984 reorganization created seven Regional Bell Operating Companies (Judge Greene’s opinion;
DOJ decree materials).
AT&T shareholders received interests reflecting the separated businesses; the government did
not confiscate Bell wealth. Structural separation prevented the monopoly local network from
favoring AT&T’s long-distance and equipment operations. The named-judge decree also shows
the value—and administrative burden—of continuing judicial supervision. For cloud and AI,
AT&T supplies both remedies: restrict a regulated bottleneck from adjacent markets, then
divest if conduct rules cannot neutralize self-preference.

3.11 IBM
The 1956 IBM consent decree required patent licensing and changed practices around
tabulating machines, including sale as well as lease options; later modification proceedings
preserve the decree’s scope and rationale (DOJ memorandum on modifying the 1956 judgment). DOJ’s
1969 monopolization case lasted until dismissal in 1982 as “without merit,” but IBM separately
announced in 1969 that software and services would be priced apart from hardware (DOJ
memorandum on the 1969 case).
Causation should not be overstated: unbundling reflected litigation pressure, customer
demand, and technological change. Yet it converted software from an included complement
into a contestable product, helping create an independent software industry and space in
which firms such as Microsoft and Intel could grow. IBM’s owners kept the company and
fortune; the switch constrained tying, closed interfaces, and patent leverage.

3.12 Microsoft
The D.C. Circuit sustained the core conclusion that Microsoft illegally maintained its operatingsystem monopoly, while vacating the initial breakup order and remanding remedy questions.
The 2001 settlement and 2002 final judgment imposed behavioral obligations: OEM freedom
regarding middleware, disclosure of communications protocols, and restrictions on retaliation
and exclusive dealing (DOJ settlement announcement; competitive-impact statement).
Microsoft remained intact and its shareholders retained their wealth. The remedy opened APIs
and distribution choices rather than divesting Windows. It is too strong to say the decree alone

“made the modern web possible”; the browser and web already existed. It is sound to say the
litigation checked foreclosure at a decisive platform layer and institutionalized interoperability
as an antitrust remedy. This is the closest operational precedent for mandatory cloud, appstore, identity, and social-graph APIs.

4. Cross-case synthesis: the American legislative toolkit
Across these cases, the recurring object is control of a dependency. The instruments differ
according to whether the dependency is organizational, technical, financial, or infrastructural:
Structural separation and line-of-business restrictions isolate a bottleneck from
businesses that depend on it: Glass–Steagall, Paramount, AT&T.
Vertical divestiture and forced spin-offs remove the incentive and ability to selfpreference: Standard Oil, Paramount, DuPont–GM.
Interoperability, unbundling, licensing, and mandatory access turn a proprietary
interface into a contestable boundary: IBM, Microsoft, AT&T, and the Telecommunications
Act of 1996’s unbundled-network framework (FCC implementation history).
Interlocking-directorate and cross-ownership bans prevent coordination without waiting
for an explicit cartel. DOJ’s renewed Clayton §8 enforcement produced multiple director
resignations beginning in 2022 (DOJ).
Holding-company simplification attacks pyramidal control and opaque intra-group
subsidy: PUHCA and bank-holding-company regulation.
Consent decrees supervised by named federal judges supply adaptable enforcement—
Greene’s AT&T jurisdiction being the model—but can become slow, personality-dependent
mini-regulators.
Sector-specific independent regulators—ICC, FCC, SEC, FERC, CFPB—sustain expertise,
disclosure, licensing, and rate oversight after litigation ends.
Merger review and notification intervene before power consolidates. Hart–Scott–Rodino
1976 created premerger filing and waiting-period machinery (FTC).
Common carriage, nondiscrimination, and public-utility rate regulation leave the network
privately owned but constrain whom it may exclude and what it may charge.
Disclosure mandates make concealed risk and preference administrable, as securities,
audit, bank, and utility law demonstrate.
National-security review treats control itself as a security variable. CFIUS reviews covered
foreign investments; the Treasury’s outbound-investment program now regulates specified
U.S. investment in sensitive technologies in countries of concern (Treasury Department).
Team Telecom performs parallel security review for FCC-linked foreign participation (FCC).
Procurement leverage conditions access to the federal customer on interoperability,
auditability, labor, cybersecurity, or sourcing rules.
The toolkit’s strength is modularity. A platform may require rate regulation at one layer,
nondiscrimination at another, and divestiture at a third. Its discipline is the “power switch”
test: identify the precise capacity to exclude, coordinate, surveil, subsidize, or disable; choose

the least administratively fragile remedy that removes that capacity; leave residual ownership
and lawful returns intact.

5. The modern problem: the hyperscaler power stack
Modern concentration is not one monopoly but a stack of mutually reinforcing chokepoints.
AWS, Azure, and Google Cloud supplied 63% of worldwide cloud-infrastructure spending in Q3
2025, with shares of roughly 29%, 20%, and 13% respectively (Omdia). The UK cloud
investigation identified egress fees, committed-spend discounts, technical barriers, and
software-licensing practices as obstacles to switching and multi-cloud use; its provisional
findings recommended considering strategic-market-status investigations of AWS and
Microsoft under the UK regime (CMA provisional findings).
Cloud concentration feeds foundation-model concentration because frontier training requires
scarce accelerators, networking, power, data centers, and capital (NBER research on AI scaling and
market structure). Nvidia is the critical accelerator and software-ecosystem supplier; fabrication
of leading chips depends heavily on TSMC and an internationally concentrated semiconductor
supply chain (Congressional Research Service on Taiwan’s semiconductor role). The security concern is
thus dual: corporate gatekeeping inside the United States and geographic manufacturing
dependency abroad.
Apple and Google control mobile operating systems, app distribution, in-app payment rules,
identity credentials, and browser defaults. The EU Digital Markets Act imposes ex ante duties
on designated gatekeepers, including anti-steering, data-use, interoperability, and selfpreference constraints (European Commission DMA overview). Google joins default search
distribution to an advertising stack; Meta controls large social graphs and identity signals;
Amazon joins marketplace rules, fulfillment, advertising, retail data, and AWS. The DOJ has
obtained liability judgments against Google in search and ad-tech cases, although remedies and
appeals determine how much control is actually severed (DOJ Google search case; DOJ Google adtech case).
Musk’s cluster adds a different vertical chain: Starlink satellite connectivity, SpaceX launch, X’s
communications graph, and xAI models and compute. Starlink’s military and emergency value
makes continuity, priority, and denial decisions matters of state capacity rather than ordinary
consumer contracting. Across the stack, payment rails and federated login determine who can
transact or authenticate; data and telemetry train models and target advertising. The switch is
therefore not “being rich” or even “being large.” It is possessing unilateral, technically
embedded discretion over access to essential digital functions.

6. A barrage of proposed measures — the modern menu
Structural architecture
1. Spin off AWS from Amazon commerce. Separate cloud hosting from Amazon’s
marketplace, retail, logistics, and advertising group, with transitional service agreements
and a ban on discriminatory data exchange. Analogue: Paramount and AT&T. Target: the
capacity to cross-subsidize commerce with infrastructure rents or privilege dependent
sellers and cloud customers.

2. Separate Google’s ad exchange and publisher tools. Require divestiture sufficient to
prevent one firm from representing buyers, sellers, and the exchange; DOJ’s ad-tech
complaint expressly sought divestiture, and the liability case is the foundation for
structural relief (DOJ ad-tech case). Analogue: Paramount and meatpackers. Target: vertically
integrated ad-auction control.
3. App-store/first-party-app separation. Bar Apple and Google from operating the sole store
while competing through first-party apps and payment services; permit separately
capitalized affiliates only with independent governance. Analogue: Paramount. Target:
rankings, fees, review delay, and access rules used against dependent developers.
4. Cloud/model functional separation. Require a dominant cloud’s foundation-model
developer to transact through published, auditable terms available to rival labs, escalating
to ownership separation if nondiscrimination fails. Analogue: AT&T’s network/adjacentmarket separation. Target: preferential accelerator allocation, credits, and distribution.
5. PUHCA-style digital holding-company law. Limit common control across cloud, frontier
models, operating systems, app stores, digital advertising, identity, and payments unless
the regulator finds an operational integration benefit that cannot be achieved by contract.
Analogue: PUHCA’s simplification mandate. Target: pyramidal cross-layer command.
6. Cross-ownership and directorate firewall. Apply Clayton §8 aggressively to platform,
model, chip, and cloud competitors; extend statutory coverage to noncorporate entities
and board-observer rights. DOJ’s 2022 campaign shows resignations can be obtained
without waiting for a merger challenge (DOJ). Analogue: Money Trust. Target: coordination
through governance and strategic minority stakes.

Common carriage, rates, and access
7. Critical-cloud common carriage. Designate providers above a market-share and federalcritical-workload threshold as common carriers for specified essential services, with
nondiscrimination, notice, continuity, and appeal duties. Analogue: ICC rail regulation.
Target: arbitrary denial or degradation of compute, storage, and network access.
8. Cloud egress and portability tariff. Cap egress charges at cost-oriented levels for
designated providers and require standardized migration estimates and machine-readable
bills. The CMA identified egress and switching barriers as competition concerns (UK CMA).
Analogue: Hepburn rate regulation. Target: exit tolls and lock-in.
9. App-store nondiscrimination and commission ceiling. Require equal review, ranking, API,
and payment access and authorize a regulator to cap commissions for gatekeepers while
preserving a cost-based security fee. Analogue: Packers and Stockyards Act plus ICC rates.
Target: gatekeeper rent and retaliation.
10. Search-neutrality code. Prohibit a designated general-search engine from favoring
affiliated verticals except under transparent relevance criteria; require labeled house
results and audit logs. Analogue: railroad nondiscrimination. Target: traffic allocation to
dependent publishers and services.
11. Starlink continuity carrier. Condition spectrum authority, federal launch support, and
defense contracts on continuity, lawful priority, emergency roaming, and a documented
government escalation process; provide compensation for compelled extraordinary

service. Analogue: common carriage and defense procurement. Target: a private operator’s
unilateral capacity to interrupt strategically essential connectivity.

Interoperability, data, and technical openness
12. Mandatory platform APIs. A Digital Platforms Commission should specify stable, secure
APIs for app stores, cloud orchestration, identity, messaging, and advertising
measurement, with change-control notice. Analogue: Microsoft’s protocol disclosure.
Target: closed interfaces used as exclusion tools.
13. Social-graph portability and federation. Give users continuous, permissioned export of
contacts, follows, group memberships, and content in standardized formats, plus real-time
interoperability for designated messaging and social services. Analogue: number
portability and the proposed ACCESS Act concept. Target: Meta/X network-effect lock-in.
14. Cloud workload portability. Mandate exportable machine images, identity policies, logs,
and managed-database formats; prohibit contractual penalties for multi-cloud
architecture. Analogue: 1996 telecom unbundling and IBM unbundling. Target: technical
switching costs.
15. Ad-tech measurement commons. Require interoperable auction logs and independent
measurement access subject to privacy controls. Analogue: regulated-carrier accounting.
Target: information asymmetry that lets an integrated intermediary mark its own
homework.
16. Frontier-model disclosure, not blanket open weights. Developers above a statutory
compute threshold should file model cards, evaluation methods, serious-incident reports,
training-compute bands, and supply-chain dependencies with a secure regulator. Do not
automatically publish weights: that can increase proliferation risk. Analogue: SEC
confidential filings and utility accounts. Target: opacity around systemic capability and
risk.

Institutions, licensing, and security
17. Digital Platforms Commission. Enact the Bennet–Welch proposal’s core idea: an expert
federal commission with rulemaking, investigation, data-gathering, interoperability, and
structural-remedy referral authority (Bennet–Welch proposal; Brookings analysis). Analogue:
ICC/FCC/SEC. Target: the enforcement gap between episodic antitrust cases.
18. Frontier-compute licensing. Require a license for training above a periodically updated
compute/capability threshold, conditioned on security, evaluation, incident response, and
beneficial-ownership disclosure. Executive Order 14110’s reporting framework was
rescinded in January 2025, demonstrating the fragility of executive-only governance (White
House rescission). Analogue: safety licensing in high-hazard sectors, administered with SECstyle disclosure. Target: catastrophic-risk externalities and hidden control of frontier
compute.
19. Pre-deployment red-team dossier. For models intended for critical infrastructure, defense,
mass bio-design, or autonomous cyber operations, require independent testing and
regulator-accessible results before release. California vetoed SB 1047 in 2024 but enacted SB
53 in 2025 with frontier-AI transparency and safety-protocol requirements, providing a

state-law baseline (Newsom veto message; California SB 53). Analogue: securities disclosure
plus regulated-product preclearance. Target: deployment externalities.
20. Compute-customer due diligence. Require hyperscale clusters and accelerator clouds to
verify customers, detect anomalous aggregation, retain privacy-protective logs, and report
prohibited uses under judicially reviewable rules. Analogue: bank customer-identification
and suspicious-activity systems. Target: clandestine frontier training and sanctions
evasion.
21. CFIUS-plus and outbound review. Expand review to foreign influence over critical cloud,
satellite, chip, model, and identity providers, and align it with Treasury’s outboundinvestment program (Treasury). Analogue: bank ownership and communications-license
review. Target: foreign control, technology leakage, and coercive dependencies.
22. Durable AI-export statute. Congress should replace oscillating executive rules with
country-, end-user-, and compute-based controls plus allied licensing. Commerce
rescinded the Biden-era AI Diffusion Rule in 2025 and announced a different approach,
illustrating policy volatility (Bureau of Industry and Security). Analogue: standing exportcontrol and communications-security regimes. Target: diffusion of frontier chips, weights,
and cluster capacity.

Competition, procurement, and public capacity
23. Dominant-platform acquisition presumption. Presume unlawful acquisitions by a
designated gatekeeper of firms in adjacent digital markets unless the buyer proves no
material loss of potential competition; require advance notice for all such deals. Analogue:
HSR plus bank-holding-company prior approval. Target: serial acquisition of nascent rivals.
24. Retrospective divestiture authority. Authorize the commission and antitrust agencies to
seek divestiture of consummated acquisitions under a clear limitations rule. The FTC lost
its Meta monopolization case at trial in late 2025 and appealed in January 2026, so
Instagram/WhatsApp divestiture is contested, not an accomplished remedy (FTC appeal).
Analogue: DuPont–GM. Target: acquired social-graph control.
25. Federal multi-cloud procurement. Require critical agencies to maintain tested portability
and an exit path across at least two providers; prohibit sole-source dependence absent a
documented national-security waiver. Analogue: government purchasing as industrial
discipline. Target: state dependence on a single private switch.
26. Contractor interoperability covenant. No major federal cloud, AI, productivity, or satellite
contract without open export formats, audit rights, software bills of materials, security-bydesign, incident reporting, and decree compliance. Analogue: regulated-carrier franchise
conditions. Target: using public money to entrench lock-in.
27. Public compute option. Make the National AI Research Resource permanent and scale
NSF/DOE compute for universities, public-interest labs, and qualified small firms. The
NAIRR pilot already federates federal and private resources for research access (National
Science Foundation). Analogue: public central-bank capacity alongside private banks. Target:
private rationing of research compute.
28. Talent-mobility statute. Congress should bar most noncompetes and restrictive repayment
provisions for technical workers while protecting trade secrets. The FTC moved in 2025 to

accept vacatur of its nationwide noncompete rule, confirming that durable national policy
requires legislation (FTC). Analogue: anti-interlock policy applied to human capital. Target:
incumbent capture of scarce engineering talent.
29. Digital systemic-risk filings. Require annual public and confidential filings on outages,
concentration of suppliers, government requests, content-moderation process, algorithmic
audits, political-ad targeting, and material model incidents. Analogue: SEC periodic
reporting and utility reliability filings. Target: informational advantage and concealed
systemic risk.
30. Remedy trustees and sunset review. Structural decrees should appoint technically expert
trustees, publish compliance metrics, protect complainants, and undergo five-year review;
sunset only after the bottleneck is demonstrably contestable. Analogue: Judge Greene’s
AT&T supervision, improved to reduce judicial micromanagement. Target: evasion and
remedy decay.

7. Implementation sequencing, 2026–2030
2026–27: use existing authority. DOJ should enforce the Google search final judgment and press
administrable divestiture/interoperability relief in ad tech; the FTC should litigate its Amazon
marketplace case and pursue its Meta appeal. The Google search judgment entered in
December 2025 bars certain exclusive distribution arrangements and requires data and
syndication access, but stops short of the government’s requested Chrome divestiture;
implementation was still underway in August 2026 (DOJ remedy announcement; DOJ compliance
status report). Agencies can also revive Clayton §8 enforcement, tighten federal contract terms,
expand NAIRR, police deception and discrimination, and apply existing FCC, export-control,
CFIUS, and Team Telecom powers. The FTC’s Amazon monopolization matter remains listed as
an active federal case (FTC case docket).
2027–28: legislate institutions and duties. Congress should create the Digital Platforms
Commission; designate covered cloud, app-store, search, social, identity, satellite, and frontierAI services; establish portability, nondiscrimination, licensing, incident-reporting, and
acquisition presumptions; and authorize rate and structural remedies. This is also the moment
for a permanent NAIRR, talent-mobility law, and stable AI export-control framework.
2028–30: execute structural cases. Following market studies and rulemaking records, pursue
separations where conduct remedies have failed—ad-tech layers first, then app-store publishing
and cloud/model affiliations, with AWS/commerce separation on a developed record.
Coordinate with the EU DMA, whose gatekeeper obligations have applied since 2024 (European
Commission), and the UK strategic-market-status regime, which now permits firm-specific
conduct requirements (UK government guidance). Sequencing matters: portability and
procurement create substitutes before divestiture tests them.

8. Counter-arguments and risks
Innovation loss. Integration can lower latency, improve security, and finance research. The
answer is not indiscriminate breakup: require the firm to show integration-specific efficiencies,
prefer interface rules where they are enforceable, and reserve separation for conflicts that
repeated conduct remedies cannot police.

Competition with China. Scale matters in chips, models, and defense. But dependence on one
American supplier is itself a strategic vulnerability; redundancy, open interfaces, and public
compute increase national resilience. Export controls should protect external advantage while
domestic rules prevent private control from becoming unreviewable.
First Amendment. Rules dictating editorial ranking or compelled carriage can burden platform

judicial review, statutory deadlines, whistleblower protection, and periodic congressional
reauthorization. Structural remedies often outperform permanent conduct supervision
precisely because they reduce the regulator’s need to second-guess daily product decisions.

9. Conclusion
The American tradition is not hostility to wealth. It is hostility to private dominion over public
dependencies. Rockefeller could remain extraordinarily rich after losing unified control of oil;
bankers retained capital after central-banking and board-network reforms; utility, studio,
telephone, computer, and software shareholders retained property after lawmakers and courts
separated functions or opened interfaces. The line was repeatedly drawn between ownership
and sovereignty.
That distinction should govern the hyperscaler era. Cloud, models, chips, app stores, search,
social graphs, identity, payments, and satellite networks are now components of state capacity.
Their owners may earn exceptional returns. They should not possess unreviewable authority to
determine who computes, communicates, transacts, authenticates, advertises, or connects
during a crisis.
The modern program is therefore neither a wealth tax dressed as competition policy nor
wholesale nationalization. It is a statutory campaign to identify each digital power switch and
place it beyond unilateral private command: separation where conflicts are inherent,
interoperability where interfaces exclude, common carriage where continuity is essential,
disclosure where opacity creates systemic risk, and public capacity where no credible
alternative exists. That is the constitutional logic of the over-mighty subject translated into an
administrative republic.
