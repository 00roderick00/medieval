| Venice | Budget c. 1500, in round figures: receipts 1,150,000 ducats (sales taxes in Venice 230,000; direct taxes 160,000; salt 100,000; miscellaneous 130,000; mainland cities 330,000; overseas domains 200,000); ordinary expenses 530,000, of which interest on government bonds 155,000; "Available for extraordinary expenses (wars) 620,000" (Lane 1973: 237). The 1469 itemised list, the earliest extant: c. 1 million ducats, "only a little more than half" from the empire and direct taxation combined; the "8 dazi" c. 235,000 yearly; the mint 2,700 ducats in the 1490 budget, against Philip the Fair's France, where the mints out-earned general taxation (Lane & Mueller 1985: 186–87). The arc: Monte Vecchio at 4 per cent and 66 in 1423 → "only 1 percent irregularly and selling at 13" by 1474; direct levies on income voted under the shock of 1453; cadaster 1463; Monte Nuovo 1482 (Lane 1973: 238); direct taxes back to a tenth of revenue by 1600 (Lane 1973: 325). | The Arsenal (ch04 §VI); on land, hired companies — "one of the most successful employers of condottieri" (Lane 1973: 232) — through "thirty years of almost constant warfare in Lombardy, becoming every year more costly" (Lane 1973: 229). | None within the class to subordinate, and no apparatus above it: "almost no bureaucracy," the Arsenal, mint and taxes run by elected committees of three to six nobles holding office a few years and ineligible to succeed themselves (Lane 1973: 98, 266–67); a centre nothing could kill — Foscari deposed in 1457 by vote of the councillors and the Ten, the system carrying on (Lane 1973: 267). | Ran the mechanism two steps in and stopped; built the instruments and refused the form (ch04 §VI–VII). Among the states of the age "those most nearly successful were, except for Venice, monarchies" (Lane 1973: 251); larger units "with which a city-state such as Venice could not in the long run compete" (Lane 1973: 241). Consolidated nothing beyond its own class (ch10 §VI). | (i) The fisc was driven by thirty years of Lombard war fought by hired companies, not by cannon — the fisc without the ratchet (Lane 1973: 229, 232, 238). (ii) Decisive force limited by men, not capital: the Arsenal "produced more galleys than Venice could find crews for," Spanish soldiers taken aboard at Lepanto (Lane 1973: 364). Cost is not the only constraint on decisive capability (ch10 §VI, §VIII). | 4 (§VI–VII), 10 (§VI, §VIII) |
| The Papacy (twelfth–thirteenth centuries) | Liber Censuum (1192): total revenue assessed by Pfaff at 1,214 gold ounces — "less than 5 per cent of Richard I's annual income from England," some items unpaid; Peter's Pence from England "just under £200" a year (Morris 1989: 215). Revenues from the city of Rome inaccessible through much of the twelfth century; the Patrimony threatened by local cities and nobles as well as by imperial pressure (Morris 1989: 214). Honorius III's 1225 offer — free curial service in return for a prebend in every cathedral church — "the most ambitious of the series of attempts to relieve by agreement the chronic underfunding of the Roman Church," rejected at Bourges (November 1225) and London (May 1226) (Morris 1989: 558). | None of its own. Hadrian IV: "no one can make war without pay" (Morris 1989: 214). Force hired or borrowed: mercenaries sent to Palermo and a crusading indulgence offered against Markward of Anweiler, 1199–1201, a success "bought at a great price" (Morris 1989: 422). | Papal governors or rectors, usually cardinals, "proved possible only in the Campagna and the Tuscan patrimony" (Morris 1989: 421); the Regno's territories held through the pope's own family (Morris 1989: 421). | A jurisdiction, not a sovereignty (ruling (q)). The territorial state acquired "more of a tribute to temporary imperial weakness than to the strength of the apostolic see": "The Papal State was created on the battlefields of Sicily and Germany" (Morris 1989: 422). | A universalism that could not pay for force — its existence a function of the balance among the territorial powers, not of anything the universalism could field (ch02 §III). The disanalogy sorts the modern churches: the European church stands where the medieval one stood; the Chinese church is fused into a state that can pay; the American church is the case the twelfth century never saw (ch02 §III). | 2 (§III) |
| The Ottomans | Prebendal (timar) base converted late: fiefs given to janissary companies, other prebends "turned into royal estates in order to pay the expanding standing army and to hire peasant mercenaries," the treasury facing "recurring deficits from the early 1590s onward" (Ágoston 2014: 121). Money taxes "revolutionized the tax system" (İnalcık 1973: 48–51, the pin carried from ch11 §IV, not re-verified in this pass). | A salaried firearms corps within the standing household troops "From the 1390s on, preceding their rivals by centuries" (Ágoston 2014: 93). The variant: administrative state before artillery, artillery locking it (ruling (g); ch11 §IV). | Timariot cavalry displaced by the private troops (kapı halkı) of district and provincial governors — 33 per cent of the timariot troops in the 1520s, 58 per cent by the late seventeenth century (Ágoston 2014: 121); governors and local notables trading troops and provisions for state offices and tax farms; "selling janissary certificates — that is, 'entry tickets' to the corps — became a lucrative business for janissary officers" (Ágoston 2014: 123). | CONVERSION FAILED: "the Ottoman experience was military devolution" (Ágoston 2014: 123). The chain ran — weapon, coin, treasury — and its output was a new stratum of intermediaries (ch11 §IV); dates for the cell 1580s–1600s. | The five moves executed before the gun, on an in-kind base; the gun's fiscal demand, arriving late, unmade them (ch11 §V, ruling (g)).
