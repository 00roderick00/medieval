## 1. Where this book comes from

My first class at university was with Roger Mettam at Queen Mary — a
specialist in seventeenth-century France, whose course on revolutionary
France I mostly flailed at. The one thing he taught me stuck for life:
understand where power lies. The interesting thing about the French
Revolution, he argued, is who is holding power, when — it moves from the
king to the Estates General to the mob to the Committee of Public Safety
and back and forth through personalities. Who has it, and when.

Everything since has been that question. My Cambridge work was a power
vacuum — an incapacitated king in a medieval state: who rules? where is
the power? My government career was the exercise of power, witnessed at
first hand. In the private sector I have watched billionaires rise and
fall as they consciously or unconsciously reach for it, and I have
advised both them and governments as they pull the levers. This book is
that career's question asked at full scale.

## 2. The error I made, and the correction that became the thesis

Studying Henry VI, I made an error: I did not consider the wider
military world he operated in — and in particular the death of John
Talbot. Talbot has always been read as throwing his life away in his
last battle, when he may have been going for a strategic knockout blow
against the French cannon. The arrival of cannon was a seismic change:
sieges that had lasted a year were over in weeks. And the ability to
pay for cannon belonged to kings, not lords — which swings power to the
administrative state, and arguably *creates* the administrative state.

Henry VI ushers in the Wars of the Roses — the historic material for
Game of Thrones. Power is moving unmercifully around, driven by trends
already running through the late Middle Ages: feudalism giving way to
bastard feudalism, over-mighty subjects dominating undermighty kings.
Exceptional monarchs — Henry V — could do exceptional things, but the
trend was clear: shared sovereignty among over-mighty lords, the
universal Church, and undermighty kings. Not an English phenomenon; a
continental one.

(On Henry V and the longbow: Agincourt is not a longbow story alone.
It is his ability to keep men from breaking on the field, and his
logistical aptitude, against an undermighty king already suffering
civil war. The undermighty-king disease then crossed the Channel and
brought England its own civil war.)

## 3. The pendulum

The neo-medievalists drew the parallel between that world and ours,
and although the 1970s coinage didn't yet apply, it is now a genuinely
useful lens on global governance: undermighty kings everywhere, power
leaking to universalist tech platforms and other over-mighty subjects.

Where the neo-medievalists see history moving one way, I see a
pendulum — and the pendulum is swinging forward, toward what came
*after* the medieval age. The book's name for it is settled: the
Tudor turn. The Church abolished and centralised; standing taxation
established; literacy used to build an administrative state — all in
the service of one core truth: kingdoms fell to artillery, and
artillery required fiscal and administrative discipline. Kings need an
administrative state to create the decisive weapons they need.

## 4. Longbow and cannon — the governing metaphor

Longbows can win you a battle. They cannot take fortresses; they are
not decisive.

Drones and cheap autonomous networked devices are our longbows — the
people's weapon: democratised, cheap, effective en masse. They can win
you a battle or close a strategic waterway. They are not decisive.

The cannon of today is the full-stack, AI-automated state — the thing
only a handful of polities can assemble, because only they can tax at
the scale it requires. That is what will be decisive on the
battlefield, and it is what tells us where the world moves next: the
Tudor state.

## 5. The polities, as I actually rank them

The candidate consolidators are the book's four: the United States,
China, the European Union, and — conditionally — India.

- **China** is probably closest to current Tudor norms — the
  settlement substantially executed.
- **The USA** has the stack but has not centralised the universalist
  platforms, and until it does it is not truly competing. It will not
  nationalise them in the socialist sense; it will require licensing
  fees to run AI and use administrative and legal tools in the
  American tradition. [CROSS TO ch12, analytically dressed: the

- **The EU bloc** has the tax base and the tools but must run its own

- **India** is the conditional fourth: the legibility stack running a
  decade ahead of the military-industrial one.
- **Russia** is not a candidate; it is the demonstration case — 
  nuclear-armed, capital-short, and shown on the battlefield unable
  to take a decisive victory. Its role in the book is evidentiary:
  what a polity with weapons but without the fiscal-administrative
  stack can and cannot do.
